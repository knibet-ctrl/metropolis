# Transfer — The Most Under-Appreciated Finding

> **Status: TIER 1 that far transfer is rare and small. TIER 2 on the
> exceptions. This negative result is better evidenced than most of the
> positive results in the notebook and is far less known.**

> **Standing caveat: written from general knowledge without source
> verification.**

> **The claim, stated flatly: skills you learn in one domain transfer to other
> domains far less than almost everyone assumes — and in most controlled tests,
> not at all. This has been demonstrated repeatedly for over a century, in
> every generation, against the strong prior of every generation.**

---

## Why this matters more than anything else in the notebook

Nearly every justification for general education, brain training, chess in
schools, coding for all, music lessons for cognition, and "teaching critical
thinking" is a **far transfer claim**. If far transfer is rare, then a large
fraction of the stated rationale for how societies spend educational time is
unsupported.

That is a serious statement and I mean it seriously. It does not follow that
these activities are worthless — they may be worth doing for their own content.
It follows that **the transfer justification specifically is not evidenced**,
and it is the justification most commonly offered.

## The taxonomy

**Barnett & Ceci (2002)** gave the standard framework. Transfer varies along
six dimensions of distance:

| Dimension | Near | Far |
|---|---|---|
| **Knowledge domain** | Same topic | Different discipline |
| **Physical context** | Same room | Different institution |
| **Temporal context** | Same session | Years later |
| **Functional context** | Same purpose (academic) | Different purpose (workplace) |
| **Social context** | Same (individual) | Different (group) |
| **Modality** | Same (written test) | Different (oral, practical) |

**Near transfer works. Far transfer usually does not.** Much apparent
disagreement in the literature is people occupying different points in this
space and using the same word.

## Thorndike killed this in 1901 and nobody noticed

**Edward Thorndike & Robert Woodworth (1901), "The Influence of Improvement in
One Mental Function Upon the Efficiency of Other Functions."**

The prevailing theory was **formal discipline**: the mind is a set of faculties
(memory, reasoning, attention) that can be strengthened by exercise like
muscles, and Latin, geometry and logic were the best exercise. Thorndike and
Woodworth trained people to estimate the areas of rectangles, then tested them
on triangles and circles. **Improvement did not transfer.** They proposed
**identical elements** instead: transfer occurs to the extent that the two
tasks share specific components.

**Thorndike (1924)** followed up with a study of roughly **8,564 high-school
students**, comparing gains in reasoning across subjects taken. Latin, geometry
and the "disciplinary" subjects produced no special gains. **The apparent
benefit of Latin was a selection effect: better students took Latin.**† This is
one of the earliest large-scale demonstrations of a selection artefact in
education research, and it is a century old.

**Formal discipline theory was refuted in 1901 and 1924, and it is the implicit
theory behind most curriculum defence in 2026.**

## The modern re-run: brain training

| Study | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Owen et al. (2010)**, *Nature* | **11,430 participants**, six weeks of online brain training, three arms including active control | Improvement on the trained tasks. **No transfer to untrained cognitive tasks, even closely related ones.** The largest study of its kind |
| **Jaeggi, Buschkuehl, Jonides & Perrig (2008)**, *PNAS* | Dual n-back training, claimed transfer to fluid intelligence | The originating positive result. Small n, passive control |
| **Redick et al. (2013)**, *JEP:General* | n-back training with **both active and no-contact controls**, 17 measures | **No transfer to fluid intelligence.** The active control is what killed it |
| **Melby-Lervåg & Hulme (2013)**, meta-analysis | 23 studies of working-memory training | Reliable **short-term** near transfer; **no convincing far transfer** to nonverbal reasoning, verbal ability, word decoding, arithmetic |
| **Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme (2016)** | Extended meta-analysis with methodological stratification | Far transfer effects were **near zero once studies with active controls were isolated** |
| **Simons, Boot, Charness, Gathercole, Chabris, Hambrick & Stine-Morrow (2016)**, *PSPI* | Comprehensive review of the commercial brain-training evidence base | Extensive evidence of near transfer; **"little evidence" of far transfer to everyday cognitive performance** |

**The 2014 duelling consensus statements** are worth knowing about: a statement
organised through the Stanford Center on Longevity and the Max Planck Institute
for Human Development, signed by roughly 70 scientists, said the commercial
claims were not supported; a counter-statement, organised largely by
researchers with industry links, gathered around 130 signatures saying the
evidence was promising.† **Counting signatures is not evidence.** The
methodological point that settles it is simpler: the positive results cluster
in studies with passive controls, and vanish with active controls.

**FTC v. Lumos Labs (January 2016): $2 million settlement over deceptive
advertising for Lumosity.** The claims at issue were exactly far-transfer
claims — improved school and work performance, protection against dementia.

## Chess, music, and the general-cognition claim

**Giovanni Sala and Fernand Gobet** ran the definitive meta-analyses.

| Domain | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Chess instruction** | Sala & Gobet (2016): overall effect on mathematics and cognitive outcomes around **g = 0.34**, but **effect size declined sharply as control-group quality improved**, and was near zero in the best-designed studies† |
| **Music training** | Sala & Gobet (2017, 2020): near-null effect on cognitive and academic outcomes once active controls are used. Earlier positive findings driven by weak designs and unmatched controls |
| **Working memory training** | Same pattern |
| **Video game training** | Same pattern, with an additional problem: participants know which condition is "the good one" |

Their summary (Sala & Gobet, 2020, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*): **"Cognitive
training does not enhance general cognition."** The regularity across four
independent literatures — where effect size is inversely proportional to
control-group quality — is itself the strongest evidence. That signature is
what expectancy effects look like.

**Steelman the other side:** music training correlates with better outcomes,
and correlations that survive controls in observational data are not nothing.
Schellenberg (2004) ran an actual RCT — 144 six-year-olds, keyboard or voice
lessons vs drama vs no lessons — and found a **~7-point IQ advantage for music
over controls**, but roughly **2.7 points** relative to the drama group, which
is small.† Schellenberg himself has since argued the observational literature
is dominated by selection: families who provide music lessons differ
systematically.

## Where transfer DOES occur — the honest exceptions

**This is the part that gets left out by people who like the negative result.**

| Exception | Evidence | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **Schooling raises IQ** | Ritchie & Tucker-Drob (2018), meta-analysis of three quasi-experimental designs (policy changes to compulsory schooling, school-age cutoffs, control for early IQ): **1 to 5 IQ points per additional year**, effects durable | **Strong. This is genuine far transfer and it is the best counterexample in the literature.** Any strong no-transfer position has to account for it |
| **Statistical and causal reasoning training** | Fong, Krantz & Nisbett (1986); Nisbett et al. (1987): brief training in the law of large numbers transferred to everyday reasoning problems in unrelated content domains, including in telephone follow-ups where subjects did not know they were being tested | Moderate. Replicated in that lab; less tested elsewhere. **The mechanism matters: these are abstract *rules* explicitly taught with varied concrete examples, not skills implicitly absorbed** |
| **Literacy itself** | Learning to read restructures cognition broadly; illiterate vs literate adult comparisons show wide differences | Strong but confounded |
| **Domain-general strategies explicitly taught for transfer** | Perkins & Salomon's "high road" transfer: mindful abstraction plus explicit bridging to new contexts | Moderate. Works when transfer is *taught for*, not assumed |
| **Expert-level abstract representation** | Chi et al. (1981): experts categorise physics problems by deep principle. That abstraction does transfer *within* the domain | Strong within domain, silent about across |

**The pattern in the exceptions is consistent and it is the practical lesson:
transfer happens when the abstraction is explicitly taught, explicitly labelled,
and practised across varied surface contexts. It does not happen as a byproduct
of practising a task.** Detterman's (1993) provocative summary — that transfer
is an epiphenomenon and that when it occurs it is because the tasks share
elements — is too strong, but it is the right default.

## The critical thinking problem

**This is the most consequential implication and it is rarely faced.**

Daniel Willingham, "Critical Thinking: Why Is It So Hard to Teach?" (*American
Educator*, 2007), makes the argument:

1. Critical thinking is not a skill that can be deployed independent of
   content, in the way that riding a bicycle can be deployed on any road.
2. Whether you can reason well about a problem depends overwhelmingly on
   whether you know the relevant domain deeply enough to recognise the deep
   structure.
3. Novices sort problems by surface features; experts by deep structure (Chi,
   Feltovich & Glaser, 1981 — physics problems sorted as "inclined plane" vs
   "conservation of energy"). **The ability to see the deep structure IS the
   domain knowledge.**
4. Therefore "teach critical thinking" as a standalone course produces students
   who can name fallacies and cannot detect them in unfamiliar content.

The Wason selection task is the standard illustration: abysmal performance in
abstract form, and dramatically better performance on the logically identical
"drinking age" version (Griggs & Cox, 1982; Cosmides). **Same logic, different
content, completely different performance.** If reasoning were a portable
skill, this would not happen.

**Steelman the other side, because there is one.** Meta-analyses of critical
thinking instruction (Abrami et al., 2008, 2015) do find positive effects,
largest when instruction is **explicit** and **embedded in subject matter with
dialogue and authentic problems** — that is, exactly the "high road" condition
above. And argumentation training does appear to produce some general gains.
The defensible position is not "critical thinking cannot be taught." It is
**"critical thinking cannot be taught content-free, and its transfer is limited
by domain knowledge."** That is still a serious challenge to how it is usually
sold.

## Why expertise is domain-specific — the mechanism

From how-learning-works.md and expertise.md: expertise is stored as **chunks and
schemas indexed by domain-specific patterns**. A chess master's 50,000-plus
chunks are chess positions. They do not decompose into general pattern
recognition.

The decisive evidence: **Chase & Simon (1973)** found masters massively
outperformed novices at reconstructing real chess positions after a five-second
exposure — and **had essentially no advantage on randomly arranged pieces.**
The superiority is entirely in the structure, not in the visual memory. (Gobet
& Simon (1996) later found masters retain a *small* advantage on random
positions, because random boards still contain fragments of legal structure.
The qualification refines the finding; it does not overturn it.)

The **Nobel disease** is the same finding at the top of the distribution:
Pauling on vitamin C, Mullis on HIV, Montagnier on homeopathy and memory of
water, Shockley on race, Josephson on telepathy. **Extreme domain expertise
provides no protection outside the domain, and may provide anti-protection by
supplying unearned confidence.** See knowledge/expertise-and-authority.md.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular story | Reality |
|---|---|
| **Learning X teaches you to think** | Formal discipline. Refuted by Thorndike & Woodworth (1901) and Thorndike (1924), and re-refuted every generation |
| **Chess/music/coding makes children smarter** | Effect size inversely proportional to control-group quality. Near-null in the best designs (Sala & Gobet) |
| **Brain training improves memory and intelligence** | It improves performance on the trained tasks. Owen et al. (2010), n = 11,430: no transfer. FTC fined Lumosity $2m |
| **Critical thinking is a transferable skill** | It is largely domain-knowledge-dependent. Teachable, but not content-free, and transfer must be designed in rather than assumed |
| **We should teach skills, not facts, because facts are Googleable** | Inverts the mechanism. Domain knowledge is what enables the skills. You cannot recognise a deep structure you have no schema for |
| **Transfer is the normal case; failure to transfer is a teaching failure** | Transfer is the **exceptional** case. It requires explicit abstraction and varied practice, and even then it is modest |
| **The negative results mean education doesn't work** | No. Schooling causally raises IQ by roughly 1-5 points per year (Ritchie & Tucker-Drob 2018). Education works; the *transfer story about why it works* is mostly wrong |

## Practical consequences

1. **If you need a capability, train the capability.** Do not train a proxy and
   hope. This applies to hiring tests, to curriculum, and to your own study.
2. **Practise in varied surface contexts** if you want the abstraction. One
   context produces one context-bound skill.
3. **Name the abstraction out loud.** The transfer exceptions all involve
   explicit rule statement plus varied examples.
4. **Assume your competence stops at the domain boundary.** Especially if you
   are very good at something. See risk/forecasting.md on the hedgehog problem.
5. **Discount any product sold on far-transfer claims** unless it cites a trial
   with an active control group. This one heuristic disposes of nearly the whole
   category.

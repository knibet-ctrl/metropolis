# Deliberate Practice — What Ericsson Actually Said

> **Status: TIER 1 that structured practice with feedback beats unstructured
> repetition. TIER 2 on how much of expert performance it explains. TIER 4 as
> usually posed ("is it 10,000 hours?").**

> **Standing caveat: written from general knowledge without source
> verification. Figures marked † are the ones to check first.**

> **The headline: "10,000 hours" is Malcolm Gladwell's framing, not K. Anders
> Ericsson's. Ericsson objected to it publicly and repeatedly, in print, for the
> rest of his life.**

---

## The source paper

**Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993), "The Role of Deliberate Practice in
the Acquisition of Expert Performance," *Psychological Review* 100(3),
363-406.**

Design: violinists at the Music Academy of West Berlin, sorted by their
teachers into three groups — "best" (potential international soloists), "good"
(department average), and students training to be music teachers. Plus a
comparison group of professional middle-aged violinists. Retrospective
estimates of accumulated solitary practice, plus diaries.

Approximate reported figures by age 20:†

| Group | Accumulated solitary practice |
|---|---|
| Best violinists | ~10,000 hours |
| Good violinists | ~7,800 hours |
| Music teachers | ~4,600 hours |

**What the paper claimed:** that the amount and quality of *deliberate practice*
— not mere experience — differentiated groups, that it accumulated
monotonically, and that expert performance is largely acquired rather than
innate. Ericsson's stronger later position (notably in *Peak*, 2016, with
Robert Pool) was that with correct training almost anyone can reach very high
levels, and that innate limits outside physical size are largely
undemonstrated.

**What the paper did not claim:**
- That 10,000 hours is a threshold.
- That 10,000 hours is sufficient.
- That everyone needs the same number.
- That the finding generalises to sports, chess, business or anything else. The
  paper's own evidence was music, plus a review.

**Gladwell's *Outliers* (2008)** turned an average into a rule. Ericsson's
response, stated in interviews, in a 2012 commentary, and at length in *Peak*,
was that the number was an average with wide variance, that it was a
correlational finding about a specific population, and that the popular version
had removed the only part that mattered — the word *deliberate*.

**Note the irony of the reception.** Gladwell's version is more optimistic than
Ericsson's on the surface (anyone can do it, just put in the hours) and more
pessimistic underneath (an intimidating fixed toll). Ericsson's actual position
is that hours are nearly irrelevant if the practice is not structured
correctly — which is at once more demanding and more hopeful.

## What deliberate practice actually requires

Ericsson's criteria. **All of them, simultaneously.**

| Requirement | Detail | Why most "practice" fails it |
|---|---|---|
| **A well-defined specific goal** | Not "get better," but "reduce the tempo variance in bars 14-18" | Most practice is repetition of the whole, aimed at nothing in particular |
| **Operating at the edge of current ability** | Just beyond comfortable competence. Necessarily involves failure | Most practice is performed in the zone of comfortable execution — because that is what is enjoyable |
| **Immediate, informative feedback** | On the specific target, fast enough to correct the next attempt | Most domains give feedback that is delayed, noisy, or absent |
| **Repetition with refinement** | Repeat the corrected version, not the original | Repetition without correction consolidates the error |
| **Full concentration** | Effortful attention, not automatic execution | Limits sessions. Ericsson's elite performers practised ~4 hours a day at most, in ~90-minute blocks, with naps |
| **A teacher, coach, or explicit model** | Someone who already knows what the target looks like and can see what you cannot | The binding constraint for almost everyone. See motivation-and-conditions.md |
| **It is not inherently enjoyable** | Ericsson was explicit. Deliberate practice is not the pleasurable part of the activity | The single most-dropped criterion in popular retellings |

**The crucial contrast is deliberate practice vs experience.** Twenty years of
doing your job is not twenty years of deliberate practice; it is usually one to
three years of learning followed by seventeen years of automatic execution. This
is why so many professionals plateau — and why time-in-role predicts
performance so weakly in many fields. See expertise.md, where the same point
appears from the other direction.

## The Macnamara challenge

**Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014), "Deliberate Practice and Performance in
Music, Games, Sports, Education, and Professions: A Meta-Analysis,"
*Psychological Science*.** 88 studies.

Variance in performance explained by accumulated practice:†

| Domain | R² (approx) | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| **Games** (chess etc.) | ~26% | Substantial |
| **Music** | ~21% | Substantial |
| **Sports** | ~18% | Substantial |
| **Education** | ~4% | Small |
| **Professions** | **<1%** | Essentially nothing |
| Overall | ~12% | |

Related results:

- **Hambrick, Oswald, Altmann, Meinz, Gobet & Campitelli (2014)**, *Intelligence*:
  re-analysing chess and music datasets, practice explained roughly **34% in
  chess and 30% in music** — higher than the 2014 meta-analysis but still
  leaving most variance unexplained.†
- **Gobet & Campitelli (2007)**: among chess players reaching master level, the
  practice required ranged from roughly **3,000 to over 23,000 hours** — an
  eightfold spread. Some players never reached master after more than 25,000
  hours.† **This is the single most damaging datum for any threshold claim.**
- **Macnamara & Maitra (2019)**, *Royal Society Open Science*: a direct
  replication attempt on the 1993 violinist design. Found a much weaker
  relationship between accumulated practice and group membership, and noted the
  original's small cells (10 per group) and reliance on retrospective estimates.
- **Age of starting** matters independently of total hours in several datasets,
  which a pure accumulation model does not predict.

## The dispute, stated fairly

This is a real scientific disagreement between competent people. Both sides
have a case. Ericsson died in 2020; the dispute did not resolve.

### Ericsson's side

| Argument | Force |
|---|---|
| **The meta-analyses coded the wrong variable.** Most studies summed *any* practice — including group practice, play, and unstructured repetition — rather than deliberate practice as defined | **Strong.** If the independent variable is mismeasured, the R² is a lower bound. Ericsson made this point explicitly in his 2016 reply to Macnamara |
| **Range restriction.** Studies of elite performers have already selected on practice. Within a group who all practised enormously, practice cannot explain much variance | **Strong and mathematically unavoidable.** Restriction of range attenuates correlations |
| **Retrospective self-report of practice hours is noisy**, and measurement error attenuates the correlation | Fair, though it cuts both ways: noise can also inflate through recall bias favouring successful narratives |
| **The professions result is expected, not damaging.** Most professions lack the conditions for deliberate practice at all — no clear goals, no immediate feedback, no coach. The theory *predicts* low returns to experience there | **Strong, and often missed.** This is a confirmation of Ericsson's framework, not a refutation |

### Macnamara's side

| Argument | Force |
|---|---|
| **Even generously coded, practice leaves most variance unexplained.** Something else is doing the work | **Strong.** The residual is large in every domain measured |
| **"Deliberate practice" as defined by Ericsson is close to unfalsifiable in retrospect.** If a performer plateaus, the practice was not truly deliberate. If they excel, it was | **Strong and the most serious charge.** The criteria are partly defined by their outcome |
| **The 1993 study is small, retrospective, correlational and did not replicate well** | Fair. The foundation is weaker than its citation count implies |
| **Individual differences — working memory, processing speed, starting age, physical attributes — predict attainment independently of practice** | Fair. See talent-and-limits.md |
| **Some people never reach expert level despite enormous practice** (Gobet & Campitelli) | Strong against thresholds; Ericsson would reply that the quality of practice was not measured |

### Where they agree, and it is more than either implies

- Unstructured repetition produces little improvement. **Nobody disputes this.**
- Structured practice with feedback beats unstructured practice. **Nobody
  disputes this.**
- Elite performance requires very large amounts of practice. **Nobody disputes
  this.** No one becomes a grandmaster in 500 hours.
- Practice is necessary. The dispute is entirely about sufficiency and about
  variance shares in specific populations.

**The honest summary: practice is necessary, is not sufficient, and its
measured variance share is genuinely uncertain, ranging from near-zero to
roughly a third depending on domain, coding and population. Anybody who gives
you one number has chosen a side.**

---

## The methodological hole under both sides

**Nobody has randomised anyone into a decade of deliberate practice.** The
entire literature is observational, retrospective, and conditioned on
survivorship at every stage:

- People who find early progress easy practise more (gene-environment
  correlation — the "multiplier effect", Dickens & Flynn 2001).
- People who find it hard quit, and are absent from every dataset.
- Coaches select promising students, who then get better coaching.
- Elite samples are range-restricted on both talent and practice.

This means **the correlation between practice and attainment is uninterpretable
as a causal share without a design nobody has run.** Both the 26% and the
"anyone can do it" readings are extrapolations from the same compromised data.
This should be stated more often than it is, by both camps.

## What survives, operationally

Regardless of the variance dispute, the practice *design* prescriptions hold up
because they are supported independently by the cognitive literature in
what-actually-works.md:

| Prescription | Independent support |
|---|---|
| Set a specific sub-goal per session | Goal-setting literature (Locke & Latham); sub-goal labelling (Catrambone 1998) |
| Work at the edge of ability | Desirable difficulties (Bjork); retrieval should be effortful but successful |
| Get fast, task-focused feedback | Kluger & DeNisi (1996) — **with the caveat that feedback directed at the self makes things worse in over a third of cases** |
| Isolate the weak component and drill it | Part-task training; chunking |
| Repeat the corrected version | Basic conditioning; avoids consolidating errors |
| Vary conditions once the basic form is stable | Interleaving and variability of practice (Schmidt & Bjork 1992) |

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular story | Reality |
|---|---|
| **"The 10,000 hour rule" is Ericsson's finding** | It is Gladwell's coinage. Ericsson rejected it in print. The 10,000 figure was an approximate *mean* for one group at one age in one study of violinists |
| **10,000 hours is enough** | Gobet & Campitelli found chess players who never reached master after more than 25,000 hours. There is no threshold |
| **10,000 hours is required** | Some reached master in around 3,000. The variance is the finding |
| **Practice explains most of expert performance** | It explains roughly a quarter to a third at best, in the domains most favourable to it, and near zero in the professions |
| **Practice explains almost nothing (the anti-Ericsson version)** | Also wrong. Nobody reaches elite level without enormous practice. The meta-analytic R² is a variance share within selected populations, not a statement about necessity |
| **Deliberate practice is what talented people enjoy doing** | Ericsson was explicit that it is not enjoyable. The "find your passion and the hours will flow" version inverts the claim |
| **Just do the hours** | Hours of comfortable repetition produce plateaus. This is the observed pattern in most professions and the reason experience predicts performance so weakly there |
| **The Ericsson/Macnamara dispute has been settled** | It has not. It is presented as settled in both directions by people with priors |

## Steelmanning the strong-talent reading

If you wanted to argue that deliberate practice is mostly a rationalisation:
starting age effects, the eightfold spread in hours-to-master, the near-zero
professional result, the failure of the 1993 study to replicate cleanly, the
heritability of the traits that predict practice *propensity* itself (Mosing et
al. 2014 found music practice is itself substantially heritable, and that
controlling for genes attenuated the practice-ability association in twins† —
this is the most awkward single finding for a pure practice account), and the
unfalsifiability of post-hoc "that wasn't real deliberate practice."

**That is a serious case and it should not be waved away.** The counter is that
none of it removes the necessity of practice, that it is all correlational, and
that the practical prescriptions do not depend on the variance share at all.

## Open questions

- [ ] Has anyone run a long-horizon randomised trial of practice *structure* (not amount) in a real skill domain? Sport science may have partial designs.
- [ ] Can "deliberate practice" be operationalised prospectively, so the unfalsifiability charge stops landing?
- [ ] What is the effect of starting age net of accumulated hours, and is it a critical-period effect or a selection effect?
- [ ] Is the Mosing et al. twin finding on heritable practice propensity robust? It is the most important single result for this dispute and I am unsure of its replication status.
- [ ] Why are the professions so bad at generating expertise? Is it purely absent feedback (Kahneman & Klein) or also absent goals?
- [ ] What is the correct range-restriction correction for elite samples, and what does the 26% become after it?
# Deliberate Practice — What Ericsson Actually Claimed

> **Status: TIER 1 that deliberate practice as defined is far more effective
> than ordinary experience. TIER 2 on how much of elite performance it
> explains. TIER 4 on "10,000 hours," which is not a finding.**

> **Standing caveat: written from general knowledge without source
> verification. The variance percentages below are the load-bearing numbers and
> should be checked before use.**

---

## The origin

**K. Anders Ericsson, Ralf Krampe & Clemens Tesch-Römer (1993), "The Role of
Deliberate Practice in the Acquisition of Expert Performance," *Psychological
Review*, 100(3), 363-406.**

Design: violinists at the Berlin Hochschule der Künste, divided by their
teachers into three groups — "best" (potential international soloists), "good"
(likely orchestral professionals), and music-education students training to
teach. Retrospective estimates of lifetime solitary practice, plus diaries.

Approximate accumulated solitary practice by age 20:

| Group | Hours (approximate) |
|---|---|
| Best violinists | ~10,000 |
| Good violinists | ~7,800 |
| Music teachers | ~4,600 |
| Amateur adults (separate comparison) | ~2,000 |

A parallel comparison of pianists gave a similar pattern.

**What the paper claimed:** that accumulated deliberate practice differs
systematically by attainment level, that the best practise more from an earlier
age, and — the strong claim — that *individual differences in expert
performance are largely accounted for by practice history rather than by innate
talent*, with the acknowledged exceptions of height and body size.

**What the paper did not claim:**
- That 10,000 hours is a threshold.
- That 10,000 hours is sufficient.
- That 10,000 hours is required.
- That everyone who practises 10,000 hours reaches elite level.

**The number is a group mean with substantial variance around it. It appears in
the paper as a description, not a rule.**

---

## The Gladwell problem

Malcolm Gladwell, *Outliers* (2008), converted a group mean into "the 10,000-
hour rule" and attached it to the Beatles in Hamburg and Bill Gates at Lakeside
School. The phrase is Gladwell's.

**Ericsson objected, repeatedly and publicly.** In *Peak: Secrets from the New
Science of Expertise* (2016, with Robert Pool) he devoted space to correcting
it, and in journal commentary he made the same points. His objections, in his
own framing:

| Ericsson's objection | Detail |
|---|---|
| **The number is arbitrary** | It is the mean at age 20, an age chosen because that is when the students were studied. Elite violinists continue practising; by the time they are actually elite the figure is far higher |
| **It is a mean, not a minimum** | Roughly half the best group had *fewer* than 10,000 hours |
| **The variance is enormous** | Individuals reach a given standard at wildly different totals |
| **It ignores the quality distinction** | Gladwell counted playing hours. Ericsson's entire theoretical contribution is the distinction between deliberate practice and mere experience. Hours of unstructured playing are not the thing being measured |
| **It reversed the causal story into "anyone can"** | Ericsson's claim was that practice explains differences *among people who are already in the pipeline*, not that anyone can enter it |

**Ericsson's own view was still strongly practice-weighted** — he was not a
moderate on this — but "10,000 hours" is a popularisation he explicitly
rejected, and it is now the single most commonly repeated false attribution in
this literature.

---

## What deliberate practice actually requires

Ericsson's definition is narrow and demanding. All of the following:

| Requirement | What it means | Why most practice fails it |
|---|---|---|
| **Well-defined specific goal** | Not "get better at the piece" but "play bars 40-48 at 92 bpm without the fingering error" | People practise wholes, not the broken part |
| **Full attention and conscious effort** | Not autopilot repetition | Most repetition is automatised and therefore does not change the representation |
| **Immediate, informative feedback** | You must know within seconds whether the attempt was right | The dominant missing ingredient in most self-directed learning |
| **Repetition with refinement** | Adjust based on feedback, repeat | People repeat without adjusting, or adjust without repeating |
| **Operating just beyond current ability** | Outside the comfort zone, inside the reachable zone | The default is to practise what you can already do, because it is pleasant |
| **A teacher, coach, or at minimum a known-good model** | Someone who knows what the target representation is and can see your deviation from it | Requires a developed field with accumulated pedagogy |
| **It is not enjoyable** | Ericsson was explicit. Deliberate practice is effortful and not intrinsically rewarding | This is why it is rare, and why volume is limited |

Ericsson also reported that elite performers practise in limited daily blocks —
commonly cited as around **4 hours per day, in sessions of roughly 60-90
minutes**, with deliberate recovery and above-average sleep including napping.†
The limit is on sustainable high-attention effort, not on time available.

**Corollary that matters:** deliberate practice as defined **cannot exist in a
domain without an established pedagogy.** There is no deliberate practice for
"being a good manager" in Ericsson's sense, because nobody can specify the
target representation, feedback is delayed by years and confounded, and there
is no known-good model. Ericsson accepted this. Most popular applications
ignore it.

---

## The counterattack: Macnamara's meta-analyses

**Brooke Macnamara, David Hambrick & Frederick Oswald (2014), "Deliberate
Practice and Performance in Music, Games, Sports, Education, and Professions: A
Meta-Analysis," *Psychological Science*.** 88 studies.

Variance in performance explained by accumulated practice:

| Domain | R² (approximate) |
|---|---|
| **Games** (chess etc.) | ~26% |
| **Music** | ~21% |
| **Sports** | ~18% |
| **Education** | ~4% |
| **Professions** | **<1%** |
| Overall | ~12% |

Related findings:

- **Hambrick, Oswald, Altmann, Meinz, Gobet & Campitelli (2014)**, *Intelligence*:
  re-analysis of chess and music datasets. Practice explained roughly **34% in
  chess and 30% in music** — higher than Macnamara's figures, still far short of
  "largely accounted for."†
- **Gobet & Campitelli (2007)**: among chess players reaching master level,
  practice to reach that level ranged from about **3,000 to over 23,000 hours**.
  Some players never reached master after more than 25,000 hours.† **This range
  is the single most damaging datum for the strong practice thesis.**
- **Macnamara & Maitra (2019)**, *Royal Society Open Science*: a direct
  replication attempt of Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993). Found a
  practice-attainment relationship but weaker, and did not reproduce the
  separation between the best and good groups on solitary practice.
- **Ullén, Hambrick & Mosing (2016)** and the Swedish twin work: practice
  itself is substantially heritable, and the practice-ability correlation is
  partly genetically confounded. **Gene-environment correlation means you
  cannot read the practice coefficient as purely causal.** This is a serious
  methodological point and it applies to every study in the table.

## The dispute, presented fairly

### Ericsson's side, steelmanned

1. **The meta-analyses measure the wrong variable.** Most included studies used
   accumulated hours of *any* practice, or self-reported "practice," not
   deliberate practice as defined. Ericsson's 2016 rebuttal ("Summing up hours
   of any type of practice…") argued the coding was invalid: if you correlate
   attainment with a poor proxy, you get an attenuated coefficient, and the
   attenuation is not evidence about the construct.
2. **Range restriction.** Studies of elite performers are conducted on people
   who all practised enormously. Within a restricted range, the correlation is
   mechanically suppressed. The professions figure of <1% is partly this: all
   qualified doctors have similar training exposure.
3. **Measurement error in retrospective hour estimates** attenuates
   correlations further, and it is severe.
4. **The professions result is a fact about the professions, not about
   practice.** Most professional work does not provide the conditions for
   deliberate practice at all — no immediate feedback, no coach, no defined
   target. Finding that "practice" doesn't predict lawyer quality is consistent
   with the theory, not against it.
5. **No one has produced an elite performer who did not practise enormously.**
   The necessity claim survives everything.

### Macnamara and Hambrick's side, steelmanned

1. **If the construct is only measurable by people trained by Ericsson, it is
   not a scientific construct.** The reply "you measured it wrong" has been
   used to dismiss every disconfirming study, which makes the theory
   unfalsifiable in practice.
2. **The unexplained variance is enormous and requires an account.** Even at
   Hambrick's more generous 34%, two thirds of the variance in chess attainment
   is something else. That something else is not nothing.
3. **Gobet & Campitelli's eightfold range at the same attainment level** is
   direct evidence of large individual differences in rate of improvement, and
   rate of improvement is what "talent" would predict.
4. **Age of starting predicts attainment beyond total hours** in several
   domains, suggesting developmental or maturational factors that hours cannot
   capture.
5. **Practice is heritable and correlated with ability.** The causal arrow is
   partly reversed: people who improve fast practise more, because improvement
   is reinforcing. Twin designs support this.
6. **The 1993 study is small, retrospective, and did not replicate cleanly.**
   The foundational datum is weaker than its influence.

### Where a fair reading lands

**Both sides are right about the other's error.** Macnamara's meta-analyses do
aggregate over incompatible measures of practice; Ericsson's construct is
defined tightly enough to be difficult to disconfirm. My reading, held loosely:

- Practice is **necessary** and **very large** in effect. TIER 1.
- Practice is **not sufficient**, and the residual variance is substantial in
  every domain measured. TIER 1.
- The specific share attributable to practice is **domain-dependent by a factor
  of twenty or more**, and unresolved within domains. TIER 2.
- "Anyone can be world class with enough practice" is **unsupported** and was
  not Ericsson's public position in that form, though his writing sometimes
  came close. TIER 3 at best.

## Domains where deliberate practice conditions exist, and where they don't

| Domain | Defined goals | Immediate feedback | Established pedagogy | DP possible? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Classical music | Yes | Yes (audible) | Centuries old | **Fully** |
| Chess | Yes | Yes (position analysis, engines) | Strong | **Fully** |
| Individual sport | Yes | Yes (measured) | Strong | **Fully** |
| Surgery | Partly | Delayed but real | Improving; simulation helps | **Partly** |
| Programming | Partly | Fast (tests, compiler) | Weak and fragmented | **Partly** |
| Writing | Weakly | Slow, noisy, taste-dependent | Weak | **Barely** |
| Management | No | Delayed years, confounded | Contested | **No** |
| Investing | Nominally yes | Delayed, noisy, high variance | Contested | **No** — see risk/forecasting.md |
| Psychotherapy | No | Almost none | Contested | **No** |
| Long-horizon forecasting | Yes if scored | Only if you keep a record | Emerging (Tetlock) | **Only with deliberate scaffolding** |

**This table is the most practically useful thing in the file.** Before
committing years, ask which row you are in. If the domain lacks feedback, your
first task is to manufacture it, not to accumulate hours. See practices.md.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular story | Reality |
|---|---|
| **"The 10,000-hour rule" is Ericsson's finding** | It is Gladwell's phrase from *Outliers* (2008). Ericsson objected publicly and at length. This is the most common misattribution in the field |
| **10,000 hours makes you an expert** | It is a group mean at age 20 with wide variance. Gobet & Campitelli found masters at ~3,000 hours and non-masters past 23,000 |
| **Talent is a myth** | Nobody with data holds this. Ericsson's claim was about differences among people already selected into a domain, and even there a large majority of variance is unexplained |
| **Practice explains 10,000 hours' worth of everything** | Domain-dependent: roughly 20-30% in games and music, near zero measurably in professions. Anyone quoting one number across domains is not reading the papers |
| **Just put in the hours** | Hours of ordinary experience produce little improvement after the first plateau. Twenty-year veterans are frequently no better than five-year ones in domains without feedback — a finding that predates Ericsson (Camerer & Johnson, 1991; Meehl) |
| **Deliberate practice is about loving what you do** | Ericsson said the opposite. It is effortful and not enjoyable. The "passion" framing is a motivational graft onto a theory that explicitly denies it |
| **Ericsson was refuted** | He was not refuted; the strong version of the practice thesis was undermined, and the definitional dispute was never settled. Reporting either "practice is everything" or "practice barely matters" as the outcome is wrong |

## Open questions

- [ ] Can deliberate practice be operationalised in a way both camps accept before coding a meta-analysis? Nobody has tried seriously since Ericsson's death in 2020.
- [ ] Are the Gobet & Campitelli range figures right? They carry a lot of weight in the argument.
- [ ] How much of the practice-attainment correlation survives within-family and twin designs? Ullén and Mosing suggest a lot of it doesn't. This is the most important open empirical question in the file.
- [ ] Is the professions result (<1%) a measurement artefact, a range-restriction artefact, or a real fact about the absence of feedback? These have completely different implications.
- [ ] Does manufactured feedback (simulation, scoring, deliberate review) actually convert a no-DP domain into a DP domain? Surgery simulation and forecasting tournaments are the two natural experiments.
- [ ] What is the true within-person dose-response of deliberate practice? Almost all data is between-person.

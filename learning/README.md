===============================================================================
WHAT THIS IS
===============================================================================
An honest map of what is actually known about getting better at things.

  How does a person go from incompetent to expert? What does practice do?
  Which study techniques survive controlled testing and which are folklore?
  Does talent set a ceiling? Does anything you learn transfer anywhere else?
  When is an expert's intuition worth trusting, and when is it theatre?

Written in the same discipline as leaders/, existence/, knowledge/ and risk/:
positions given fairly, disputes flagged as disputes, confidence marked, and a
standing refusal to pretend something is settled when it isn't.

**No live sources were used. This was written from general knowledge with no
verification against primary documents.** Names, dates, effect sizes and
sample sizes are given because vague claims are unfalsifiable and therefore
useless — but every specific number here should be checked before you cite it.
Several are from memory and may be off by a meaningful margin. Where a figure
is load-bearing for an argument, I say so explicitly.

**Second warning, and this one is structural.** Education and educational
psychology have a replication problem at least as bad as social psychology's,
plus a distinctive pathology of their own: **systematic effect-size
inflation.** Small samples, unblinded outcome assessors, researcher-designed
tests that closely mirror the intervention, publication bias, and Cohen's
benchmarks (0.2 / 0.5 / 0.8) applied to a field where they are wildly
inappropriate. Kraft (2020) argued that for a well-run RCT with a standardised
achievement outcome, **0.20 SD is a large effect, not a small one.**
Lortie-Forgues & Inglis (2019) examined 141 large randomised trials from the
US NCEE and UK EEF and found a mean effect near **0.06 SD**, with many trials
statistically uninformative either way.† Treat any education finding reported
as d = 0.7 with suspicion until you know who measured the outcome.
See what-we-know.md for the full ledger, and the-control-group.md for the
graveyard.

THE FIVE IDEAS THAT ORGANISE EVERYTHING
===============================================================================

  1. **RETRIEVAL, NOT EXPOSURE.** Learning is caused by the effortful act of
     pulling information out of memory, not by putting it in. Rereading feels
     like learning and mostly isn't. -> what-actually-works.md

  2. **DIFFICULTY IS THE MECHANISM, NOT THE COST.** Robert Bjork's *desirable
     difficulties*. Conditions that slow acquisition and feel worse — spacing,
     interleaving, testing, generating — usually produce better long-term
     retention. Conditions that feel fluent usually produce worse. **Your sense
     of how well you are learning is negatively correlated with how well you
     are learning, over a wide range.** -> how-learning-works.md

  3. **EXPERTISE IS DOMAIN-SPECIFIC AND DOES NOT TRAVEL.** This is the most
     under-appreciated finding in the field and the one with the largest
     implications for how education is justified. Far transfer is rare, small,
     and mostly absent when you use active control groups. -> transfer.md

  4. **INTUITION IS TRUSTWORTHY ONLY IN REGULAR ENVIRONMENTS WITH FEEDBACK.**
     Kahneman and Klein's two conditions. This single criterion sorts
     firefighters and chess masters from stock pickers and clinical
     interviewers, and it does so cleanly. -> expertise.md

  5. **PRACTICE MATTERS ENORMOUSLY AND IS NOT SUFFICIENT.** The share of
     performance variance it explains is real, large in some domains, small in
     others, and genuinely disputed. Anyone who tells you the number is settled
     is selling something. -> deliberate-practice.md, talent-and-limits.md

TEN COMPRESSED HEADLINE FINDINGS
===============================================================================

 1. **Testing yourself is not measurement of learning; it is the cause of it.**
    Roediger & Karpicke (2006): after a 5-minute delay, restudying beat
    testing. After one week, testing beat restudying by roughly 20 percentage
    points of retention.† The direction reverses with delay, which is why
    students who optimise for how they feel today choose wrong.

 2. **The most popular study techniques are the weakest.** Dunlosky, Rawson,
    Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013) rated ten techniques. Rated HIGH utility:
    practice testing, distributed practice. Rated LOW: rereading, highlighting,
    summarisation, imagery-for-text, keyword mnemonics. Surveys consistently
    find rereading and highlighting are the two most-used methods.

 3. **"10,000 hours" is Malcolm Gladwell's phrase, not Anders Ericsson's, and
    Ericsson publicly rejected it.** The 1993 Berlin violinist study reported
    an *average* accumulated by the best group by age 20, with wide variance
    and no threshold claim. Gobet & Campitelli (2007) found chess players
    reaching master level anywhere from ~3,000 to over 23,000 hours.†

 4. **How much of performance is practice? It depends enormously on the
    domain.** Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014), 88 studies: practice
    accounted for roughly 26% of variance in games, 21% in music, 18% in
    sports, 4% in education, and under 1% in professions.† Ericsson disputed
    the coding of "practice" in these studies, and the dispute is unresolved.

 5. **Far transfer barely exists.** Thorndike & Woodworth (1901) demolished
    formal discipline theory; a century later, working-memory training,
    commercial brain training, chess instruction and music lessons all show
    near transfer and essentially no far transfer once active control groups
    are used (Melby-Lervåg & Hulme 2013; Simons et al. 2016; Sala & Gobet
    2017-2020). **The implication for "teaching critical thinking" as a
    content-free skill is severe and rarely faced.**

 6. **Intuitive expertise is real under two conditions and unreliable
    otherwise.** Kahneman & Klein (2009): (a) an environment regular enough to
    contain valid cues, (b) an opportunity to learn those cues through
    practice with feedback. Firefighters and chess players qualify. Long-horizon
    political forecasters, stock pickers and clinical interviewers do not.

 7. **Learning styles have been tested with the correct design and failed.**
    Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008) noted that the meshing hypothesis
    requires a crossover interaction; almost no published study used the
    design, and those that did found nothing. Belief among teachers remains
    somewhere around **90%** across many countries (Dekker et al. 2012; Newton
    & Salvi 2020).†

 8. **Feedback is not reliably good.** Kluger & DeNisi (1996), 607 effect
    sizes: mean effect around d = 0.41, **but over one third of feedback
    interventions made performance worse.**† Feedback that directs attention to
    the self rather than the task is the main culprit. "Give more feedback" is
    not advice.

 9. **Growth mindset is real, small, and was massively oversold.** Sisk et al.
    (2018): mindset-achievement correlation around r = 0.10; intervention
    effects around d = 0.08 overall, concentrated in low-SES and at-risk
    students. Yeager et al. (2019), n ≈ 12,500 across 65 US schools, found
    roughly 0.10 grade points for lower-achieving students — a genuine but
    modest result, and dependent on the school's norms supporting it.

10. **Fluency is a trap that has been measured directly.** Deslauriers et al.
    (2019, PNAS): students in active-learning physics classes learned
    measurably more than in polished lectures **and rated their own learning as
    lower.** The subjective signal points the wrong way.

===============================================================================
HONEST CAVEATS
===============================================================================
- **No sources were checked.** Written from general knowledge. Numbers marked
  with † are the ones I would check first; but check all of them.
- **The literature is contaminated by incentive.** Study-skills advice, brain
  training, mindset programmes and speed reading are commercial products. The
  evidence base is downstream of an enormous amount of money.
- **The talent-versus-practice question is genuinely unresolved and politically
  charged.** talent-and-limits.md tries to state exactly what the evidence
  supports and exactly what it does not, and steelmans both readings. It is the
  file most likely to be quoted out of context, in either direction.
- **Effect sizes in education are not comparable to effect sizes in medicine or
  psychophysics.** See the second warning above. A "large" education effect is
  often 0.2 SD.
- **Most of the strong findings come from laboratory studies with verbal
  materials over short intervals.** The extrapolation to a year of learning a
  hard skill is an extrapolation. It is a reasonable one; it is still an
  extrapolation, and classroom trials shrink effects substantially.
- **Nothing here is about children specifically.** Developmental questions —
  critical periods, early intervention, reading instruction — are gestured at
  but not covered properly.

## What expertise is not

| Not this | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Better general memory** | Chase & Simon (1973): chess masters' advantage vanishes on randomly arranged boards. Ericsson & Chase's SF study — a runner trained to a digit span of about 79 by mapping digits onto running times — showed **no improvement in letter span at all**† |
| **Faster processing** | Expert chess players consider *fewer* moves than intermediates, not more. De Groot found grandmasters and experts searched to similar depth; the difference was which moves they considered at all |
| **More years on the job** | Time in role predicts performance weakly or not at all in many professions. Choudhry, Fletcher & Soumerai (2005) found physician performance often **declines** with years since training† |
| **Confidence** | Confidence and accuracy decouple badly in low-validity environments. Confident experts in unpredictable domains are the specific failure mode |

## What expertise is

### 1. Chunked, structured domain knowledge

**Adriaan de Groot**, *Het denken van den schaker* (1946; English as *Thought
and Choice in Chess*, 1965). Dutch psychologist and chess master. Compared
grandmasters with strong club players using think-aloud protocols.

Finding: grandmasters did **not** calculate more variations or search deeper.
They *perceived* the position differently — the strong moves came to mind
immediately, and analysis served to verify rather than to discover.

**William Chase & Herbert Simon (1973)** formalised it. The chunking theory,
and the decisive experiment:

| Condition | Master | Novice |
|---|---|---|
| **Real game position, 5s exposure** | ~16 pieces recalled | ~4 pieces |
| **Random arrangement, 5s exposure** | **~3-4 pieces** | ~3 pieces |

**The master's advantage is entirely in the structure.** Estimated chunk store:
Simon and Gilmartin put it at roughly **50,000 chunks** for a master; later
template theory (Gobet & Simon, 1996) revised the units upward in size and
downward in count. Gobet & Simon also found masters retain a *small* advantage
on random positions — because "random" boards still contain fragments of legal
structure — which refines rather than overturns the result.

**The ten-year rule** (Simon & Chase, 1973): no one reached grandmaster with
less than about a decade of intense involvement. **John Hayes (1989)** extended
it: examining 76 composers, he found essentially no acknowledged masterworks
produced before about ten years of composing. Mozart is the standard
counterexample and does not survive inspection — his early works are not the
ones performed, and his first widely acknowledged masterwork came after roughly
a decade of unusually intensive training under his father.

### 2. Better problem representation

**Chi, Feltovich & Glaser (1981)**: physics problems sorted into categories by
novices and by PhD physicists.

| | Sorted by |
|---|---|
| **Novices** | Surface features — "inclined plane problems," "pulley problems," "spring problems" |
| **Experts** | Deep principle — "conservation of energy problems," "Newton's second law problems" |

**Experts spend longer before starting and less time overall.** They build a
qualitative representation first. Novices pattern-match to an equation and
start substituting.

This finding is the mechanistic core of the argument in transfer.md: the ability
to see deep structure **is** domain knowledge, not a separable skill.

### 3. Automaticity that frees working memory

Skill acquisition frees cognitive resources. Fitts & Posner's (1967) three
stages — cognitive, associative, autonomous — remain the standard description.
Anderson's ACT-R models the shift from declarative rules to compiled procedures.

**The cost of automaticity is the expertise reversal effect** (Kalyuga et al.,
2003) and the loss of ability to explain. Experts are frequently poor teachers
because the steps have compiled away — the **expert blind spot**. Hinds (1999)
found experts systematically underestimated how hard tasks were for novices,
and *training them to consider novice difficulty did not fully correct it*.

## The Kahneman-Klein result — the cleanest thing here

**Daniel Kahneman & Gary Klein (2009), "Conditions for Intuitive Expertise: A
Failure to Disagree," *American Psychologist* 64(6), 515-526.**

Two researchers with opposite programmes:

| Kahneman | Klein |
|---|---|
| Heuristics and biases. Expert judgement is systematically flawed | Naturalistic Decision Making. Studied firefighters, nurses, military commanders; documented genuine expert intuition |
| Prior: intuition is mostly overconfidence | Prior: intuition is mostly compressed expertise |

They spent years working out where they actually disagreed. **They found they
did not.** The joint conclusion:

> **Intuitive expertise develops when and only when two conditions are met:**
> **(1) an environment of sufficiently high validity — regular enough that
> valid cues exist to be learned;**
> **(2) an opportunity to learn those cues, through prolonged practice with
> rapid, unambiguous feedback.**

Both are necessary. Neither is sufficient.

### Applying the two conditions

| Domain | Regular environment? | Feedback? | Verdict on intuition |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chess** | Yes — deterministic rules | Yes — immediate, unambiguous | **Trust it.** Real skill |
| **Firefighting** | Yes — fire physics | Yes — within minutes | **Trust it.** Klein's ground truth |
| **Anaesthesiology** | Yes | Yes — seconds | **Trust it** |
| **Reading radiographs** | Yes | **Only if follow-up exists** | Trust it where feedback loops are closed; not where they aren't |
| **Nurses detecting neonatal sepsis** | Yes | Yes, fast | **Trust it.** Klein documented pre-verbal recognition |
| **Athletics, music** | Yes | Yes | **Trust it** |
| **Clinical psychology (diagnosis, prognosis)** | Low validity | **Almost none** — practitioners rarely learn long-run outcomes | **Do not trust it.** Meehl's finding |
| **Stock picking** | Very low validity | Noisy, delayed, confounded by market moves | **Do not trust it.** Consistent with the index-fund literature |
| **Long-range political forecasting** | Low validity | Delayed by years, ambiguous resolution | **Do not trust it.** Tetlock (2005) |
| **Recruiting from unstructured interviews** | Low validity | Never — you don't see the counterfactual hire | **Do not trust it.** Structured beats unstructured decisively |
| **Parole and sentencing** | Moderate | Absent for the counterfactual | **Do not trust it** |
| **Wine tasting** | Moderate | Weak and socially contaminated | Genuine for gross discrimination, unreliable for fine and for price-blinded ranking |

**Why the second condition is usually the binding one.** Most professional
environments are regular enough. What they lack is feedback: you never learn
what the rejected candidate would have done, whether the untreated patient
would have recovered, or whether the stock you didn't buy went up. **Absent
feedback, twenty years of experience is one year repeated twenty times, and
confidence grows anyway.** That last clause is the dangerous part.

**Shanteau (1992)** independently catalogued the task characteristics that
distinguish domains where experts perform well (static stimuli, decisions about
things, repetitive, feedback available, objective analysis possible) from those
where they do not (dynamic stimuli, decisions about behaviour, unique cases,
feedback unavailable). The two lists converge with Kahneman and Klein's, from a
completely different starting point.

## The mechanical prediction result

**TIER 1, seventy years old, still ignored.**

- **Paul Meehl (1954)**, *Clinical versus Statistical Prediction*: reviewed
  around 20 studies. Simple actuarial formulas equalled or beat expert
  clinical judgement in nearly all.
- **Grove, Zald, Lebow, Snitz & Nelson (2000)**: meta-analysis of **136
  studies**. Mechanical prediction equalled or beat clinical judgement in
  roughly **94%**; clinical judgement was clearly superior in about 8 studies,
  with no discernible pattern.†
- **Goldberg (1968)**: built a linear model of each clinician's own judgements
  from their MMPI diagnoses. **The model of the clinician outperformed the
  clinician.** The reason is that the model applies the clinician's own rules
  without the noise. This is "bootstrapping the expert" and it is one of the
  most under-known results in psychology.
- **Kahneman's *Noise* (2021, with Sibony and Sunstein)** generalises it: much
  of what looks like bias is **variability** — the same expert, same case,
  different day, different answer.

**What experts are still needed for:** identifying the variables, recognising
when the case is outside the model's range, and generating the inputs. **What
they should not do is the integration.** See risk/forecasting.md, which covers
the same result from the prediction side.

## The 10-year rule and its exceptions

The ten-year figure is more robust than the 10,000-hour figure because it makes
a weaker claim: it is a **lower bound on elapsed time**, not a required dose.

Exceptions worth knowing:
- **Bobby Fischer** reached grandmaster at 15, after roughly nine years of
  intense chess. Cited as a counterexample; it is within the rule.
- **Modern chess prodigies** reach grandmaster faster than earlier generations
  — because databases, engines and accumulated pedagogy compress the
  acquisition. **This is evidence that the bound is about knowledge
  transmission efficiency, not about biological maturation.** It is the single
  best argument that the "rule" is a fact about pedagogy, not about people.
- **Domains with shallow structure** (some sports skills, some crafts) reach
  ceiling far faster. The rule is about domains with large chunk stores.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular story | Reality |
|---|---|
| **Experts have better memories** | Domain-specific only. Chess masters' advantage disappears on random positions; a trained digit-span expert showed no letter-span improvement |
| **Experts think harder and consider more options** | De Groot: they consider *fewer* moves. The generation of candidates is where the expertise lives, not the search |
| **Experience makes you an expert** | Only in high-validity environments with feedback. In their absence, experience produces confidence without accuracy — and physician performance often declines with years since training |
| **Trust the expert's gut** | Trust it in chess, firefighting, anaesthesia, athletics. Do not trust it in stock picking, clinical prediction, unstructured interviewing, or long-range forecasting. **The distinguishing criterion is known and it is Kahneman and Klein's two conditions** |
| **Experts are the best teachers** | Often the worst. Automaticity compiles away the intermediate steps, producing the expert blind spot. Hinds (1999) found this resistant to correction |
| **Algorithms are cold and inferior to human judgement** | Grove et al. (2000): mechanical prediction wins or ties in ~94% of 136 comparisons. Goldberg: a model of the expert beats the expert. This has been known since 1954 and is still resisted, which is itself the interesting phenomenon |
| **Kahneman showed intuition is unreliable** | Kahneman co-authored the paper establishing when it is reliable. The two-condition criterion is his conclusion as much as Klein's |

## Steelmanning the naturalistic decision-making side

Klein's position deserves more than a footnote. The heuristics-and-biases
programme's laboratory tasks are frequently artificial, deliberately designed
to elicit errors, and often use abstract stimuli that no expert has ever
learned cues for. **Demonstrating that experts fail on unrepresentative tasks
does not establish that they fail on their own.** Klein's field studies of
fireground commanders documented recognition-primed decision making —
experienced commanders generating a single workable option immediately and
mentally simulating it, rather than comparing alternatives — and getting it
right under conditions where deliberation was impossible. That is real, and the
laboratory literature had no vocabulary for it. The fact that Kahneman
conceded this in print, rather than defending his programme, is why the 2009
paper is worth more than either researcher's solo work.

**Steelman the other direction too:** Klein's evidence is largely retrospective
interview-based and selected on notable incidents. Base rates of failed
intuitions in firefighting are not recorded. The asymmetry in evidence quality
between the two programmes is real, and their agreement should not be mistaken
for symmetric confirmation.

## Open questions

- [ ] Can you manufacture feedback in a low-validity domain and thereby create genuine intuitive expertise? Forecasting tournaments and surgical simulation are the two natural experiments; both look promising and neither is decisive.
- [ ] Why is mechanical prediction still resisted after seventy years? The sociology is more interesting than the statistics.
- [ ] How large is the chunk store really, and does template theory's revision change any practical implication?
- [ ] Is the expert blind spot correctable at all? Hinds's result says training helps little.
- [ ] Does the acceleration of chess mastery generalise? If accumulated pedagogy compresses the ten-year rule in chess, which other domains have compressible pedagogy and which have not been compressed yet?
- [ ] What is the base rate of failed expert intuition in the domains Klein studied? Nobody has the denominator.

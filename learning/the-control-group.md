# The Control Group — What Doesn't Work

> **Status: TIER 1 for most entries. Negative findings are often better
> evidenced than positive ones, because a null replicates more reliably than a
> small effect.**

> **Standing caveat: written from general knowledge without source
> verification.**

> **Why this file exists: a notebook that only lists what works is a sales
> brochure. The graveyard is where the field's actual epistemics are visible.
> Note how many of these are still funded, still taught in teacher training,
> and still believed by a large majority of practitioners years after being
> tested and failed.**

## The summary table

|---|---|---|---|

## The detailed entries

### Learning styles — the flagship failure

**The claim (the "meshing hypothesis"): people learn better when instruction
matches their preferred modality — visual, auditory, reading/writing,
kinaesthetic.** Fleming's VARK inventory (1987) is the most common instrument;
there are over 70 competing taxonomies.

**Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008)**, *Psychological Science in the
Public Interest*, made the decisive methodological point: **testing the meshing
hypothesis requires a crossover interaction.** You must (a) classify learners by
style, (b) randomly assign each to matched or mismatched instruction, (c) give
everyone the same test, and (d) find that visual learners do better with visual
instruction *and* auditory learners do better with auditory instruction. Nothing
less will do.

**Their review found almost no published study used this design. The handful
that did found no such interaction.** Later direct tests — Massa & Mayer
(2006); Rogowsky, Calhoun & Tallal (2015, 2020) — used the correct design and
found nothing.

**What is true, and gets confused with it:**
- People have genuine *preferences*. Preferences are real; they just don't
  predict learning outcomes.
- **Material has an optimal modality.** Teach geography with maps and music with
  sound. This is about the content, not the learner (Willingham's point).
- Multiple representations help **everyone** — that is dual coding (Mayer), and
  it is the exact opposite of the learning-styles prescription to give each
  learner one channel.

**Belief prevalence, which is the real story:** surveys repeatedly find roughly
**90% or more of teachers** endorse learning styles. Dekker, Lee, Howard-Jones
& Jolles (2012) found around 93% in UK and Dutch samples; Howard-Jones (2014),
*Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, reported comparable rates across five countries;
Newton & Salvi (2020) found around **89%** of educators internationally still
endorsed it, more than a decade after Pashler.

**Why it persists:** it is egalitarian (nobody is bad at learning, only
mismatched), it explains failure without blaming anyone, there is a
consultancy and inventory industry attached, and it has been embedded in
teacher training for thirty years. Debunking it also feels like an attack on
individualising instruction, which teachers reasonably value. **The neuromyth
literature is a case study in how professional consensus can be entirely
decoupled from evidence, in a profession that is not otherwise
anti-scientific.**

### Brain training

Covered in transfer.md. The short version: near transfer is robust, far transfer
is absent once you use active control groups, and the entire commercial claim is
a far-transfer claim.

**The tell:** across working-memory training, chess instruction, music training
and video-game training, **effect size is inversely proportional to control-
group quality.** Passive control → moderate effects. Active control → near
zero. That signature is what expectancy and demand characteristics look like,
and it appears in four independent literatures.

**ACTIVE trial caveat, in fairness:** the ACTIVE trial of cognitive training in
older adults did find durable effects on the trained abilities and, in the
speed-of-processing arm, some contested longer-term effects including a claimed
dementia-risk reduction. That last finding is disputed and comes from
secondary analyses; it is the strongest thing the field has and it is not
strong.

### Brain Gym

Educational Kinesiology. A set of 26 movements claimed to improve learning
through "brain buttons," "midline crossing," and "cerebral repatterning."
Widely adopted in UK primary schools through the 2000s.

**The stated mechanisms are physiologically false** — e.g. that pressing points
on the chest increases blood flow to the brain, that water is absorbed directly
through the roof of the mouth, that specific movements "reconnect"
hemispheres. Ben Goldacre's *Bad Science* documented the claims and their
adoption. **There is no credible controlled evidence of learning benefit.**

**Partial rescue, offered honestly:** movement breaks, hydration and short
physical activity plausibly help children concentrate. If Brain Gym works at
all, it works as a movement break, and any movement break would do — which is
exactly what an active control group would show, and which nobody ran.

### The learning pyramid — a fabrication

The claim: we retain 10% of what we read, 20% of what we hear, 30% of what we
see, 50% of what we see and hear, 70% of what we discuss, 90% of what we teach.

**These numbers have no source.** The pyramid is usually attributed to the NTL
Institute in Bethel, Maine, which has stated it cannot produce the original
research. It is often conflated with **Edgar Dale's Cone of Experience (1946)**,
which is a real thing and **contains no percentages at all** — Dale explicitly
warned against treating it as a hierarchy of effectiveness.

**Diagnostic feature: the numbers are all round multiples of ten.** No
empirical study of human memory has ever produced that. It is the clearest case
in the notebook of a citation-laundered fiction that is still on training
slides worldwide.

**And the underlying ranking is partly wrong anyway.** "Learning by teaching"
does have support — but the mechanism is retrieval practice and generation, not
the modality of delivery.

### The Mozart effect

**Rauscher, Shaw & Ky (1993)**, *Nature*, one page, **36 undergraduates**.
Listening to ten minutes of a Mozart sonata produced a temporary improvement on
a **paper-folding-and-cutting spatial task**, equivalent to about 8-9 IQ points,
**lasting 10-15 minutes**.

What the paper claimed: a short-lived improvement on one spatial task.
What the world heard: classical music makes babies smarter.

**Consequences:** Governor Zell Miller of Georgia budgeted for a classical CD
for every newborn in the state (1998); Florida legislated classical music in
state-funded daycare.

**Disconfirmation:**
- **Steele, Bass & Crook (1999)** and others failed to replicate.
- **Chabris (1999)** meta-analysis: **d ≈ 0.09**, and explicable as arousal.
- **Nantais & Schellenberg (1999)**: the effect is obtained equally with a
  **Stephen King audiobook**, provided the participant prefers it. **The
  mechanism is arousal and mood, not Mozart.**

**Why it persists:** it validates cultural preferences, it involves babies, and
"there is no Mozart effect" is a less pleasant headline than the original.

### Speed reading

**Rayner, Schotter, Masson, Potter & Treiman (2016)**, *Psychological Science in
the Public Interest*, "So Much to Read, So Little Time," is the comprehensive
treatment. The constraints are physiological and linguistic:

| Constraint | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Foveal span** | High-acuity vision covers roughly 7-8 characters. You cannot read a line "at a glance" |
| **Normal reading rate** | ~200-400 wpm with full comprehension. Higher rates trade off comprehension roughly monotonically |
| **Regressions are functional** | Skilled readers regress on ~10-15% of fixations to resolve ambiguity. RSVP methods (Spritz and similar) eliminate the possibility of regression, which is why comprehension falls |
| **The bottleneck is language, not vision** | Comprehension speed is limited by syntactic and semantic processing, not by eye movement |

**What is real:** skimming and scanning are genuine, teachable and useful — for
deciding what to read and for locating information. They are not reading.
Subvocalisation suppression does not produce the claimed gains. Kim Peek and a
handful of others are genuine outliers and not a training model.

**The honest gain available:** better *selection* of what to read, prior domain
knowledge (which does dramatically increase effective reading speed in that
domain), and not rereading passively. All boring, all real.

### Left-brain / right-brain

**Lateralisation is real** — language is left-dominant in roughly 95% of
right-handers; split-brain research (Sperry, Gazzaniga) is genuine and won a
Nobel in 1981. **The pedagogical application is not.**

**Nielsen, Zielinski, Ferguson, Lainhart & Anderson (2013)**, *PLoS ONE*:
resting-state fMRI in **1,011 individuals aged 7-29**. No evidence that
individuals have globally stronger left- or right-hemisphere networks. **There
are no left-brained or right-brained people.**

**Why it persists:** it maps onto a folk personality typology (logical vs
creative) that people find useful for self-description, and it has a real
scientific finding underneath, which makes it feel grounded.

### Digital natives

**Marc Prensky (2001)** coined it: people born after ~1980 supposedly process
information fundamentally differently and can multitask effectively.

**Kirschner & De Bruyckere (2017), "The Myths of the Digital Native and the
Multitasker,"** *Teaching and Teacher Education*: no evidence for a distinct
generational cognitive profile; young people's technology use is narrow
(social media and entertainment) and does not confer information-literacy
skill; **multitasking degrades performance for everyone, and those who
multitask most are, if anything, worse at it** (Ophir, Nass & Wagner, 2009).

**Why it persists:** it justifies technology purchases, it flatters the young,
and it gives institutions a reason to change that does not require evidence.

### Multiple intelligences

**Howard Gardner (1983)**, *Frames of Mind*: eight or nine relatively
independent intelligences. **The problem is empirical: the proposed
intelligences correlate positively when measured, which is the standard
positive-manifold finding that gave rise to *g* in the first place.** Waterhouse
(2006) reviewed the evidence and found no support for the modular claim.

**To Gardner's credit:** he has repeatedly said MI is not a prescription for
instruction, has explicitly rejected the conflation with learning styles, and
has objected to teachers "teaching to each intelligence." **The theory's
educational applications are not his and he says so.** The failure here is one
of adoption, not of the original claim's honesty.

### Whole language and the reading wars

**The highest-cost entry in this file.** The claim: children learn to read
naturally, as they learn to speak, through immersion in meaningful text;
explicit phonics instruction is unnecessary and damaging. Operationalised as
the three-cueing system — guess unknown words from context, picture cues, and
initial letter.

**The evidence:** the US **National Reading Panel (2000)** found systematic
phonics instruction produced significant benefit, largest for younger and
at-risk readers. The UK **Rose Review (2006)** reached the same conclusion.
Cognitive science on reading acquisition (Seidenberg, *Language at the Speed of
Sight*, 2017; Dehaene, *Reading in the Brain*, 2009) is not ambiguous: the
brain does not acquire reading the way it acquires speech.

**Three-cueing actively teaches the strategy that characterises poor readers.**
Skilled readers process nearly every letter of nearly every word; they do not
guess from context.

**Why it persisted for forty years:** deep ideological commitment, a large
publishing and professional-development industry, teacher-training curricula
that did not include the reading science, and the fact that roughly half of
children learn to read under almost any method — so the failure is only visible
in the tail.

### Self-esteem

**Baumeister, Campbell, Krueger & Vohs (2003)**, *Psychological Science in the
Public Interest*: reviewed the literature commissioned partly to defend the
self-esteem movement. Found self-esteem correlates weakly with achievement and
**the causal arrow runs from achievement to self-esteem**, not the reverse.
Raising self-esteem directly does not improve performance and in some studies
made it worse. California's state Task Force on self-esteem (1986-90) is the
canonical policy example.

## Why bad interventions survive — the general mechanisms

| Mechanism | How it operates |
|---|---|
| **Near transfer feels like far transfer** | You genuinely get better at the trained task. Both trainer and trainee observe real improvement and misattribute its scope |
| **No control group in daily life** | You never see what would have happened otherwise. Every intervention appears to work because students learn something during it |
| **Fluency illusion** | The intervention feels good. See what-actually-works.md — subjective learning is anti-correlated with actual learning over part of its range |
| **Commercial and professional constituencies** | Inventories, certifications, CPD days, consultancies, publishers. Debunking destroys revenue and identity simultaneously |
| **Moral framing** | Learning styles, multiple intelligences and self-esteem are all *kind*. Arguing against them reads as arguing against the children |
| **The correction never reaches the practitioner** | Pashler 2008 is in a journal teachers do not read. Newton & Salvi (2020) found ~89% endorsement twelve years later. **The bottleneck is transmission, not evidence** |
| **Effect-size inflation makes weak things look strong** | Small samples, researcher-designed outcomes, unblinded assessment. Cheung & Slavin (2016): researcher-made measures roughly double the effect of standardised ones† |

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular story | Reality |
|---|---|
| **"Learning styles have some truth to them"** | The specific testable claim — meshing — has been tested with the correct design and failed. Preferences exist and do not predict outcomes. There is no salvageable core |
| **"Brain training at least does no harm"** | It consumes time and money, and it displaces domain practice, which would have produced real domain gains. Opportunity cost is harm |
| **"These myths are held by the ignorant"** | ~90% of teachers endorse learning styles. This is a highly trained professional population. The failure is institutional transmission, not individual stupidity |
| **"The debunking is settled and everyone knows"** | Newton & Salvi (2020): ~89% endorsement, twelve years after the definitive review. Nothing about debunking is settled in practice |
| **"Scared Straight and DARE were just ineffective"** | Scared Straight **increased** offending in the Cochrane review. Interventions can be worse than nothing, and intuitive plausibility is no protection |
| **"Whole language was a reasonable theory that lost"** | It was contradicted by the reading science from early on, and the cost was borne by exactly the children with the least margin. It is the clearest case of ideology beating evidence in modern education |

## Open questions

- [ ] What is the actual cost of neuromyth belief in classroom time and outcomes? Everyone assumes it is large; nobody has measured it.
- [ ] Why did the learning-styles debunking fail to transmit? Is there a working model for correcting professional consensus?
- [ ] Does the ACTIVE trial's dementia finding survive scrutiny? It is the only serious positive result in cognitive training and it is a secondary analysis.
- [ ] Are there interventions in the "works" column that will end up here? My candidates: some multimedia design principles, the more elaborate spacing algorithms, and possibly parts of the interleaving literature at classroom scale.
- [ ] How many currently-taught practices have never been tested at all? Probably a large majority, and untested is not the same as refuted.
- [ ] Would preregistration and active-control requirements in education research have prevented any of these? Almost certainly several. Nobody has proposed it as policy.

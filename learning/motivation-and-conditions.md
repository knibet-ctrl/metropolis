# Motivation and Conditions — What Makes Practice Possible

> **Status: TIER 2 throughout, with two TIER 1 items (the conditions problem,
> and the feedback-harm finding) and two heavily oversold items handled at
> length (growth mindset, grit).**

> **Standing caveat: written from general knowledge without source
> verification. This is the file where the replication crisis has done the most
> damage, and where the gap between the popular and the scientific literature
> is widest.**

---

## The uncomfortable framing first

Deliberate practice, as defined in deliberate-practice.md, requires: a coach or
expert model, immediate informative feedback, hours of protected time daily,
tolerance for sustained unpleasant effort, and years of continuity.

**Most people do not have these, and no amount of motivation supplies them.**

| Requirement | Who has it | Distribution |
|---|---|---|
| Expert coaching | Children of the affluent; institutional athletes; conservatoire students; a few apprenticeship trades | Extremely unequal |
| Immediate feedback | Whoever is in a domain that provides it | Determined by domain, not by effort |
| 3-4 protected hours daily | Almost nobody with a job or dependents | Extremely unequal |
| Continuity over a decade | Requires financial stability | Extremely unequal |

**Motivation literature systematically studies the last mile of a road most
people cannot reach the start of.** This is not a reason to ignore it. It is a
reason to treat "they lacked motivation" as a hypothesis of last resort.

## Self-Determination Theory

**Edward Deci & Richard Ryan**, from the mid-1970s; *Intrinsic Motivation and
Self-Determination in Human Behavior* (1985); the summary statement in
*American Psychologist* (2000).

Three basic psychological needs, whose satisfaction predicts intrinsic
motivation, persistence and wellbeing:

| Need | Definition | In a learning context |
|---|---|---|
| **Autonomy** | Volition; experiencing behaviour as self-endorsed | Choice over what and how; rationale given for requirements. Note: autonomy ≠ independence, and SDT does not claim structure is harmful |
| **Competence** | Effectance; experiencing oneself as capable | Optimally challenging tasks. Maps directly onto desirable difficulty |
| **Relatedness** | Connection and mattering to others | Teacher and peer relationships; the least-studied of the three and probably the most important in practice |

**Status: TIER 2, well-supported but with the field's standard problems.** SDT
has an enormous literature and a large share of it is cross-sectional
self-report from researchers committed to the theory. The **autonomy-support**
intervention literature is the strongest part; the needs taxonomy is more a
useful framework than a demonstrated decomposition. Cross-cultural
universality of autonomy as a need has been contested (Iyengar & Lepper 1999
found Asian-American children performed better when choices were made by
trusted others), and SDT's reply — that this is a different *expression* of the
same need — is close to unfalsifiable.

## The overjustification effect

**The claim: rewarding an already-intrinsically-motivated behaviour reduces
subsequent voluntary engagement in it.**

| Study | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Deci (1971)** | Soma puzzle; paid vs unpaid; free-choice period observed | Paid participants spent less free time on the puzzles after payment stopped |
| **Lepper, Greene & Nisbett (1973)** | Preschoolers who liked drawing; expected-award, unexpected-award, no-award | The **expected-award** group drew substantially less in free play afterwards. Unexpected reward did no harm. **The expectancy, not the reward, is the mechanism** |
| **Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999)** | Meta-analysis, 128 experiments | Tangible, expected, task-contingent rewards undermine intrinsic motivation. Verbal praise and unexpected rewards do not |

**Contested.** Cameron & Pierce (1994) meta-analysed the same literature and
concluded the undermining effect was limited and overstated; a genuinely bitter
exchange followed. The current fair reading:

- The effect is **real but narrow**: it requires pre-existing intrinsic
  interest, a tangible expected reward contingent on doing the task, and it
  shows up mainly in free-choice persistence rather than in performance.
- **Cerasoli, Nicklin & Ford (2014)** meta-analysis gives the most useful
  practical formulation: **intrinsic motivation predicts quality of
  performance; incentives predict quantity.** Directly-salient incentives
  weaken the intrinsic-performance link; indirectly-salient ones do not.
- For tasks with no intrinsic interest, rewards do not undermine anything,
  because there was nothing to undermine.

**Practical implication:** do not attach payment to something you want someone
to keep doing voluntarily. Do attach it to volume of something they would never
do for its own sake. Beware of Goodhart on top of this — see risk/ and
knowledge/ on metrics becoming targets.

## Growth mindset — the full story

**Carol Dweck**, from the 1980s; *Mindset* (2006). The claim: believing ability
is malleable (incremental theory) rather than fixed (entity theory) produces
better responses to failure, more effort, and better achievement.

### The original findings

| Study | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Mueller & Dweck (1998)** | Children praised for *intelligence* after success subsequently chose easier tasks, showed less persistence, and performed worse after failure than children praised for *effort*. Six studies |
| **Blackwell, Trzesniewski & Dweck (2007)** | Longitudinal: 7th-graders' mindset predicted maths trajectory over two years; an eight-session intervention reversed a declining trend |
| **Aronson, Fried & Good (2002)** | Mindset intervention improved grades, with larger effects for Black students |

These are good studies with a coherent theory. The theory was then scaled into
a global educational movement, corporate training, and government policy.

### The replication record — and it is bad

| Study | Result |
|---|---|
| **Sisk, Burgoyne, Butler, Macnamara (2018)**, *Psychological Science* | **Two meta-analyses.** Mindset-achievement correlation: **r ≈ 0.10** (weak). Intervention effects on achievement: **d ≈ 0.08** overall, and non-significant in most subgroups. **Effects were detectable for low-SES and academically at-risk students** — a real but narrow result |
| **Yeager et al. (2019)**, *Nature* | **National Study of Learning Mindsets.** n ≈ 12,490, 65 US schools, nationally representative, preregistered. A short online intervention raised GPA for lower-achieving students by roughly **0.10 grade points**, and increased enrolment in advanced maths. **Effects depended on peer norms — the intervention only worked where the school's culture supported challenge-seeking** |
| **Foliano, Rolfe, Buzzeo, Runge & Wilkinson (2019)**, EEF (UK) | Large randomised trial. **Null** |
| **Li & Bates (2019)** | Direct replications of Dweck's core praise/failure paradigms in China. **Failed to replicate** the mindset effects on response to failure |
| **Macnamara & Burgoyne (2023)** | Meta-analysis correcting for publication bias and design quality. Effects **near zero** in the highest-quality studies |
| **Bahník & Vranka (2017)** | n ≈ 5,600 Czech university applicants. Growth mindset was **slightly negatively** associated with test scores |

### The fair reading

**Growth mindset is a real, small effect, concentrated in specific
populations, dependent on context, and it was oversold by roughly an order of
magnitude.**

- The Yeager 2019 result is the best-designed study and it found something. A
  0.10 GPA effect from a one-hour online intervention is, in
  cost-effectiveness terms, respectable — Kraft's benchmarks would call it a
  meaningful effect for a nearly-free intervention.
- It is **not** a general theory of achievement, it does not work for
  everyone, and school-wide "growth mindset culture" programmes have a poor
  trial record.
- **Dweck's own response** to the replication failures has been partly
  substantive (context dependence, fidelity of implementation, the "false
  growth mindset" of praising effort regardless of strategy) and partly
  unfalsifiable-sounding. Both readings are available and honest people
  disagree.

**Steelman for Dweck:** many replication attempts used one-shot online
manipulations with no fidelity checks in populations where the intervention was
not designed to apply; the theory always specified moderators; and the
best-powered, best-designed study in the whole literature (Yeager 2019) found a
positive effect. That is not a pattern of a false theory.

**Steelman against:** the effect sizes in the original papers were far larger
than anything found since, the paradigm-defining praise studies failed direct
replication, and the movement's policy influence was built on the original
magnitudes, not the corrected ones. The correction has not propagated: teacher
training materials still cite the 2006 book.

## Grit

**Angela Duckworth, Peterson, Matthews & Kelly (2007)**: grit as "perseverance
and passion for long-term goals." Two facets — consistency of interests, and
perseverance of effort. Predicted West Point summer training retention,
National Spelling Bee performance, and educational attainment.

**Credé, Tynan & Harms (2017)**, meta-analysis, **88 samples, n ≈ 67,000**:

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Grit correlates with conscientiousness at about r = 0.84**† | At that level the constructs are close to indistinguishable after measurement error. This is the central charge and it is serious |
| **The two facets behave differently** | *Perseverance of effort* predicts performance; *consistency of interests* barely does. The higher-order "grit" construct averages a predictor with a non-predictor |
| **Grit-performance correlation with academic performance** | Around r = 0.18 for GPA† — real, modest, and **not larger than conscientiousness measured directly** |
| **Incremental validity over conscientiousness** | Small to negligible |

**Duckworth's response** has been reasonably gracious: she has publicly said
grit was oversold, cautioned against using it for school accountability, and
acknowledged the conscientiousness overlap. She has continued to argue that the
perseverance facet has practical utility as a self-directed frame even if it is
not a new construct.

**The fair reading: grit is largely a repackaging of conscientiousness with a
better name and a TED talk. Conscientiousness is one of the best-validated
predictors in psychology, so this is not nothing — but the marginal scientific
content is small, and using grit scores to sort children is unsupported.**

## Feedback — the finding that should be better known

**Kluger & DeNisi (1996)**, meta-analysis of **607 effect sizes** from 131
studies with adequate controls:

- Average effect of feedback interventions on performance: **d ≈ 0.41**.
- **In over one third of cases, feedback interventions REDUCED performance.**†

The moderator: **where feedback directs attention.**

| Feedback directed at | Effect |
|---|---|
| **The task**, and how to do it better | Positive, usually substantial |
| **Task-motivation processes** | Positive |
| **The self** — praise, criticism, comparison to others, ranking | **Often negative.** Attention moves from the task to the ego, and self-regulatory resources go to protecting the self-image |

**"Give more feedback" is therefore not advice.** Feedback that names the person
rather than the work is a live risk of harm, and it is the default register of
most performance management, most grading, and most parenting.

This connects directly to Mueller & Dweck's praise result, which survives even
if the wider mindset literature is weak: **praising the person is a different
intervention from praising the work, and the first has a worse track record.**

## Interest, and the "follow your passion" problem

**O'Keefe, Dweck & Walton (2018)**: people holding a "fixed theory of interest"
— that passions are discovered, not developed — showed less openness to new
interests and gave up faster when a new field turned out to be difficult.
Modest studies, sensible mechanism, unreplicated at scale.

The practical point does not depend on the study: **interest is substantially
downstream of competence, not upstream of it.** People typically come to like
things they are getting good at. That maps onto SDT's competence need and onto
the ordinary observation that the passion arrives after the plateau is passed,
not before. **Advice to "find your passion first" inverts the causal order for
most people and licenses quitting during the phase where quitting is most
tempting and least justified.**

Held loosely — this is TIER 3. It is a widely shared clinical impression with
weak experimental support.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular story | Reality |
|---|---|
| **Growth mindset transforms achievement** | r ≈ 0.10 correlational; d ≈ 0.08 interventional; the best trial found ~0.10 GPA points for lower achievers only, contingent on school norms. Real, small, oversold by an order of magnitude |
| **Grit is a new discovery that explains success** | It correlates with conscientiousness at ~0.84 and adds little incremental prediction. The useful half is "perseverance of effort," which is the conscientiousness facet |
| **Rewards always destroy motivation** | Only for tasks with pre-existing intrinsic interest, when the reward is tangible, expected and task-contingent. Unexpected rewards and verbal praise do not undermine |
| **Rewards never destroy motivation (the economist's reply)** | Also wrong. Deci, Koestner & Ryan's 128-study meta-analysis is not explained away, and Cerasoli et al. reconcile the two literatures rather than dismissing one |
| **Feedback is good; give more of it** | **Over a third of feedback interventions reduce performance.** Feedback aimed at the self rather than the task is the culprit. This is the most actionable finding in the file |
| **Find your passion, then the work is easy** | Interest largely follows competence. The passion-first framing predicts quitting at the hard part |
| **Motivation is the bottleneck** | For most people the bottleneck is coaching, feedback and protected time. Calling that a motivation problem misattributes a structural constraint to a character defect |
| **These findings are replicated psychology** | Much of this literature is exactly the part of psychology that replicated worst. The SDT, mindset and grit literatures should all be held more loosely than they are presented |

## Open questions

- [ ] Does the Yeager 2019 mindset effect replicate outside the US, and does it persist beyond one year? The EEF null is a warning sign.
- [ ] Is the Kluger & DeNisi one-third-harmful figure robust? It is thirty years old and load-bearing for a lot of advice, mine included.
- [ ] What is the incremental validity of the perseverance facet over conscientiousness in a well-powered preregistered study? Credé's meta-analysis suggests near zero; a direct test would settle it.
- [ ] Is autonomy a universal need or a culturally-specific expression of one? SDT's reply to Iyengar & Lepper is hard to falsify.
- [ ] Can the "conditions" problem be measured? Nobody quantifies access to coaching and feedback as a variable, so it never appears in models — which guarantees motivation absorbs its variance.
- [ ] Does interest genuinely follow competence, prospectively? The claim is plausible, widely believed, and thinly evidenced.

# Case: Modern Collapses

> **Status: Tier 1 on events, Tier 2 on causation.** These are the only
> collapses we can observe with statistics, film, surviving participants and
> declassified archives. **They are therefore the calibration set for
> everything we believe about the ancient cases — and what they show is that
> our ancient reconstructions are probably too tidy.**

**Written from general knowledge without live source verification.** Mortality
and emigration figures below are politically contested and must be checked.

---

## Why the modern cases matter disproportionately

For Rome or the Maya we have destruction layers, monument dates and elite
narrative sources. For the USSR we have oil price series, grain import records,
Politburo minutes, television footage, opinion polling and thousands of living
witnesses.

**And the modern cases keep showing us things we would never have inferred from
material evidence:**

1. **Collapse can be extremely fast.** The USSR went from a superpower with an
   intact army and no defeat in the field to non-existence in about two years.
   Nothing in the material record would show that.
2. **Collapse can happen without population decline.** In 1991 nobody starved
   at the moment of dissolution. The archaeology would show continuity.
3. **Elites can dismantle a state deliberately.** The Belovezha Accords were
   signed by three men in a hunting lodge. No archaeological method could
   recover that.
4. **Almost nobody sees it coming, including the specialists.** Essentially the
   entire Western Sovietology profession, with access to better data than any
   ancient historian will ever have, failed to predict 1991. **This should
   permanently lower our confidence in "they should have seen it coming"
   arguments about ancient elites.**

# 1. The Soviet Union, 1991 — the best-documented state collapse

## The sequence

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1979–89 | Afghanistan war: cost, casualties, and a visible failure of the military-ideological project |
| 1985 | Gorbachev becomes General Secretary; *perestroika* and *glasnost* begin |
| **1986** | **Oil price collapse.** Saudi output increases; prices fall by roughly two-thirds. Soviet hard-currency earnings depended heavily on hydrocarbon exports |
| April 1986 | Chernobyl. The disaster and the initial cover-up damaged the system's credibility with its own population more than any dissident had |
| 1988–89 | Nationalist movements in the Baltics, Caucasus and Ukraine. The Baltic "Human Chain" of August 1989 involved roughly two million people |
| 1989 | Eastern Europe goes, and **Moscow does not intervene** — the single most consequential decision of the period |
| 1990 | Republics declare sovereignty; the Communist Party's constitutional monopoly (Article 6) is abolished |
| March 1991 | Union referendum: about 76% vote to preserve a renewed Union — **but six republics boycotted it entirely, and the question was ambiguously worded.** Cite this figure with care |
| **19–21 Aug 1991** | Hardline coup attempt. Collapses in three days. Yeltsin on the tank outside the White House. **The coup's failure destroyed the central state's remaining authority** |
| **8 Dec 1991** | Belovezha Accords: the leaders of Russia, Ukraine and Belarus declare the USSR dissolved |
| **25 Dec 1991** | Gorbachev resigns. The flag comes down over the Kremlin |

## What caused it — the competing accounts, steelmanned

| Account | Core claim | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Economic / oil (Yegor Gaidar)** | The system had become dependent on oil revenue to buy grain and consumer goods; the 1986 price collapse made the fiscal position unsustainable and forced borrowing and then reform | Quantitative, dated, mechanism explicit | Other petrostates survived the same shock |
| **Systemic economic failure** | Central planning could not process the information required to run a modern consumer economy; the Hayekian calculation problem, arriving on schedule | Explains stagnation from the 1970s | Explains decline, not the *timing* of dissolution |
| **Nationalities / federal design** | The USSR was constitutionally a federation of national republics with formal secession rights and titular national elites. Once the centre weakened, the structure supplied ready-made successor states | Explains why it broke *along those lines* exactly | Doesn't explain why the centre weakened |
| **Gorbachev's agency** | Reform destabilised the system faster than it improved it; *glasnost* removed the fear that made compliance rational without replacing it with performance | Contemporaries agreed; Gorbachev remains unpopular in Russia for this reason | Counterfactual — the unreformed system had its own trajectory |
| **Elite defection (Stephen Kotkin's "uncivil society")** | The nomenklatura discovered it could convert political power into private property. **The people who ran the state had more to gain from its dissolution than its survival** | Explains the bloodlessness and the 1990s asset grab | Understates genuine popular nationalism |
| **Ideological exhaustion (Alexei Yurchak)** | *Everything Was Forever, Until It Was No More* (2005): the system's official discourse had become pure ritual form, performed by everyone and believed by no one. The collapse was therefore **simultaneously totally unexpected and completely unsurprising** | Best account of the *phenomenology*; explains why nobody predicted it and nobody was shocked | Not a causal mechanism in the usual sense |
| **External pressure / Reagan** | Arms racing and Afghanistan imposed unbearable costs | Popular in US political memory | Soviet military spending was already high and stable; most specialists weight this well below the internal factors |

**Best synthesis: a stagnant system, an exogenous revenue shock, a reform
programme that removed coercion before replacing it with performance, a federal
structure that pre-fabricated the successor states, and an elite that
calculated it would do better on the other side.** No single factor is
sufficient. This is exactly the multi-causal shape we infer for ancient
collapses — and here we can actually see it.

## The delayed human cost

**The dissolution itself was nearly bloodless. The decade after it was not.**

- Russian male life expectancy fell from roughly 64 years in 1990 to about
  57 by 1994 — a peacetime decline with almost no precedent in an
  industrialised country.
- Excess mortality estimates for 1990s Russia commonly run in the range of
  2–3 million, and are contested. **Treat any specific figure as contested.**
- Alcohol — especially binge spirit drinking — is the best-established proximate
  cause in the epidemiological literature (Leon, Shkolnikov and colleagues), on
  top of collapsed healthcare, unemployment shock and loss of social role.
- **"Shock therapy" is the central political dispute.** Sachs and defenders
  argue rapid liberalisation was necessary and that the failure was in the
  stabilisation and aid that never arrived; Stiglitz and critics argue
  sequencing mattered and that privatisation before institutions produced the
  oligarchy. The 1995 loans-for-shares scheme, which transferred major
  resource companies to a handful of bankers at low prices, is the exhibit both
  sides argue about.

**The lesson that transfers: the collapse event and the human catastrophe were
separated by several years. If you were reading only the political record you
would date the disaster wrongly.** Now apply that thought to the ancient cases,
where we have only the political record.

# 2. Yugoslavia, 1991–2001 — collapse into war

**What happened:** after Tito's death in 1980, a rotating federal presidency
managed an increasingly indebted, decentralised federation. Economic crisis and
IMF-conditioned austerity through the 1980s eroded federal legitimacy. Slobodan
Milošević's rise from 1987 rebuilt Serbian politics on national grievance.
Slovenia and Croatia declared independence in 1991; war followed in Croatia,
then Bosnia (1992–95), then Kosovo (1998–99), then North Macedonia (2001).
Roughly **140,000 deaths** across the wars is a commonly cited total; the
Bosnian war alone is usually given at around 100,000. **Srebrenica in July 1995
— 8,372 named victims — was ruled a genocide by international courts.**

**The causal dispute matters:**

- **"Ancient hatreds"** (associated with Robert Kaplan's *Balkan Ghosts*, and
  influential on Western policymakers in the early 1990s) — largely **rejected**
  by area specialists. Intermarriage rates in Bosnian cities were high; the
  conflict was not a resumption of a permanent state.
- **Elite instrumentalism** (V.P. Gagnon and others) — nationalist conflict was
  *manufactured* by threatened elites to demobilise domestic opposition to
  economic reform. **The demand for ethnic conflict was created, not
  discovered.**
- **Structural/economic** (Susan Woodward's *Balkan Tragedy*) — debt crisis,
  austerity and the incentives of a decentralised federation set republics
  against each other before any nationalist entrepreneur did.

**Why this case matters for the notebook: it is direct, documented evidence that
"ethnic collapse" narratives can be constructed in real time by identifiable
people for identifiable reasons.** When we read an ancient source explaining
that a collapse was caused by the eternal enmity of some group, this is the
control case for what such explanations are often doing.

# 3. Venezuela, 2013– — collapse without war or state dissolution

**The most extreme peacetime economic contraction in modern records.**

| Indicator | Approximate figure | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| GDP decline, ~2013–2021 | **Around 75%** | High as a direction; the precise figure is disputed and official statistics stopped |
| Hyperinflation peak | Annualised rates in the hundreds of thousands to over a million percent, ~2018–19 | Directionally certain, precisely unreliable |
| Emigration | **Around 7–7.7 million** people, per UNHCR/IOM regional response figures | Moderate; one of the largest displacement events in the world |
| Oil production | Fell from roughly 3 million barrels/day to a fraction of that | High |

**Mechanism:** an economy dependent on one export, whose state oil company
(PDVSA) was progressively staffed for political loyalty after the 2002–03
management strike, hit by the 2014 oil price fall, with price controls and a
multi-tier fixed exchange rate that created enormous arbitrage rents,
compounded by monetisation of the deficit.

**The contested part — US sanctions.** Financial sanctions from 2017 and oil
sanctions from 2019 unquestionably deepened the crisis. **The crisis began
before them**, which is the strongest point against the sanctions-caused-it
argument. How much of the *depth* is attributable to sanctions is a live
empirical question with partisan answers on both sides, and anyone confident
either way is telling you their politics.

**What makes it striking: the state did not fall.** Maduro's government survived
a 75% economic contraction, hyperinflation, mass emigration, an international
recognition campaign for a rival president, and sustained protest.
**Regime survival and economic collapse are much more separable than the
collapse literature usually assumes.**

# 4. Near-misses and recoveries — the under-studied column

**If you only study collapses, you learn nothing about what prevented them.**

| Case | Shock | Outcome | What did the work |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cuba, "Special Period," 1991–95** | Loss of Soviet subsidy: imports fell by ~80%, oil by more than half; average calorie intake fell sharply | **State survived; no state collapse** | Rationing, urban agriculture, a coercive apparatus intact, and a population with nowhere to exit |
| **South Korea, 1997** | Asian financial crisis; IMF programme | Full recovery within ~2 years | Existing industrial capacity, institutional depth, external support |
| **Argentina, 2001–02** | Default, currency board collapse, five presidents in two weeks | Recovered; recurrent crises since | Food self-sufficiency, an urban middle class, functioning courts |
| **Greece, 2010–18** | ~25% GDP contraction, comparable to a depression | Remained in the EU and the euro; recovered slowly | External institutional anchor; no currency to collapse |
| **Rwanda, post-1994** | Genocide; ~800,000 dead in ~100 days | State rebuilt; sustained growth | Authoritarian consolidation plus large aid inflows. **A morally complicated case, not a template** |
| **Germany, 1923–24** | Hyperinflation | Currency stabilised within months (Rentenmark) | Institutional and industrial capacity survived the monetary destruction |
| **UK, 1976** | IMF loan | Recovered | Institutional continuity |

**What the recoveries appear to share** (Tier 2, this is pattern-matching on a
small non-random sample):

1. **Food security or the ability to buy food.** The cases that recovered could
   feed people. This is the least glamorous and most consistent factor.
2. **An intact administrative and legal apparatus** underneath the failing
   political layer. Bureaucracies that keep functioning during a political
   crisis are what recovery is built on.
3. **A credible external anchor** — the EU, the IMF, an alliance — supplying
   both money and a story about the future.
4. **A succession mechanism that worked.** Argentina cycled five presidents in
   two weeks *constitutionally*. That is a functioning system under extreme
   stress, not a failing one.
5. **Exit options mattered both ways.** Emigration relieved pressure in
   Venezuela and Greece while also stripping human capital.

## What separates modern from ancient collapses

| Dimension | Ancient | Modern |
|---|---|---|
| **Speed** | Typically 50–250 years | 2–10 years |
| **Documentation** | Elite monuments, scattered texts | Statistics, film, archives, survivors |
| **Population effect** | Sometimes severe | **Generally none at the moment of collapse; delayed excess mortality instead** |
| **Exit** | Move to the next valley | International migration, remittances |
| **Rescue** | None available | IMF, EU, aid, diaspora money |
| **Knowledge** | No comparative cases available to participants | Participants had read about previous collapses |
| **Successor states** | Improvised | **Pre-existing administrative units with borders, bureaucracies and flags** |
| **What "collapse" means** | Loss of complexity, sometimes of population | Loss of a *state*, with the society continuing |

**The most important asymmetry: modern societies collapse politically while
remaining materially and demographically intact.** Ukraine in 1992 had the same
factories, roads, engineers and universities it had in 1990. The Petén in 900 CE
did not.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Reagan brought down the Soviet Union" | Most specialists weight internal stagnation, the 1986 oil shock, Gorbachev's reforms and elite defection far higher. External pressure is a minor term |
| "The Soviet people rose up and overthrew communism" | The decisive events were an elite coup attempt that failed and an elite agreement in a hunting lodge. Mass mobilisation mattered in the Baltics and less elsewhere |
| "Nobody suffered because it was peaceful" | The 1990s Russian mortality crisis is one of the worst peacetime health disasters on record |
| "Yugoslavia collapsed because of ancient ethnic hatreds" | Rejected by specialists. Elite manufacture of conflict is far better supported |
| "Sanctions caused Venezuela's collapse" / "Sanctions were irrelevant" | The collapse preceded the sanctions; the sanctions deepened it. Both confident positions are political |
| "Collapse means the country empties or dies" | Venezuela lost ~a quarter of its population to emigration and the state survived. Cuba lost 80% of its imports and nothing fell |
| "Experts saw it coming" | **Sovietology, with far better data than any ancient historian, missed 1991 almost completely.** Randall Collins's geopolitical prediction of Soviet decline (from around 1980) is the notable exception and is one data point |

## Open questions

- [ ] What is the defensible excess-mortality figure for 1990s Russia, and how
      much is attributable to policy versus transition per se?
- [ ] Would a non-Gorbachev USSR have survived the 1990s? The strongest
      counterfactual in modern history and untestable
- [ ] Why did Cuba's state survive a comparable import shock when others didn't?
      Coercive capacity, isolation, or food policy?
- [ ] Can the "elite defection" mechanism be identified prospectively, or only
      after the fact?
- [ ] How much of Venezuela's contraction is attributable to sanctions? An
      answerable question that nobody is answering neutrally
- [ ] Is the absence of population decline in modern collapses a permanent
      feature of industrial societies, or an artefact of the specific cases —
      i.e. would a collapse with disrupted food logistics look ancient again?
- [ ] Why did the entire Sovietology profession miss it, and what is the
      analogous blind spot now?

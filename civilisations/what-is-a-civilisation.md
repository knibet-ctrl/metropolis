# What Is a Civilisation?

> **Status: Tier 2, sliding into Tier 4 at the edges.** The empirical questions
> — when did states form, what did agriculture do to human bodies — are
> tractable and partly answered. The definitional question "what counts as a
> civilisation" has no fact of the matter and the attempt to settle it has
> wasted a century.

**Written from general knowledge without live source verification.** Dates for
early state formation are especially uncertain and revised regularly.

---

## The word is contaminated

"Civilisation" entered wide use in the 18th century as a ranking device. It
sorted societies into civilised and savage, and the sorting was not incidental
to the word — it was the point. Lewis Henry Morgan's *Ancient Society* (1877)
formalised the ladder as savagery → barbarism → civilisation, and that scheme
was absorbed by Engels and by colonial administration alike.

**We keep the word because the field is called that. We should not pretend it is
a neutral analytic category.** Everything below uses "complex society" where
precision matters.

The most-cited checklist is V. Gordon Childe's (1950), sometimes given as ten
traits: cities, full-time specialists, surplus concentration, monumental
architecture, a ruling class, writing, predictive sciences, monumental art,
long-distance trade, and state-organised society. It is a useful heuristic and
a bad definition:

| Childe trait | Counterexample |
|---|---|
| Writing | The Inca ran an empire of ~10 million with *quipu* knot-records and no phonetic script |
| Cities | Ancient Egypt's Old Kingdom was overwhelmingly rural and administered from courts, not cities |
| Monumental architecture | Göbekli Tepe (~9500 BCE) has monuments and, apparently, no agriculture, no city, no state |
| Ruling class | Tripolye/Cucuteni mega-sites in Ukraine (~4100–3300 BCE) housed thousands with no identified palaces or elite burials |
| Surplus concentration | Some Amazonian earthwork societies were large, managed and dispersed |

**Conclusion: "civilisation" is a family-resemblance term. There is no set of
necessary and sufficient conditions and the search for one has produced nothing.**

## What actually happened: sedentism, agriculture, states

The sequence people are taught — *farming → surplus → cities → states →
civilisation* — is wrong in its causal ordering and wrong on timing.

| Approximate date | Event | Note |
|---|---|---|
| ~23,000 BP | Ohalo II (Israel): wild cereal processing | Pre-agricultural grain use |
| ~12,000–9500 BCE | Natufian sedentism in the Levant | **Sedentism precedes agriculture, not the reverse** |
| ~9500 BCE | Göbekli Tepe monumental enclosures | Monuments by foragers; overturned the standard sequence |
| ~9000–7000 BCE | Domestication of wheat, barley, sheep, goats | Gradual, over millennia, not a "revolution" |
| ~7100–5700 BCE | Çatalhöyük | Thousands of people, no palaces, no clear rulers |
| ~4000–3000 BCE | Uruk expansion, Mesopotamia | Cities and temple-administered economies |
| ~3400–3100 BCE | Proto-cuneiform; Egyptian unification | **The first cuneiform texts are overwhelmingly accounting** |
| ~2600–1900 BCE | Indus/Harappan urbanism | Standardised weights, planned drainage, undeciphered script, no clear palaces or royal iconography |
| ~1600 BCE onward | Shang China, oracle bones | |
| ~1200 BCE–900 CE | Olmec → Maya → Teotihuacan | Independent invention of writing and states in Mesoamerica |
| ~900 CE–1532 | Wari, Tiwanaku, Inca | Andean states without phonetic writing |

**The gap between the first farming and the first states is roughly four
thousand years.** That is the single most important fact for undoing the
standard story. Agriculture does not automatically produce states. Something
else has to happen.

**James C. Scott's answer (*Against the Grain*, 2017), steelmanned:** early
states are built on **cereal grains specifically**, because grains are
*legible* — they ripen predictably, above ground, at a known time, and can be
counted, divided, transported and stored. A tax collector can assess a wheat
field. He cannot assess a cassava patch, because tubers stay in the ground and
can be dug up whenever you want. On this account the state is a **grain-and-
manpower appropriation machine**, and the early state's biggest problem was not
producing food but stopping people from leaving. Hence walls (which keep people
in as much as out), hence the ubiquity of slavery and corvée in early states,
hence the recurrent state obsession with counting people.

**Steelman of the opposing view:** the appropriation story understates the
genuine public goods early states supplied. Flood control, irrigation
coordination, famine buffering through granaries, dispute resolution and
protection from raiding are real services, and people did in fact move toward
states as well as away from them. The "hydraulic hypothesis" (Wittfogel, 1957)
in its strong form — irrigation *caused* despotism — is dead, refuted by the
finding that in Mesopotamia and elsewhere large-scale irrigation systems
typically *postdate* state formation rather than preceding it. But the weaker
claim, that coordination problems make centralisation valuable, survives. **The
honest position is that early states were both protection rackets and providers
of genuine coordination, and the mix varied.**

## The agricultural revolution as a disaster: the contested case

This is the highest-value contested claim in the file, so it gets a full
treatment on both sides.

**The case that agriculture made individual lives worse:**

| Evidence | Detail | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Stature decline | Skeletal series across the Neolithic transition in the Near East, Europe, and later in Mesoamerica and North America show height falling — commonly cited as several centimetres | Strong, replicated in multiple regions |
| Dental pathology | Caries rates rise sharply with carbohydrate-heavy diets; enamel hypoplasia (a marker of childhood stress) increases | Strong |
| Anaemia markers | Porotic hyperostosis and cribra orbitalia increase | Moderate — the aetiology of these lesions is debated |
| Diet narrowing | Foragers ate dozens to hundreds of species; early farmers depended on two or three | Strong |
| Zoonotic disease | Measles, smallpox, influenza, tuberculosis largely enter human populations via domesticated animals and dense settlement | Strong on origin; timing debated |
| Workload | Time-allocation studies of extant foragers (e.g. Marlowe on the Hadza; earlier, Lee on the Ju/'hoansi) suggest subsistence work well below the agricultural norm | Moderate — extant foragers live in marginal habitats and may not represent Pleistocene conditions |
| Famine vulnerability | A failed monoculture harvest has no fallback; a diversified forager range does | Strong in principle |

The classic collection is Cohen and Armelagos, *Paleopathology at the Origins of
Agriculture* (1984). The popular version is the "biggest mistake in the history
of the human race" line, made famous by Diamond in 1987 and by Harari's
*Sapiens* (2011).

**Steelman of the defence of agriculture:**

1. **It won overwhelmingly on fertility.** Sedentism shortens birth intervals
   (no need to carry two children at once; weaning foods available). Farming
   populations out-reproduced foragers by a wide margin. On the only currency
   evolution counts, agriculture was not a mistake — it was the most successful
   subsistence strategy in human history. "Bad for individuals, good for
   populations" is the standard reconciliation and it is coherent.
2. **The osteological paradox** (Wood et al., 1992) is a serious methodological
   objection that cuts against the pessimists. Skeletons showing chronic-stress
   lesions are skeletons of people who *survived long enough to develop
   lesions*. A population with more visible pathology may be a population with
   better survival of stress, not worse health. This is not a fringe point; it
   is the standard caution in the field, and it is under-reported in popular
   treatments of the "agriculture was a disaster" story.
3. **Sample bias.** Forager skeletal samples are small, scattered and drawn
   from different environments than the farming samples they are compared with.
4. **The comparison is between the wrong things.** The relevant counterfactual
   is not "forager life at low density" but "what happens to a population that
   has already grown past the forager carrying capacity."
5. **Everything downstream depended on it.** Whatever the individual cost,
   literacy, medicine, and the possibility of a life not spent entirely on
   subsistence all sit on the agricultural surplus.

**Where this lands:** the pessimist case on *individual health in the first few
millennia* is Tier 1–2 and largely holds, with the osteological paradox as a
real weakening. The maximalist framing — "the worst mistake in human history" —
is a rhetorical position, not a finding, and it quietly assumes a welfare metric
(average individual wellbeing) over alternatives (total wellbeing, population,
capability) without arguing for it.

## Writing and bureaucracy

**Writing was invented for accounting, not literature, and this is not close.**

The Uruk IV proto-cuneiform corpus (~3300 BCE) is dominated by administrative
records: quantities of grain, beer, textiles, livestock, labour rations. The
same is true of Linear B, which recorded Mycenaean palace inventories — sheep
counts, chariot wheels, personnel lists — and of the earliest Egyptian labels.
Narrative and religious literature arrives centuries later.

This matters for collapse for a specific reason: **writing is the state's
sensing organ, and its loss is both a symptom of collapse and a cause of our
blindness to what followed.** When Linear B disappears from Greece after the
palaces burn c. 1200 BC, we lose ~400 years of visibility — the so-called Greek
Dark Age. Excavations at Lefkandi on Euboea, notably the Toumba building
(c. 1000–950 BC, with a rich warrior-and-woman burial and imported goods),
show that this was much less dark than the term implies. **We had confused the
absence of records with the absence of activity.**

Three consequences for the notebook:

1. Bureaucratic complexity is what Tainter is measuring. It is also the
   easiest thing to detect archaeologically, which risks circularity.
2. Literacy loss is *reversible* and cultural continuity often survives it.
3. **Our evidence for collapse is disproportionately the failure of the very
   system that generates evidence.** This is the central methodological hazard
   of the field.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Agriculture → surplus → cities → civilisation" | Sedentism came first, monuments came before agriculture at Göbekli Tepe, and ~4,000 years separate farming from states |
| "The Agricultural Revolution" | Domestication took millennia and happened independently in at least 7–11 world regions. "Revolution" is Childe's coinage and misleads on tempo |
| "Early farmers were healthier" | On stature, teeth and dietary diversity, the evidence points the other way — with the osteological paradox as a caveat |
| "Civilisation requires writing" | The Inca ran a continental empire without phonetic writing |
| "Irrigation created despotism" | Wittfogel's hydraulic hypothesis is rejected. Large systems generally postdate the states that get credit for them |
| "Cities are where civilisation happens" | The Old Kingdom, the Inca and Harappa complicate this in three different ways |
| "Hunter-gatherers lived in constant scarcity" | Sahlins's "original affluent society" overstates it, but the Hobbesian version is worse. Foragers in good habitats had substantial leisure |
| "The Indus script will be deciphered soon" | It has resisted for a century, corpus texts are extremely short (average ~5 signs), and some researchers argue it may not encode language at all |
| "Göbekli Tepe was built by a lost civilisation" | It was built by hunter-gatherers, which is far more interesting, and the "lost civilisation" reading is a fringe claim with no support from the excavators |

## Open questions

- [ ] What *did* cause the transition from farming villages to states, given
      that agriculture alone plainly didn't do it for four millennia?
- [ ] Does the osteological paradox substantially weaken the Neolithic
      health-decline finding, or only qualify it? The field has not converged.
- [ ] How much early-state formation was voluntary aggregation vs. coercive
      capture? Scott says mostly coercive; the evidence base is thin either way
- [ ] Were the Tripolye mega-sites really non-hierarchical, or is our failure
      to find elites a preservation artefact?
- [ ] Is there any principled definition of "state" that the archaeology of
      Harappa satisfies or fails? (Currently the case is argued both ways from
      the same evidence.)
- [ ] Can we date the loss of literacy in post-palatial Greece more precisely,
      and does anything survive in between?
- [ ] How much does the "grain states" thesis generalise beyond Eurasia? Maize
      is a grain; manioc is not. Do the Andean and Amazonian cases fit?

# Cases: Maya, Rapa Nui, Late Bronze Age

> **Status: mixed, and that is the point of putting them together.** The Maya
> story is popularly wrong about *what collapsed*. The Rapa Nui story is
> probably wrong about *why*. The Late Bronze Age story is roughly right and is
> the strongest evidence in the record for interconnection fragility.

**Written from general knowledge without live source verification.** The
radiocarbon dates, population figures and ancient-DNA findings below are the
highest-priority items to check — especially the Rapa Nui genomic results.

---

# 1. The Maya — a decline, a relocation, and a living people

## What actually happened

Between roughly **750 and 900 CE**, the great cities of the **southern Maya
lowlands** — Tikal, Calakmul, Palenque, Copán, Caracol, Dos Pilas — stopped
erecting dated stone monuments, stopped building, and were progressively
depopulated. This is the "Terminal Classic collapse."

**What the evidence actually consists of:** the Long Count monument record. Maya
kings dedicated carved stelae with precise dates recording their accessions,
victories and rituals. Those dates thin out and then stop, city by city, over
about 150 years. The last known Long Count monument date is commonly given as
909 CE (Toniná).

**Note carefully what that index measures.** Stela dedication is an act of
**divine kingship** — the *k'uhul ajaw* institution. Its cessation is direct
evidence that a specific political-religious institution failed. It is *indirect*
evidence about population, and it is *no* evidence at all about culture,
language or descent.

## What did not happen

- **The Maya did not disappear.** Roughly six to seven million Maya people are
  alive today across Guatemala, Mexico, Belize and Honduras, speaking around
  thirty Mayan languages.
- **The northern lowlands flourished afterwards.** Chichén Itzá reached its
  height in the Terminal Classic and Early Postclassic, roughly when the south
  was emptying. Uxmal and the Puuc sites likewise. Mayapán dominated the north
  into the 15th century. **The population moved north and to the coasts; the
  region did not go dark.**
- **Maya states survived to meet the Spanish.** The conquest of the Yucatán took
  decades. The independent Itza kingdom at **Nojpetén (Tayasal), on Lake Petén
  Itzá, was not taken until 1697** — 175 years after Cortés landed in Mexico.
- **Books survived until they were burned.** Bishop Diego de Landa's *auto-da-fé*
  at Maní in 1562 destroyed Maya codices deliberately. **Some of what we call
  "lost Maya knowledge" was not lost. It was destroyed, recently, by an
  identifiable person.**

## Causes — multi-causal, drought weighted heavily

| Factor | Evidence | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| **Drought** | Lake Chichancanab sediment cores (Hodell and colleagues) showing oxygen-isotope aridity signals; the Yok Balum cave speleothem in Belize giving high-resolution rainfall, indicating multi-decadal drying phases c. 820–870 and later | Strong. Best-supported single factor |
| **Endemic warfare** | The hieroglyphic record itself documents escalating conflict, notably the long Tikal–Calakmul rivalry and the destruction of sites like Dos Pilas; defensive works appear late | Strong |
| **Environmental degradation** | Deforestation, partly to fuel lime kilns for the plaster that coated Maya buildings; soil erosion in the Petén | Moderate |
| **Overpopulation relative to a fragile karst landscape** | LiDAR surveys (notably the PACUNAM project's 2018 results, mapping tens of thousands of structures under the Guatemalan canopy) forced substantial *upward* revision of Classic population estimates | Moderate-to-strong, and recent |
| **Elite ideological failure** | Kings claimed to guarantee rain and agricultural fertility. Repeated drought is a direct falsification of the central legitimacy claim | Underrated. See mechanisms.md #4 |
| **Trade route shifts** | Rise of coastal circum-peninsular trade favouring northern and coastal sites | Moderate |

**Arthur Demarest and others have argued forcefully against monocausal drought
accounts**, noting that Maya sites failed at different times, that some
well-watered sites failed while drier ones persisted, and that the political
dynamics of an unstable system of competing divine kingships are sufficient to
explain a lot. The current mainstream position is a conjunction: **a fragile
political system of competing city-states, at high population density on thin
soils, hit by repeated multi-decadal drought, with an ideology that made the
drought the kings' fault.**

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| "The Maya vanished" | 6–7 million living Maya, ~30 languages. This is the most consequential popular error in the entire notebook |
| "It was one collapse" | Sites failed over ~150 years in no single pattern. The north grew while the south emptied |
| "They destroyed their environment and died" | Environmental degradation is real; the north's simultaneous flourishing is not explained by it |
| "The Maya predicted the world would end in 2012" | The 13th *b'ak'tun* ended in December 2012. This is a calendar odometer rolling over. Maya inscriptions reference dates far beyond it |
| "It's a mystery" | It is a well-studied multi-causal decline. "Mystery" sells documentaries |
| "The Spanish conquered them easily" | Nojpetén held out until 1697 |

# 2. Rapa Nui (Easter Island) — the ecocide story is probably wrong

## The standard narrative

Popularised by Jared Diamond in *Collapse* (2005), and by Clive Ponting before
him. Polynesians arrive c. 400–800 CE, population grows to perhaps 15,000, they
fell the island's palm forest to transport and erect nearly 900 *moai*, the
loss of trees eliminates canoes and hence deep-sea fishing, agriculture fails,
society descends into warfare and reportedly cannibalism, statues are toppled,
and by European contact the population is a fraction of its peak. **Presented as
a self-contained parable of ecological suicide — "Easter Island as Earth."**

## The revisionist case (Terry Hunt and Carl Lipo, and others)

| Claim | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Settlement was much later — c. 1200 CE, not 400–800** | Systematic re-dating of the earliest reliable radiocarbon samples. This compresses the whole timeline and leaves far less time for a slow Malthusian build-up |
| **Rats, not people, killed the palms** | The Polynesian rat (*Rattus exulans*) arrived with the settlers. Excavated *Jubaea* palm nut shells show rodent gnaw marks. Rats eat the seeds; a forest that cannot regenerate dies out even without heavy felling |
| **Islanders adapted successfully to deforestation** | Extensive **lithic mulching / rock gardening** — deliberately strewing and stacking stones across fields to retain moisture, moderate temperature and release minerals. This is a sophisticated agronomic adaptation, not a society failing |
| **The statues did not require rolling logs** | Hunt and Lipo argue the *moai* were moved upright by rocking with ropes — matching oral tradition that they "walked" — and demonstrated it experimentally in 2012 with a small team and a replica |
| **The "weapons" were not weapons** | *Mata'a*, the obsidian tools long read as spearheads, are morphologically inconsistent with weapons and better interpreted as general cutting and plant-processing tools |
| **The population crash is post-contact** | Roggeveen arrived in 1722. Then: introduced disease; the **1862–63 Peruvian slave raids**, which took on the order of 1,500 people; the return of a handful of survivors carrying smallpox; and continued epidemics. **The recorded population reached 111 people in 1877.** That is the collapse |
| **Ancient DNA shows no pre-contact bottleneck** | Genomic work published in 2024 (Moreno-Mayar and colleagues, in *Nature*) reported no signal of a severe pre-European population crash, and also evidence of Native American admixture dated to roughly 1250–1430 — implying contact across the eastern Pacific |

## Steelmanning Diamond's side

This should not be presented as a rout. The honest position:

- **The deforestation is not in dispute.** The island did have a palm forest and
  does not now. Whether rats or humans were the principal agent is a question of
  weighting, and humans burned and cleared land regardless.
- Toppled statues are real, and oral tradition does record inter-group conflict.
  The revisionists argue the toppling was largely post-contact and connected to
  the collapse of the *moai* cult, but the chronology is not airtight.
- Population estimates for the pre-contact peak remain uncertain; some estimates
  are far below the 15,000 figure, which cuts both ways.
- Radiocarbon re-dating depends on sample selection criteria that are
  themselves methodological choices, and not everyone accepts the ~1200 CE date.
- **Diamond's general point — that isolated societies can degrade their
  resource base — survives even if this specific case does not support it.**

## Where this lands

**Rapa Nui is the clearest case in modern popular science of a compelling
parable outrunning its evidence.** The story was attractive because it was a
self-contained morality tale with a visible island for a planet. The revised
account is messier: a small society that deforested its island partly by
accident, adapted competently for centuries, and was then destroyed by
outsiders — disease, slavers and annexation.

**Note who the revised story implicates.** That is not a reason to believe it,
but it is a reason to notice why the other version was easier to tell.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| "They cut down all the trees to move statues" | Rat predation on palm seeds is a strong competing explanation; the statues may not have required logs at all |
| "Ecocide killed the population" | The documented crash follows European contact, disease and the 1862–63 Peruvian slave raids |
| "Easter Island is a model for Earth" | It is a model for a small isolated society with a specific invasive rodent and a specific colonial history |
| "They were down to a few hundred when Europeans arrived" | Roggeveen in 1722 described a functioning society. The fall to 111 people came 150 years later |
| "Mata'a were spearheads" | Better interpreted as multipurpose cutting tools |

# 3. The Late Bronze Age collapse, c. 1200–1150 BC — the real thing

> **This is the case where the dramatic version is closest to correct.** Eric
> Cline's *1177 B.C.: The Year Civilization Collapsed* (2014) is the standard
> accessible treatment, and its subtitle overstates a case its contents
> carefully qualify.

## What collapsed

Within roughly fifty years, across the eastern Mediterranean:

| Polity | Fate |
|---|---|
| **Hittite Empire** | The capital **Hattusa** was abandoned and burned around 1190 BC. The empire ends. Only small "Neo-Hittite" successor states in northern Syria survive |
| **Mycenaean Greece** | The palaces at Pylos, Mycenae, Tiryns and Thebes are destroyed or abandoned. **Linear B writing disappears entirely for roughly four centuries** |
| **Ugarit** (northern Syria) | Destroyed c. 1190–1185 BC and never reoccupied. Its final tablets, some found in a kiln where they were being fired when the city fell, include a letter to Cyprus: the enemy's ships have arrived, the city's own ships and troops are away, and the country is left undefended |
| **Cyprus, the Levantine coast** | Widespread destruction layers at Enkomi, Hazor, Megiddo, Lachish, Ashkelon and others |
| **Kassite Babylonia** | Weakened; falls to Elam c. 1155 BC |
| **Egypt** | **Survives, diminished.** Ramesses III repels the "Sea Peoples" in his eighth year (conventionally 1177 BC, per the Medinet Habu inscriptions), but the New Kingdom's power and reach decline sharply thereafter |
| **Assyria** | **Survives.** A key control case: an inland, agrarian, less trade-dependent polity comes through |

## The interconnection that made it fragile

**Bronze requires tin, and there was almost no tin in the eastern
Mediterranean.** It came overland from Central Asia — Afghanistan and beyond —
possibly also from Cornwall. Copper came largely from Cyprus. Every palace army
in the region depended on a supply chain thousands of kilometres long that no
single polity controlled.

**The Uluburun shipwreck** (off southern Turkey, sunk c. 1300 BC) is the best
single artefact of this world: roughly ten tonnes of copper ingots, about a
tonne of tin in the correct alloy proportion, Canaanite jars, Baltic amber,
African ebony, ivory, Mycenaean and Cypriot pottery, and Egyptian goods — a
single hull carrying material from at least seven or eight cultures.

**The Amarna letters** (Egypt, 14th century BC) show the diplomatic layer: kings
of Egypt, Babylon, Mitanni, Hatti and Assyria addressing each other as
"brother," exchanging gold, gifts, princesses, and complaints about the quality
of gifts. **This was an integrated international system with a shared elite
culture, and it went down together.**

## Causes — genuinely multi-causal

| Proposed cause | Evidence | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **Drought / climate** | Pollen cores from Cyprus (Larnaca Salt Lake) and the Levant indicating a dry phase; Dead Sea level records; Hittite texts requesting grain shipments from Egypt | Strong contributory |
| **Earthquakes** | An "earthquake storm" hypothesis (Amos Nur, with Cline): a sequence of seismic events along a connected fault system over decades, consistent with destruction layers at multiple sites | Plausible; earthquakes rarely end civilisations by themselves |
| **"Sea Peoples"** | Egyptian inscriptions naming groups (Peleset, Shekelesh, Denyen, Weshesh and others) | **Real but over-read.** "Sea Peoples" is a 19th-century collective coinage, and the sources are triumphal propaganda from the one polity that survived. Migration is at least as plausibly a *symptom* of collapse elsewhere as its cause |
| **Internal rebellion** | Palace-redistributive economies concentrate everything in one burnable building | Plausible; hard to distinguish archaeologically from external sack |
| **Systems collapse** | The interdependence documented above | **The best organising frame.** No single cause needs to be sufficient if the network amplifies |
| **Iron replacing bronze** | Iron becomes dominant afterwards | **Consequence, not cause.** Iron ore is common and local; societies turned to it *because* the tin trade failed |

**Cline's own conclusion is the right one: no single cause is sufficient, and a
"perfect storm" of drought, seismic activity, internal unrest, migration and
trade disruption hit a system with no redundancy.**

## What makes this case unusually strong evidence

1. **Simultaneity across independent polities.** Multiple states with different
   internal politics failed within decades of each other. That pattern demands a
   system-level explanation in a way single-polity collapses do not.
2. **Genuine loss of a technology of record.** Linear B literacy vanished for
   ~400 years. When alphabetic Greek appears in the 8th century BC it is a
   different system borrowed from Phoenician, not a revival.
3. **Control cases inside the same event.** Egypt survived weakened; Assyria
   survived; Phoenician cities like Tyre and Byblos did *well* in the aftermath
   and went on to dominate Mediterranean trade. **The variation is the data.**

## The "Dark Age" caveat

The subsequent Greek "Dark Age" (c. 1100–800 BC) is less dark than the name.
**Lefkandi on Euboea**, especially the Toumba building of around 1000–950 BC
with its rich burials and imported goods, shows continuing elite wealth,
long-distance contact and monumental construction. Once again: **absence of
writing was read as absence of activity.**

## Cross-case comparison

| | Maya | Rapa Nui | Late Bronze Age |
|---|---|---|---|
| Elite institutions failed | **Yes** | Partly (moai cult ended) | **Yes, comprehensively** |
| Population fell substantially | Yes, in the south; moved north | **Only after European contact** | Yes, in the Aegean and Anatolia |
| Culture/people survive | **Yes, 6–7m today** | **Yes, Rapa Nui people today** | Language yes, script no, states no |
| Environmental factor | Drought: strong | Deforestation: real, causally disputed | Drought: contributory |
| Popular story accuracy | Badly wrong on "vanished" | **Probably wrong on cause** | Roughly right, overdramatised on the Sea Peoples |
| Best-supported cause | Multi-causal, drought-weighted | Colonial contact, disease, slaving | Systems failure under multiple shocks |

## Open questions

- [ ] Does the 2024 Rapa Nui aDNA result hold up, and how have Diamond's
      defenders responded? **This is the single highest-priority verification
      item in this notebook**
- [ ] What was the actual Classic Maya lowland population? LiDAR revised it
      upward substantially; how much of that revision is structure counts
      versus occupancy assumptions?
- [ ] Can the Maya site-abandonment sequence be correlated site-by-site with the
      Yok Balum rainfall record at annual resolution?
- [ ] How much Late Bronze Age destruction is seismic versus human? Can the two
      be distinguished reliably in the destruction layers?
- [ ] Who were the Sea Peoples? Multiple identifications (Sherden/Sardinia,
      Peleset/Philistines) are proposed and none is secure
- [ ] Why did Assyria survive when Hatti did not? The comparison is the most
      informative available and is under-exploited
- [ ] Was Linear B literacy ever more than a palace scribal specialism? If it
      was only ever ~100 people, its loss means something quite different

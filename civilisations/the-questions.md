# The Questions — Disaggregated

> **Status: Tier 1 as a methodological claim.** That "why do civilisations
> collapse?" is several questions wearing one costume is not seriously
> disputed by anyone who works in the field. The tiers below apply to the
> sub-questions, not to the disaggregation itself.

**Written from general knowledge without live source verification.** Names and
dates should be checked.

---

## The costume

"Why do civilisations collapse?" is asked as if it were one question with one
answer. It is at least fourteen questions, drawn from at least seven
disciplines, with wildly different evidential situations. Some are Tier 1
answerable. Some are definitional and cannot be answered until you decide what
you meant. At least two are Tier 4 — not hard, but malformed.

**The single highest-value move in this whole topic is to ask "collapse of
what, for whom, measured how?" before accepting any answer.**

---

## The fourteen

| # | Question | Discipline | Tier | Short answer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | What counts as a collapse? | Definitional | **T4 as asked** | No fact settles it. Pick a definition, then it becomes tractable |
| 2 | Did the population fall? | Archaeology, demography | **T2** | Sometimes sharply, sometimes not at all. Separate evidence required |
| 3 | Did political authority fragment? | History | **T1 per case** | Usually the best-documented thing |
| 4 | Did material living standards fall? | Archaeology, bioarchaeology | **T2** | Skeletal and material evidence; the answer varies and sometimes standards *rose* |
| 5 | Did the culture disappear? | Anthropology, linguistics | **T1 per case** | Usually not. Cultural continuity survives political collapse routinely |
| 6 | Was it fast or slow? | History | **T2** | Slower than the popular story almost every time, faster than the revisionist story sometimes |
| 7 | Was it one cause or many? | All | **T2** | Multi-causal in every well-documented case. Monocausal accounts are a genre, not a finding |
| 8 | Was there a trigger vs. a vulnerability? | Systems | **T2** | The most useful distinction in the field. Triggers are cheap; vulnerabilities are the explanation |
| 9 | Could it have been prevented? | Counterfactual | **T3** | Rarely testable. Beware anyone confident here |
| 10 | Collapse for whom? | Social history | **T2** | Elites, always. Peasants, sometimes not at all, sometimes better off |
| 11 | Why do some societies last so long? | Institutional | **T2, badly under-studied** | See what-makes-them-last.md. The literature is ~10:1 skewed toward failure |
| 12 | Do collapses follow a pattern? | Cliodynamics | **T2/T3** | Structural-demographic cycles are the strongest candidate and are contested |
| 13 | Does any of this transfer to the present? | Applied | **T2/T3** | Some mechanisms yes, most analogies no. See applications.md |
| 14 | Is "civilisation" a natural kind at all? | Philosophy of social science | **T4-ish** | Probably not. It's a family-resemblance term doing ranking work it shouldn't |

## Question 1 in detail — what counts as collapse?

Because everything downstream depends on it.

**Tainter's definition** (1988) is the standard one and is deliberately narrow:
collapse is **a rapid, significant loss of an established level of
sociopolitical complexity.** Complexity means differentiation (number of
distinct social roles) plus integration (amount of specialist coordination
needed to run the thing). Note what this does *not* say: nothing about death,
nothing about decline in wellbeing, nothing about culture, nothing about moral
failure. On Tainter's definition, **collapse is a simplification, and
simplification can be an improvement for the simplified.**

**Competing definitions in circulation:**

| Definition | Used by | What it catches | What it misses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loss of sociopolitical complexity | Tainter | Rome (West), Maya lowlands, Mycenae | Cases where complexity persists but the state changes hands |
| Population decline > X% | Demographic archaeology | Rapa Nui post-contact, plague events | Political fragmentation without depopulation |
| End of a state or dynasty | Political history | Every dynasty ever | Makes "collapse" trivially frequent and boring |
| Loss of a cultural tradition | Anthropology | Almost nothing; traditions are sticky | Everything |
| Systems failure / loss of function | Systems science, Holling | Late Bronze Age network failure | Slow decline without a cascade |
| Failure of a society to reproduce its conditions | Marxist / world-systems | Structural transitions | Very hard to operationalise |

**The trap:** authors switch between these mid-argument. A book will establish
collapse in Tainter's sense (complexity fell — well evidenced), then draw
conclusions that only follow in the population sense (everyone died — not
evidenced), then moralise in the fourth sense (they destroyed their culture —
usually false). **Watch for the switch. It is the single most common error in
popular collapse writing.**

## Decline vs. transformation vs. fragmentation

Three different things, routinely merged.

- **Decline** — the same system, doing less. Fewer people, less trade, smaller
  buildings, worse pottery. Post-Roman Britain is the extreme case: by the mid
  5th century, wheel-thrown pottery, coinage and mortared stone construction
  effectively vanish from the archaeological record for generations.
- **Transformation** — different system, comparable complexity. The eastern
  Mediterranean under Islam after the 630s–650s was not less complex than under
  Rome and Persia. It was differently organised. Calling that "collapse"
  smuggles in a value judgment.
- **Fragmentation** — same complexity, distributed differently. Post-Carolingian
  Europe, Warring States China, the *taifa* kingdoms after the Caliphate of
  Córdoba fell in 1031. Fragmented polities can be *more* dynamic:
  competitive fragmentation is one standard explanation for European
  divergence (Jones's *The European Miracle*, and the "political fragmentation"
  strand in Mokyr and Scheidel).

**The honest answer is that the boundaries between these three are not sharp
and no procedure exists to place a given case cleanly.** They are useful
categories, not natural ones.

## "Collapse" is a category imposed by historians

This is the load-bearing point of this file.

**No participant in any of these episodes woke up and thought "the collapse
began today."** The category is retrospective, applied by people with access to
an end-state that participants did not have.

Consider what a well-off farmer in the Auvergne experienced between 400 and 550
CE: tax collectors got less regular and then stopped; the local bishop became
the person you took disputes to; the coins got worse and then you mostly stopped
using them; the villa's mosaic floor was not repaired; a Gothic or Frankish
lord's name appeared on documents instead of a distant emperor's. Each of those
was a change. None of them was "the fall of the Roman Empire," which is a
concept invented later by people writing books.

Three consequences:

1. **The unit of analysis is chosen by the analyst.** "Rome" is a
   choice. Choose "the Mediterranean economy," "Latin Christendom," "the
   Italian peninsula" or "the Roman state's tax system" and you get four
   different chronologies with four different endpoints.
2. **Periodisation creates the phenomenon.** Once you name a period "the Dark
   Ages," evidence of continuity within it reads as anomaly rather than as
   refutation. The renaming to "Late Antiquity" (Peter Brown, from the 1970s)
   changed what researchers noticed — which is a warning about how much the
   framing does.
3. **The moral frame comes free with the word.** "Collapse" implies failure,
   failure implies blame, blame implies a lesson. Diamond's subtitle — *How
   Societies Choose to Fail or Succeed* — makes this explicit, and it is the
   thing his critics object to most.

**Counter-argument, steelmanned:** this can be taken too far. The
constructivist point does not make material change unreal. Ward-Perkins's
response to the transformation school is essentially: *the pottery got
measurably worse, people's houses got smaller, and literacy fell — you can call
that a transformation if you like, but the people living in it experienced a
sharp fall in material quality of life and it is condescending to redescribe
that as a change of style.* He is right about the West between roughly 400 and
600. **The category is imposed; the potsherds are not.**

## Which discipline owns which question

| Question type | Owned by | Typical evidence | Characteristic failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Did the state end? | Political history | Texts, king lists, legal documents | Over-reliance on elite narrative sources |
| Did people leave / die? | Settlement archaeology, palaeodemography | Survey, house counts, LiDAR, cemeteries | Occupancy assumptions; the osteological paradox |
| Did living standards change? | Bioarchaeology | Stature, dental enamel, isotopes | Skeletal samples are non-random |
| Was there a climate shock? | Palaeoclimatology | Ice cores, speleothems, tree rings, lake sediments | Correlation with dated events; dating error bars |
| Was there a plague? | Palaeogenomics | Ancient DNA from teeth | Presence ≠ demographic impact |
| Why did the system fail? | Systems science, economics | Models | Unfalsifiability |
| Is there a cycle? | Cliodynamics | Time series | Overfitting, cherry-picked series |
| What does it mean? | Nobody. It's a choice | — | Pretending it's a finding |

## The two Tier 4s

**"Was the collapse inevitable?"** — Counterfactual history has no procedure for
settling itself. You cannot rerun the Roman Empire. Statements like "Rome was
doomed by the third century" are unfalsifiable in principle; what *can* be
argued is that specific vulnerabilities existed, which is a different and
weaker claim. Beware the slide from "vulnerable" to "doomed" — it happens
constantly and it is where most collapse writing goes bad.

**"Are we next?"** — Not because the future is unknowable in some deep sense,
but because the question has no defined referent ("we"?), no defined predicate
("collapse" in which of six senses?), and no base rate (n≈30 poorly-measured
cases across 5,000 years with no independence between them, since later
societies learned from earlier ones). See applications.md for the more
answerable reformulations.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Collapse means everyone died" | Almost never. It usually means the tax and monument system stopped |
| "Civilisations rise and fall like organisms" | Spengler's biological metaphor. Rejected as method — societies have no life cycle, no gestation, no fixed lifespan |
| "There's a standard lifespan for empires, about 250 years" | Sir John Glubb's essay, endlessly recirculated. It is not a study, it has no method, the sample is picked to fit, and "empire" is defined ad hoc for each case |
| "Historians know why Rome fell" | There are 200+ published hypotheses. The field's honest position is multi-causal with contested weightings |
| "Collapse is always bad" | For elites, yes. For the median person, sometimes the opposite. See the-control-group.md |
| "Collapse is sudden" | The Roman West took ~250 years. The Maya Terminal Classic took ~150. The genuinely fast cases (Late Bronze Age, USSR) are the minority |
| "Ancient people didn't see it coming" | Some did. Roman writers complained about decline continuously for 600 years — which is exactly why their complaints are weak evidence |
| "We're the first civilisation that could collapse globally" | Possibly true, but note this is asserted by every generation and the specific mechanism matters more than the vibe |

## Open questions

- [ ] Is there any operationalisation of "collapse" that most researchers would
      accept, or has the field permanently fragmented into incommensurable
      definitions?
- [ ] Can we distinguish, from material evidence alone, elite institutional
      failure from population decline in cases lacking texts?
- [ ] What is the actual base rate of collapse-with-depopulation vs.
      collapse-without? Nobody appears to have counted systematically.
- [ ] Does the framing shift from "Dark Ages" to "Late Antiquity" reflect new
      evidence, or a change in scholarly taste? (Both, but in what proportion?)
- [ ] Is competitive fragmentation systematically better for innovation than
      unified empire, or is that a post-hoc European self-flattery?
- [ ] How much of the "n≈30 cases" problem can be mitigated by treating regions
      rather than polities as units?
- [ ] Which of the fourteen questions above would move most with better data,
      and which are stuck for principled reasons?

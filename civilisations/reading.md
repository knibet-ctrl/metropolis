# Reading

## Start here — three books

| Work | Why |
|---|---|
| **Joseph Tainter, *The Collapse of Complex Societies* (1988)** | The most rigorous single framework. Short, dense, argues rather than narrates. Even if you reject the thesis, the structure of the argument is the field's spine |
| **Eric Cline, *1177 B.C.: The Year Civilization Collapsed* (2014)** | The best accessible treatment of the strongest case, and honest about the limits of what can be known. The subtitle overstates what the book carefully qualifies |
| **Ibn Khaldun, *The Muqaddimah* (1377)** | Franz Rosenthal's translation, or the Dawood abridgement. The most under-read foundational text in the field |

## General theory

| Work | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Joseph Tainter, *The Collapse of Complex Societies* (1988)** | Declining marginal returns on complexity. Collapse as economising, not failure |
| **Jared Diamond, *Collapse* (2005)** | The most influential popular framework. **Read with *Questioning Collapse* alongside** — see correctives |
| **Peter Turchin, *Historical Dynamics* (2003); *Secular Cycles* with Sergey Nefedov (2009); *Ages of Discord* (2016); *End Times* (2023)** | Structural-demographic theory. The only framework here making ex-ante quantitative predictions. Start with *Ages of Discord* if you want the mechanism, *End Times* if you want the popular version |
| **Jack Goldstone, *Revolution and Rebellion in the Early Modern World* (1991)** | The origin of the structural-demographic model. Prior to and arguably better argued than Turchin's popularisations |
| **Ibn Khaldun, *The Muqaddimah* (1377)** | *Asabiyyah*, cyclical dynastic decay, and a fiscal model anticipating the Laffer curve by six centuries |
| **Arnold Toynbee, *A Study of History* (1934–61)** | Challenge-and-response, creative minorities. Methodologically abandoned; historically important; the "dominant minority" concept prefigures elite overproduction |
| **Oswald Spengler, *The Decline of the West* (1918, 1922)** | Organicist morphology of cultures. Read as an artefact of its moment, not as method |
| **Thomas Homer-Dixon, *The Upside of Down* (2006)** | The "ingenuity gap" — societies must generate solutions faster than complexity generates problems. Underrated |
| **Ian Morris, *Why the West Rules — For Now* (2010)** | Energy capture as a quantitative index of social development. The index is contested; the attempt is valuable |
| **Charles Perrow, *Normal Accidents* (1984)** | Not about civilisations, and the best available source on why tightly coupled complex systems fail |
| **Lance Gunderson and C.S. Holling, *Panarchy* (2002)** | Adaptive cycles and resilience from ecology. The source of most "resilience" vocabulary in the field |

## Rome

| Work | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Edward Gibbon, *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire* (1776–89)** | Superseded and still worth reading — for the prose, and to see how a field's founding frame gets set |
| **Peter Heather, *The Fall of the Roman Empire* (2005)** | The exogenous-shock case: Hunnic pressure pushed intact barbarian groups across the frontier faster than Rome could absorb them. A functioning empire, destroyed from outside |
| **Bryan Ward-Perkins, *The Fall of Rome and the End of Civilization* (2005)** | The material-catastrophe case, from pottery, tile, coinage and cattle bones. **The essential corrective to the softest transformation arguments** |
| **Peter Brown, *The World of Late Antiquity* (1971)** | Created the field of Late Antiquity. The transformation case at its most persuasive |
| **Chris Wickham, *Framing the Early Middle Ages* (2005)** | The most careful synthesis. Regionally differentiated; the deep change is tax-state to land-aristocracy. Long and worth it |
| **Walter Goffart, *Barbarians and Romans* (1980)** | The accommodation thesis: settlement by tax reallocation rather than conquest. Contested and important |
| **Guy Halsall, *Barbarian Migrations and the Roman West* (2007)** | Argues the migrations were substantially a *consequence* of the western empire's weakening, not the cause. Inverts the standard order |
| **Kyle Harper, *The Fate of Rome* (2017)** | Climate and disease as the organising frame. **Ambitious, influential, and criticised for environmental over-attribution** |
| **Adrian Goldsworthy, *How Rome Fell* (2009)** | The internal-political case: civil war and the succession problem consumed the capacity to face outward |
| **A.H.M. Jones, *The Later Roman Empire 284–602* (1964)** | The administrative and fiscal foundation everything since is built on. A reference work, not a read |

## Maya and Mesoamerica

| Work | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Michael Coe and Stephen Houston, *The Maya*** | The standard general introduction, revised across many editions |
| **David Webster, *The Fall of the Ancient Maya* (2002)** | A careful, sceptical treatment of collapse explanations by an excavator |
| **Arthur Demarest, *Ancient Maya* (2004)** | The case against monocausal drought accounts, from someone who dug the sites |
| **Michael Coe, *Breaking the Maya Code* (1992)** | The decipherment story. Explains why the Maya went from "peaceful astronomers" to a documented history of dynasties and warfare within a generation of scholarship — **a case study in how much a single methodological breakthrough can rewrite a field** |
| **Diego de Landa, *Relación de las cosas de Yucatán* (c. 1566)** | Primary source, by the man who burned the codices. Read for what it is |

## Late Bronze Age

| Work | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Eric Cline, *1177 B.C.* (2014), and *After 1177 B.C.* (2024)** | The accessible standard, and the sequel on what came next — which is the more interesting question |
| **Robert Drews, *The End of the Bronze Age* (1993)** | Argues for a military-technological explanation: changes in infantry tactics undermining chariot-based armies. A serious minority position |
| **The Ugarit archive tablets** | Primary. The final letters, including the appeal describing enemy ships arriving while the city's own forces were away, are as immediate as ancient evidence gets |
| **The Amarna letters** | Primary. The 14th-century BC diplomatic correspondence showing an integrated international elite system |
| **Medinet Habu inscriptions of Ramesses III** | Primary, and **propaganda from the one polity that survived.** Read as a source about Egyptian self-presentation |

## Rapa Nui

| Work | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Terry Hunt and Carl Lipo, *The Statues That Walked* (2011)** | The revisionist case: late settlement, rat predation, lithic mulching, upright statue transport, post-contact collapse |
| **Jared Diamond, *Collapse* (2005), chapter on Easter Island** | The ecocide case. **Read it, then read Hunt and Lipo, in that order.** The pair is the best available lesson in how a compelling parable can outrun its evidence |
| **Moreno-Mayar et al., ancient-DNA study (*Nature*, 2024)** | Reported no genomic signal of a pre-European population bottleneck. **Verify this citation before relying on it — it is the single most load-bearing recent finding in this notebook** |

## Modern collapses

| Work | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Stephen Kotkin, *Armageddon Averted* (2001)** | The elite-defection account of the Soviet collapse, and the argument that it was astonishing chiefly for how little violence it produced |
| **Alexei Yurchak, *Everything Was Forever, Until It Was No More* (2005)** | The ethnography of late-Soviet ideological form. Explains how the collapse could be simultaneously unexpected and unsurprising. **The most conceptually original book in this section** |
| **Yegor Gaidar, *Collapse of an Empire* (2007)** | The oil-and-grain fiscal account, by the man who then ran the Russian economy. A participant's argument, read accordingly |
| **Serhii Plokhy, *The Last Empire* (2014)** | The narrative of 1991 itself, month by month |
| **Susan Woodward, *Balkan Tragedy* (1995)** | The structural and economic account of Yugoslavia's dissolution, against the "ancient hatreds" reading |
| **V.P. Gagnon, *The Myth of Ethnic War* (2004)** | Elite manufacture of ethnic conflict as a demobilisation strategy. Direct evidence that collapse narratives get built in real time |
| **Robert Kaplan, *Balkan Ghosts* (1993)** | **Included as a negative example.** Influential on 1990s Western policy, and the "ancient hatreds" thesis is rejected by area specialists |

## Endurance and institutions — the under-read section

| Work | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (1990)** | The eight design principles for institutions that lasted centuries. **The most useful book in this entire list and the least read by people interested in collapse** |
| **Douglass North, *Institutions, Institutional Change and Economic Performance* (1990)** | Why institutions persist even when inefficient. The path-dependence argument |
| **Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson, *Why Nations Fail* (2012)** | Inclusive vs. extractive institutions. Sweeping, influential, and the historical cases — Venice especially — are contested |
| **Walter Scheidel, *Escape from Rome* (2019)** | Why Europe's post-Roman fragmentation may have been the condition for later divergence. The most interesting inversion of the "collapse is bad" frame |
| **John Haldon, *Byzantium in the Seventh Century* (1990)** | Deliberate simplification as a survival strategy. **The single best counterexample to the assumption that complexity loss is failure** |
| **Mark Elvin, *The Pattern of the Chinese Past* (1973)** | The "high-level equilibrium trap" — why China's sophistication may have blocked the transition Europe made. Contested and worth arguing with |
| **Frederic Lane, *Venice: A Maritime Republic* (1973)** | The institutional detail behind the thousand years |

## Environment, climate and disease

| Work | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Geoffrey Parker, *Global Crisis* (2013)** | The 17th-century general crisis as a climate-driven global event. Enormous, evidence-dense, and the maximal climate-causation case done properly |
| **Kyle Harper, *The Fate of Rome* (2017)** | See Rome section. The disease and climate strand at full strength |
| **William McNeill, *Plagues and Peoples* (1976)** | The founding text for disease in world history. Superseded in detail, still the right frame |
| **Charles Mann, *1491* (2005) and *1493* (2011)** | The pre-contact Americas and the Columbian exchange. Journalism, and unusually careful about the size of the disagreements it reports |
| **Alfred Crosby, *The Columbian Exchange* (1972); *Ecological Imperialism* (1986)** | The originals |

## The control group and the critique of the whole enterprise

| Work | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **James C. Scott, *Against the Grain* (2017)** | Early states as grain-appropriation machines; the periphery often better off. Short, sharp, arguable |
| **James C. Scott, *The Art of Not Being Governed* (2009)** | Zomia. State avoidance as an active strategy rather than a developmental failure |
| **James C. Scott, *Seeing Like a State* (1998)** | **Arguably the most useful of the three.** How states' need for legibility drives interventions that destroy the local knowledge they depend on. Applies directly to error-correction failure |
| **Pierre Clastres, *Society Against the State* (1974)** | Institutional defences against the emergence of coercive authority |
| **David Graeber and David Wengrow, *The Dawn of Everything* (2021)** | The most ambitious attempt to break the ladder model. Valuable corrective, weak monograph. **Read the archaeological reviews alongside it** |
| **Walter Scheidel, *The Great Leveler* (2017)** | Inequality has fallen historically only through catastrophe, including state collapse. Uncomfortable for everyone |
| **Prümers et al., Casarabe LiDAR study (*Nature*, 2022)** | Monumental low-density urbanism in the Bolivian Amazon. Emblematic of the systematic upward revision |

## Correctives — read these against the famous ones

**This section is the point of the file.** Several of the best-known works above
are substantially contested, and the contest is not visible from inside them.

| Corrective | Corrects |
|---|---|
| **Patricia McAnany and Norman Yoffee, eds., *Questioning Collapse* (2010)** | **Diamond's *Collapse*.** The organised archaeological response: case selection, underweighting of colonialism, and the charge that he describes as "collapsed" societies whose descendants are alive |
| **Terry Hunt and Carl Lipo, *The Statues That Walked*** | Diamond on Rapa Nui specifically |
| **Bryan Ward-Perkins** | Peter Brown and the softest versions of "transformation" |
| **Peter Brown and Chris Wickham** | Ward-Perkins's implication that the Mediterranean experience was uniform |
| **John Haldon and colleagues' critique of climate-determinist history** | Kyle Harper's *Fate of Rome* |
| **Victor Lieberman's critique of Zomia** | Scott's *The Art of Not Being Governed* |
| **Archaeological reviews of *The Dawn of Everything*** | Graeber and Wengrow's arguments from absence of evidence |
| **Wood, Milner, Harpending and Weiss, "The Osteological Paradox" (*Current Anthropology*, 1992)** | **Every skeletal-health argument in the field, in both directions.** If you cite bones, you have to have read this |
| **The Justinianic-plague minimalist literature** | The traditional catastrophic reading of 541 and after |
| **Critiques of the Seshat databank's coding reliability** | Turchin's quantitative claims |
| **Sir John Glubb, *The Fate of Empires* (1976)** | **Listed as an anti-recommendation.** The source of the "empires last 250 years" claim. No method, ad hoc definitions, sample fitted to the conclusion. It circulates constantly and should not |

## Where the popular story is wrong about the sources

| Popular belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Gibbon is the authority on Rome" | Magnificent prose, superseded scholarship, and a thesis refuted by the eastern empire's survival |
| "Diamond's *Collapse* is the standard account" | It is the standard *popular* account. The archaeological profession published an edited volume against it |
| "Reading one big book gets you the field" | Every major work in this list has a serious opposing work. The pairs are the content |
| "Older sources are outdated, newer ones supersede them" | Ibn Khaldun (1377) is better than most of what came after. Ostrom (1990) is unimproved. Age is not the variable |
| "Primary sources are more reliable" | The Medinet Habu inscriptions are propaganda. Roman moralists are moralists. Primary means closer, not truer |
| "Popular science books cite their evidence, so they're checkable" | Citation density is not the same as citation load-bearing. **Check whether the cited source supports the specific claim, which in the Rapa Nui case it frequently did not** |

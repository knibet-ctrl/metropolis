# Mechanisms

> **Status: Tier 2 for most entries, Tier 1 for a few.** These are the
> cross-cutting causal mechanisms that recur across cases. **None of them is
> sufficient on its own and none of them is present in every case.** Treat this
> as a checklist for interrogating a case, not as a theory.

**Written from general knowledge without live source verification.**

**A warning that applies to the whole file, borrowed from
`leaders/mechanism-matrix.md`: I defined these mechanisms and then found them
in the cases. That is circular. The genuinely informative content is where a
mechanism is conspicuously *absent* in a case it should fit.**

---

## The trigger/vulnerability distinction

Before the list. **Triggers are cheap and common. Vulnerabilities are the
explanation.**

Droughts, plagues, earthquakes, invasions and bad emperors happen constantly.
Most of the time nothing much follows. The question is never "what hit them" but
"why did this one propagate." Mechanisms 1–3 and 8 are mostly about
vulnerability. Mechanisms 6–7 are mostly triggers. Mechanisms 4, 9, 10 are about
why the response failed.

---

## 1. Energy return and surplus

**The claim:** every society runs on an energy surplus over subsistence, and
the size and reliability of that surplus caps everything else — how many
non-farmers, how much administration, how much monument-building.

**Mechanism:** when the energy return on energy invested (EROI) falls — soil
exhaustion, deforestation requiring longer fuel hauls, mines going deeper, the
best land already farmed — the surplus supporting the specialist class shrinks
while the specialist class does not.

**Cases:** Roman lead and silver mining reaching diminishing depths (visible as
falling lead deposition in Greenland ice cores); Maya deforestation for lime
plaster production; medieval English wood shortages driving the coal transition;
Venezuela as an EROI story in reverse — an economy that outsourced all
production to a single high-EROI resource and had nothing when it faltered.

**Weakness:** EROI is a modern industrial-energy concept applied retrospectively
to societies for which we cannot measure it. **The framing is illuminating and
the numbers are made up.** Ian Morris's "energy capture" index is the serious
attempt to quantify it and is contested for exactly this reason.

## 2. Complexity costs and declining marginal returns

**The claim (Tainter):** complexity solves problems, complexity costs energy,
and each additional increment buys less than the last. Eventually the
maintenance cost of existing complexity consumes the surplus that would fund
adaptation.

**Cases:** Diocletian's response to the third-century crisis — doubling the
provinces, expanding the army and bureaucracy, systematising taxation — solved
the crisis and permanently raised the state's running cost. The late Roman
bureaucracy is the archetype. Modern analogue: regulatory and compliance
accretion, where each rule is individually justified and the aggregate is an
unbudgeted tax on everything.

**The absence that matters: China re-complexified after every collapse.** If
complexity had genuinely reached negative returns, that should not have been
possible. See theories-of-collapse.md.

## 3. Elite overproduction and intra-elite competition

**The strongest single mechanism in the file.**

**The claim (Goldstone, Turchin; anticipated by Ibn Khaldun):** when the number
of people with elite credentials, expectations and resources grows faster than
the number of elite positions available, the surplus aspirants do not quietly
accept lower status. They compete harder, which raises the cost of competition,
which degrades the norms that made peaceful competition possible; and the
losers become **counter-elites** — well-educated, well-connected, resourced
people with a grievance and the skills to organise around it.

**Cases:**

| Case | Detail |
|---|---|
| Late Roman Republic | Senatorial families multiplying against a fixed number of consulships; the *cursus honorum* jammed; competition escalated to civil war |
| Late Roman Empire | Inflation of senatorial rank and titles until they meant little |
| Ming and Qing China | Degree-holders far exceeding available offices. Hong Xiuquan, who failed the imperial examinations four times, went on to lead the Taiping Rebellion — one of the deadliest conflicts in history |
| French Revolution | A large, educated, wealthy but politically excluded bourgeoisie |
| Ottoman Empire | *Timar* holders squeezed as the fiscal system changed; janissary corps expanding beyond its function |
| Modern | Overproduction of law graduates, doctoral holders and MBAs relative to the positions their credentials were sold as buying |

**Why it is powerful:** it is a *supply-and-demand* mechanism with countable
quantities, which makes it unusually testable for this field. **Why to be
careful:** the counts require historical series that are frequently
reconstructions, and the theory's predictions are often stated loosely enough
to be hard to score.

## 4. Legitimacy failure

**The claim:** states run on voluntary compliance far more than on coercion. No
state has enough enforcers to compel a genuinely non-compliant population. When
the story that makes compliance feel obligatory stops working, enforcement costs
rise faster than any state can pay.

**Cases:** Maya divine kings claimed responsibility for rain and were falsified
by repeated multi-decadal drought — **an ideology with an empirical hostage to
fortune built into it.** The Mandate of Heaven's genius by contrast is that it
is a *transferable* legitimacy: disaster proves the current dynasty has lost the
mandate, not that the mandate is fiction. Chernobyl in 1986 and its cover-up
damaged Soviet legitimacy with its own population more effectively than decades
of dissident writing. Ibn Khaldun's *asabiyyah* is legitimacy theory in a
different vocabulary.

**The diagnostic question: what would falsify this regime's central claim, and
what happens when it does?**

## 5. Debt, debasement and fiscal capacity

**The claim:** states that cannot tax must borrow, print or debase. All three
buy time at a rising price, and the last one destroys the medium in which
economic coordination happens.

| Case | Detail |
|---|---|
| Rome | Denarius from ~90%+ silver in the early empire to an effectively bronze, silver-washed coin by the 260s. Diocletian's Edict on Maximum Prices (301) tried to fix the symptom and produced shortages |
| Ottoman Empire | Repeated *akçe* debasements; eventual default in 1875 and the foreign-controlled Public Debt Administration |
| Revolutionary France | *Assignats* hyperinflated to worthlessness by 1796 |
| Weimar Germany | 1923 hyperinflation; **note it was stopped within months** by the Rentenmark — monetary collapse is often recoverable when the state's underlying capacity survives |
| Venezuela | Deficit monetisation with price and exchange controls |

**The sharper version: fiscal capacity is the best single predictor of state
survival.** A state that can assess and collect taxes can do everything else. A
state that cannot is a state that will soon have no army. Rome's loss of North
Africa in 439 is the cleanest instance in the notebook.

## 6. Climate shocks

**Trigger, not cause.** See theories-of-collapse.md §5 for evidence and
instruments. Key entries: the 536–660 CE Late Antique Little Ice Age; the Maya
Terminal Classic droughts; the 4.2 ka BP aridity event (contested); the Little
Ice Age and the 17th-century General Crisis.

**The correct formulation: identical shocks hit societies that survived them.
Climate explains timing. It never explains outcome by itself.**

---

## 7. Disease

**Trigger, with an unusually direct mechanism:** mortality removes taxpayers,
soldiers and specialists simultaneously and non-selectively. Unlike war it does
not spare the productive.

Key entries: Antonine (165–180 CE), Cyprian (~249–262), Justinianic (from 541,
*Y. pestis* confirmed by aDNA, demographic scale now disputed), Black Death
(1347–51, ~30–60% European mortality), Columbian exchange (1492 onward,
magnitude unknowable within a factor of several).

**The counter-intuitive finding: post-plague societies often improve for
survivors.** English real wages roughly doubled after the Black Death and stayed
elevated for over a century, because labour became scarce relative to land.
See the-control-group.md.

## 8. Interconnection fragility

**The claim:** networks optimised for efficiency lose the buffers that absorb
shocks. Specialisation means no node can substitute for another; tight coupling
means failures propagate faster than they can be contained.

**Best case: the Late Bronze Age.** Bronze required tin from thousands of
kilometres away. Every palace depended on a chain no palace controlled. When
enough links failed within decades, nobody had a fallback, and the region
switched to iron — which is locally available and was adopted *because* the
network failed, not the other way round.

**Steelman of the opposite view:** interconnection also confers resilience.
Diversified supply, information sharing, and the ability to import food during a
local failure are what prevent famines. **The Bronze Age network was fragile
because it had few nodes and no redundancy — nearly the opposite of a modern
high-redundancy network.** Anyone arguing "globalisation = Bronze Age" needs to
address the redundancy difference and usually doesn't.

## 9. Succession failure

**Grossly underrated relative to its explanatory power.**

**The claim:** every polity must transfer supreme power periodically. A
mechanism that is ambiguous, contested or absent converts each transfer into a
crisis, and repeated crises destroy the state's ability to face outward.

| Case | Detail |
|---|---|
| Roman third century | Roughly 26 claimants in 50 years, almost all dying violently. **The empire spent five decades fighting itself while Persia and the Goths were on the frontiers** |
| Alexander's empire | No designated heir; disintegrated among the Diadochi within a generation of 323 BCE |
| Mongol Empire | Möngke's death in 1259 recalled the princes for a succession *kurultai* and effectively ended the westward campaign. **A succession rule terminated the largest contiguous land empire's expansion more decisively than any army did** |
| Ottoman Empire | Institutionalised fratricide, then the *kafes* (confining princes), which produced rulers with no administrative experience |
| USSR | Gerontocratic succession — three General Secretaries died between 1982 and 1985 — then Gorbachev, with no mechanism for testing a reformer before granting supreme power |

**Contrast with what-makes-them-last.md #2: Venice's elaborate lottery-and-vote
ducal election, and China's examination system feeding the bureaucracy, are
succession technologies. The polities that lasted had them.**

## 10. Information distortion at the top

> **Cross-reference: `leaders/patterns-and-strategies.md` mechanism #13, Error
> Correction. This is that mechanism failing at civilisational scale, and the
> parallel is exact.**

**The claim:** decision-makers act on a model of reality assembled from reports
by people whose interests depend on how the reports are received. Without a
structural channel that forces bad news upward with enough force that it cannot
be politely ignored, the model degrades. **And the degradation is invisible from
inside by construction** — that is what makes it the most dangerous mechanism
here.

| Case | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Great Leap Forward, 1958–62** | Local cadres reported grain yields they knew to be false because the reported figures determined their careers. **The state then requisitioned against the false figures.** Famine deaths are usually estimated in the range of 15–45 million; the range is a political dispute and should be flagged as such |
| **Chernobyl, 1986** | A design flaw known to some officials was not communicated to operators; the initial response was to suppress the magnitude, including internally |
| **Soviet economic statistics** | Systematically fictional in ways the planners partly knew and could not correct without discrediting the plan |
| **Ceaușescu's Romania** | A leadership operating on ceremonial reports until the moment a crowd booed on live television |
| **Late Roman court** | Emperors increasingly insulated in ceremonial isolation after Diocletian's *dominus* reforms; accessible only through officials with an interest in what reached him |
| **Ming grain and military reporting** | Chronic misreporting of frontier strength and revenue |

**The general form: any hierarchy where the reporter's outcome depends on the
report's content will produce fiction, and the fiction accumulates fastest at
the top where it is least detectable.**

**Cross-reference `leaders/` mechanism #1** — asymmetric information is
load-bearing for everything, and #13 is what protects it over time. **At
civilisational scale, collapse is very often an error-correction failure: the
society had the information somewhere and could not route it to the people with
authority to act.**

## 11. Elite exclusion and the failure of absorption

**The claim:** polities that can absorb new elites — provincial, ethnic,
commercial, military — convert potential rivals into stakeholders. Polities that
close their elite create counter-elites with capability and grievance.

**Cases:** Rome's extension of citizenship, culminating in the Constitutio
Antoniniana (212 CE), which turned provincial elites into Roman ones and is
plausibly one of the reasons the empire lasted as long as it did. Chinese
examinations. Ottoman *devşirme*, which recruited administrators and soldiers
from outside the hereditary elite entirely. Against these: Venice's Serrata of
1297, the French *ancien régime*'s sale of offices producing an educated but
excluded bourgeoisie, and — arguably — modern credential inflation, where the
credential is sold as an entry ticket to a room that has not grown.

**This is mechanism #3's mirror image.** Overproduction is about supply;
exclusion is about the door.

## 12. Institution-building versus personal rule

> **Cross-reference: `leaders/` mechanism #7 (Institutions).**

**The claim:** capability embedded in a person dies with the person. Capability
embedded in a procedure, a training pipeline, a legal code or an archive can be
reproduced by successors who are less able than the founder — **which is the
normal case, since founders are selected from the extreme tail and successors
are not.**

**Cases:** Augustus built an administrative system; Alexander built a personal
following and it did not outlive him by a decade. Lee Kuan Yew's Singapore is
the modern textbook instance of converting personal capability into
institutional procedure. Against: personalist regimes generally — where the
succession problem (mechanism #9) and the information problem (mechanism #10)
compound, because a system built around one man's judgment has no mechanism for
telling that man he is wrong and no mechanism for replacing him.

**The three-way interaction is the important part: personal rule → no error
correction → no succession mechanism.** These are not three separate failures.
They are one failure with three symptoms, and it is the most common shape of
collapse in the record.

## How the mechanisms combine

**No case runs on one mechanism.** The recurring combinations:

| Combination | Typical outcome | Cases |
|---|---|---|
| Climate shock (6) + legitimacy claim about weather (4) | Rapid delegitimation of the ruling institution | Maya Terminal Classic |
| Fiscal loss (5) + territorial loss + army payment failure | Self-reinforcing spiral, hardest to escape | Western Rome after 439 |
| Elite overproduction (3) + fiscal distress (5) + immiseration | Civil conflict, elite purge, reset | Late Republic, French Revolution, Taiping |
| Interconnection (8) + multiple simultaneous shocks (6,7) | Cascading systems failure | Late Bronze Age |
| Personal rule (12) + no error correction (10) + no succession (9) | The commonest personalist failure | Third-century Rome, late USSR, most autocracies |
| Revenue shock (5) + reform that removes coercion before adding performance (4) + elite defection | Fast, bloodless dissolution | USSR 1991 |

**The diagnostic use: given a case, ask which mechanisms are present. If only
one, you are probably missing evidence rather than looking at a genuinely
monocausal collapse.**

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Collapse has a cause" | Every well-documented case is multi-causal. Monocausal accounts are a genre |
| "Moral decay causes collapse" | Every society's moralists say this about their own era, always. It is evidence of moralists, not of decay |
| "Invasion causes collapse" | Invasion is a trigger that usually fails. Rome repelled invasions for centuries. The question is why one succeeded |
| "They ran out of resources" | Rarely a physical exhaustion story. Usually a *fiscal capacity* or *distribution* story |
| "Leaders didn't see the warning signs" | Often they could not, because mechanism #10 had already destroyed the information supply. **Sovietologists with modern data missed 1991** |
| "Technology would have saved them" | The Late Bronze Age had the technology (iron) and adopted it *after* collapse. Availability is not adoption |
| "Complexity is bad" | Complexity is a problem-solving strategy that has costs. Byzantium's deliberate simplification bought 800 years, and China's re-complexification worked repeatedly |

## Open questions

- [ ] Can any of these mechanisms be measured prospectively rather than
      diagnosed retrospectively? Elite overproduction is the best candidate
- [ ] Is the personal-rule/error-correction/succession triad genuinely one
      mechanism or three? The distinction matters for whether it can be fixed
      piecewise
- [ ] What is the base rate of each mechanism across a properly sampled set of
      cases? Nobody has done this without a framework that pre-selects
- [ ] Does interconnection net out as fragility or resilience? The Bronze Age
      and the modern food system may be on opposite sides of a redundancy
      threshold nobody has located
- [ ] Is there a mechanism this list is missing entirely? Candidates: gender
      and reproductive dynamics, migration as a safety valve, the role of
      religion beyond legitimacy, information technology change as such
- [ ] The circularity problem: would a researcher with a different framework
      find different mechanisms in the same cases? Almost certainly yes. How
      much does that discount everything above?

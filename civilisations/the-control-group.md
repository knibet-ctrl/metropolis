## The problem in one sentence

**We study societies that left monuments, writing, durable goods and a dry
climate, and then we generalise about "civilisation" from that sample.**

Every stage of the filter runs the same direction:

| Filter | What it removes | What survives |
|---|---|---|
| **Material durability** | Wood, thatch, textile, leather, basketry, earth architecture | Stone, fired brick, ceramic, metal |
| **Climate** | Wet tropics, temperate forests where organics rot | Deserts, permafrost, anaerobic bogs |
| **Writing** | Oral traditions, mnemonic systems, knot records | Inscribed stone, baked clay, papyrus in dry conditions |
| **Monumentality** | Dispersed, low-density, egalitarian settlement | Pyramids, palaces, walls |
| **Excavation history** | Regions without funded archaeology; areas under modern cities | Colonial-era and nationally prestigious sites |
| **Scholarly interest** | Societies without a claimed descendant nation-state promoting them | Cases connected to European classical education |
| **Being conquered by literate neighbours** | Societies whose neighbours were also non-literate | Anyone Rome, China or Spain wrote about |

**Result: the archetype "civilisation" — dense, urban, monumental, literate,
hierarchical, state-organised — may be substantially an artefact of what
survives and what we dug for.**

## The evidence that the sample is biased, not just theoretically biased

This is not a philosophical worry. It has been demonstrated repeatedly, and
recently, by remote sensing.

| Discovery | What it overturned |
|---|---|
| **LiDAR in the Maya lowlands** (notably the PACUNAM survey published in 2018, mapping tens of thousands of structures under Guatemalan forest canopy) | Classic Maya population and landscape modification were substantially larger than a century of ground survey had established |
| **LiDAR at Angkor** | Revealed a vast low-density urban landscape around the temples — Angkor was a city-region, not a temple complex in jungle |
| **Amazonian earthworks and geoglyphs**, revealed as deforestation and LiDAR exposed them; and the **Casarabe culture** sites in the Llanos de Mojos, Bolivia (Prümers and colleagues, published in *Nature* in 2022, showing monumental platform and causeway settlements) | The "pristine Amazon" and "Amazonia could not support complex society" positions |
| **Terra preta** — anthropogenic dark earths across Amazonia | Large, sustained, soil-engineering populations in a region long described as marginal |
| **Tripolye/Cucuteni "mega-sites"** in Ukraine and Moldova (c. 4100–3300 BCE), e.g. Taljanky and Maidanetske | Settlements of thousands of people, plausibly among the largest in the world at the time, **with no identified palaces, temples or elite burials** |
| **Poverty Point, Louisiana** (c. 1700–1100 BCE) | Monumental earthworks built by hunter-fisher-gatherers, not farmers |
| **Göbekli Tepe** (c. 9500 BCE) | Monumental construction *before* agriculture |

**The pattern: every time our detection capability improves, we find that
societies were larger, more numerous, more sophisticated and less
state-shaped than we thought.** That is a systematic direction of error, not
noise. **The rate of upward revision should make you discount confident claims
about "the first," "the largest" and "the only."**

## Societies that did not become complex — and the ones that refused

### The default assumption is wrong

The standard implicit model treats state formation as an achievement that some
societies reached and others failed to reach — a ladder with rungs. **The
alternative is that state formation is one option among several, that it has
costs, and that many societies evaluated it and declined.**

### James C. Scott, *The Art of Not Being Governed* (2009)

**The Zomia thesis.** "Zomia" names the highland massif — roughly 2.5 million
square kilometres — running across upland Southeast Asia, southwest China and
northeast India. Scott argues its peoples were not "primitive remnants who
never developed states." They were **deliberate refugees from state-making**:
people who moved uphill and adopted practices that made them hard to tax,
conscript and enslave.

The practices, on his reading, are legible as **anti-state technology**:

| Practice | Anti-state function |
|---|---|
| Root and tuber crops (cassava, yams), swidden agriculture | Not harvestable on a schedule; not storable in a granary a tax collector can find |
| Dispersed, mobile settlement | Nothing to besiege, nobody to count |
| Segmentary, acephalous social organisation | No chief to co-opt or decapitate |
| Fluid, situational ethnic identity | Cannot be enumerated |
| **Non-literacy, possibly strategic** | Scott's most provocative claim: some groups have oral traditions of *having had* writing and losing it. Literacy is the state's technology |

**Steelmanned criticism:** Victor Lieberman's is the serious historical
objection — that Scott's chronology is off, that upland-lowland relations were
far more symbiotic and negotiated than a flight-from-the-state model implies,
that the model generalises a Burmese-Siamese pattern too widely, and that
"strategic illiteracy" is a romantic reading of what is more simply explained.
There is also a functionalist hazard in the whole framework: every observed
trait gets explained by its anti-state utility, which makes the thesis
unfalsifiable in the same way Tainter's is.

**Where it lands: the *possibility* of state avoidance as an active strategy is
established and important. The claim that it explains the highland cultures in
detail is Tier 2/3.**

### Other entries in the refusal column

- **Pierre Clastres, *Society Against the State* (1974).** From Amazonian
  ethnography: some societies have chiefs with *no coercive power at all* —
  the chief is obliged to be generous, to speak often and be ignored, to give
  away goods. Clastres reads this not as an absent state but as an **active
  institutional defence against the emergence of one.**
- **Graeber and Wengrow, *The Dawn of Everything* (2021).** The most ambitious
  recent attempt to break the ladder model. Key exhibits: Teotihuacan appearing
  to abandon pyramid-building around 300 CE in favour of high-quality standard
  apartment compounds for most of its population; Tripolye mega-sites without
  elites; societies with seasonal dual constitutions (following Mauss on Inuit
  winter/summer social forms); and Poverty Point. **Steelmanned criticism from
  archaeologists: extensive arguing from absence of evidence, selective case
  choice, and a tendency to read political intent into material patterns that
  admit simpler explanations.** It is a valuable corrective and a weak
  monograph, and both are true.
- **The North American Plains after the horse.** Groups that had been
  agricultural villagers *abandoned* farming for mounted bison hunting when the
  horse arrived. A voluntary move away from sedentary agriculture, well
  documented, and inconvenient for ladder models.

## The uncomfortable part: collapse was sometimes good

**Tier 2, with real evidence and real caveats, and it is the most
counterintuitive thing in the notebook.**

### The evidence

| Case | Evidence | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Post-Black Death Europe** | English real wages roughly doubled after 1348–50 and remained elevated for well over a century. Labour scarcity broke serfdom's economics in western Europe; the Statute of Labourers (1351) is legislative evidence that elites were trying and failing to hold wages down | **Strong** |
| **Post-Roman Britain and parts of the West** | Some skeletal series suggest stature and certain health indicators improved after the Roman withdrawal, plausibly reflecting the end of taxation, conscription, urban crowding and long-distance disease transmission. **Contested and sample-dependent** | Moderate |
| **Post-Classic northern Maya** | Population and construction shift north; the political collapse of the southern divine kingships was not a regional demographic catastrophe | Moderate-strong |
| **Post-collapse Mesopotamian peripheries** | Scott's argument that "barbarian" periphery populations frequently enjoyed better diet, more leisure and more autonomy than tax-paying agrarian subjects | Moderate |
| **End of the Angkorian hydraulic state** | The end of a massive corvée-labour obligation | Weak-moderate; under-studied |

### Scheidel's argument — the sharpest version

Walter Scheidel, *The Great Leveler* (2017), makes a claim that is deeply
unwelcome across the political spectrum: **the only forces that have reliably
reduced material inequality in recorded history are catastrophes.** He names
four: mass-mobilisation warfare, transformative revolution, **state collapse**,
and lethal pandemic.

The mechanism for state collapse is specific and clean: **large-scale
inequality requires a state to enforce property claims.** Landholding at scale
depends on courts, records, and men with weapons who will evict people. Remove
the state and the top of the distribution has no way to defend its position.
Everyone gets poorer; the rich get poorer faster; the distribution flattens.

**This is not an argument for collapse.** Scheidel is explicit that the levelling
is bought at appalling cost and that nobody should want it. **It is an argument
against assuming that collapse is uniformly and for everyone bad**, which is
what the word smuggles in.

### The caveats, stated properly

1. **The osteological paradox** (Wood et al., 1992) cuts both ways here.
   Skeletal indicators of stress can indicate *survival* of stress. Health
   inferences from bones are less direct than they appear.
2. **Skeletal samples are not random.** Who gets buried where, and which
   cemeteries get excavated, are both non-random.
3. **"Better for the median person" is not the only metric.** Total population,
   accumulated knowledge, technological capability and the possibility of a life
   not spent on subsistence all fell.
4. **The transition itself was violent even where the endpoint was better.** The
   Black Death killed a third to half of Europe to produce the wage rise.
5. **These findings are asserted more confidently in popular writing than the
   underlying literature supports.** Treat "collapse was good actually" as an
   important corrective that has itself become a fashionable overstatement.

## What the control group does to the rest of the notebook

Five specific discounts:

1. **The n is smaller than it looks, and it is not random.** Perhaps thirty
   reasonably documented cases, all from the monument-and-writing filter, none
   independent of each other.
2. **"Collapse" may be over-detected in exactly the societies that show it
   most legibly.** A society with no monuments cannot show us monument
   cessation.
3. **Our baseline for "normal" is a state society.** So state absence reads as
   deficit rather than as a different equilibrium.
4. **Elite-produced evidence produces elite-centred theory.** Every one of the
   twelve mechanisms in mechanisms.md is a story about elites, because that is
   whose records we have.
5. **The upward-revision trend should be priced in.** Every improvement in
   detection has found more, bigger and earlier. Assume current estimates are
   floors.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Some societies never developed civilisation" | Some evaluated state formation and moved away from it. The ladder model is a 19th-century inheritance |
| "Hunter-gatherers were on their way to becoming farmers" | Many were in contact with farmers for millennia and declined. Some Plains groups *abandoned* farming for mounted hunting |
| "The Amazon was pristine wilderness" | Terra preta, earthworks, geoglyphs and Casarabe monumental sites show large, landscape-engineering populations |
| "We have a good sample of past societies" | We have a sample filtered seven ways, all in the same direction |
| "Collapse is uniformly catastrophic" | For elites, yes. For the median person, sometimes the opposite. Post-Black-Death wages are the clearest instance |
| "Inequality falls through reform" | Scheidel's uncomfortable finding is that it has almost only fallen through catastrophe. Uncomfortable for everyone, including him |
| "Illiteracy means backwardness" | Scott's argument that non-literacy can be strategic is contested, but "writing = advancement" is a value judgment wearing a fact's clothes |
| "Archaeology has mostly found what there is to find" | LiDAR has forced major upward revisions in three regions in twenty years |
| "Dark ages were dark" | Lefkandi in post-palatial Greece; continued elite wealth and long-distance trade in a period defined by the absence of the *records* we use to detect activity |

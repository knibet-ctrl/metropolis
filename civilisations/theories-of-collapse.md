# Theories of Collapse

> **Status: Tier 2 overall.** Each framework below has real evidence and real
> critics. None commands consensus. **The strongest position in the field is
> that these are not rival theories of one thing but partial theories of
> different things, operating at different timescales, and the search for a
> single master variable has failed.**

**Written from general knowledge without live source verification.** Attributions
of specific claims to specific scholars are the least reliable content here.

---

## The seven frameworks at a glance

| Framework | Core claim | Timescale | Best case for it | Worst case for it | Falsifiable? | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Tainter** — declining marginal returns on complexity | Complexity is an investment; returns fall; collapse is rational disinvestment | Centuries | Western Rome; Maya | Cases where complexity kept paying (China, Byzantium) | Weakly — marginal returns are hard to measure | T2 |
| **Diamond** — environment + response | Five factors: env. damage, climate, hostile neighbours, lost trade partners, societal response | Decades–centuries | Norse Greenland | Rapa Nui (probably wrong) | Partly | T2, mixed record |
| **Turchin / Goldstone** — structural-demographic | Elite overproduction + popular immiseration + fiscal distress → instability | ~50 and ~200–300 year cycles | Early modern revolutions; Han/Tang/Ming cycles | Cases with no measurable elite series | Yes, in principle; contested in practice | T2 |
| **Ibn Khaldun** — *asabiyyah* decay | Group solidarity is highest at the margin, erodes with urban comfort over ~4 generations | ~120 years | Maghrebi dynasties; arguably the Ottomans | Sedentary states with no nomadic periphery | Loosely | T2/T3 |
| **Climate forcing** | Exogenous shocks exceed adaptive capacity | Years–decades | 536–660 CE cooling; Maya droughts | Cases where similar shocks caused nothing | Increasingly yes | T2, improving fast |
| **Disease** | Mortality shocks break the fiscal and labour base | Years | Justinianic plague; Columbian exchange | Presence of pathogen ≠ demographic impact | Yes, via aDNA | T1 on occurrence, T2 on effect |
| **Systems fragility** | Tight coupling makes networks fail cascadingly | Instant–years | Late Bronze Age; 2008 financial | Hard to distinguish from "everything is connected" | Weakly | T2/T3 |

## 1. Tainter — collapse as economising

*The Collapse of Complex Societies* (1988). The most influential single book in
the field and the most rigorously argued.

**The argument in four steps:**
1. Human societies are problem-solving organisations.
2. Solving problems requires increased complexity — more specialists, more
   administration, more information processing — and complexity requires energy
   subsidy.
3. **Investment in complexity, like any investment, reaches a point of declining
   marginal returns.** The cheap, high-yield problems get solved first. Each
   additional layer of bureaucracy, each additional frontier legion, each
   additional level of appeal in a legal system, buys less than the one before.
4. Once marginal returns turn negative, collapse — rapid simplification —
   becomes an *attractive economising option* for large parts of the
   population. **Collapse is not a failure to adapt; it is an adaptation.**

**Why it is strong:** it explains the otherwise puzzling observation that
collapse is often not resisted. Roman peasants in Gaul in the 5th century
sometimes preferred Gothic rule, because the Goths taxed less. It gives collapse
a mechanism rather than a morality. And it survives the "but they had warning"
objection: the theory predicts elites will *keep investing* precisely because
they capture the remaining returns.

**Steelmanned criticism:**
- **Marginal returns on complexity are close to unmeasurable.** Tainter's own
  charts (medical spending vs. life expectancy, patents per R&D dollar) are
  suggestive analogies, not measurements of the ancient cases.
- The theory is nearly unfalsifiable in practice: any collapse can be
  redescribed as declining marginal returns after the fact.
- **China is a standing problem.** It underwent repeated dynastic collapse and
  each time *reconstituted a comparably complex state*, sometimes within a
  generation. If complexity had genuinely hit negative returns, that shouldn't
  happen. Tainter's answer — that a competitive peer-polity environment forces
  re-complexification — is plausible but ad hoc.
- Byzantium after the 7th century *simplified deliberately* (the theme system,
  a demonetised soldier-farmer army) and then survived another 800 years, which
  looks like adaptive simplification rather than collapse. Haldon's work on this
  is the best counter-case: **it shows simplification can be a recovery
  strategy, which blurs Tainter's key category.**

## 2. Diamond — environment and response

*Collapse: How Societies Choose to Fail or Succeed* (2005).

**Steelmanned first, because it is usually caricatured.** Diamond's framework is
explicitly five-factor and the fifth factor is *the society's response* — so
"environmental determinism" is not a fair reading of the book, whatever the
subtitle implies. His strongest case is **Norse Greenland** (settled ~985 CE by
Erik the Red, gone by the mid-15th century), where the argument is genuinely
good: the Norse maintained a Northern European cattle-and-sheep economy in a
cooling sub-Arctic environment, appear to have eaten very little fish despite
abundant stocks, and did not adopt Inuit technologies (kayaks, ring seal
hunting, snow shelters) that were demonstrably viable in the same place at the
same time. **A cultural identity that blocked adaptive borrowing is a real and
important mechanism.**

**Steelmanned criticism:**
- The Rapa Nui chapter is probably wrong in its central claim. See
  case-maya-easter-bronze-age.md.
- *Questioning Collapse* (McAnany and Yoffee, eds., 2010) is the organised
  archaeological response. Its core charges: Diamond selects cases to fit,
  underweights colonialism and disease as causes of the population declines he
  attributes to self-inflicted ecological damage, and — the sharpest charge —
  **describes as "collapsed" societies whose descendants are alive, present, and
  reading his book.**
- "Societies choose to fail" attributes agency to a collective that has no
  single decision procedure. Who chose? Elites choosing to protect their
  position is a different phenomenon from a society choosing.
- The Greenland Norse case itself has been complicated by later work suggesting
  significant marine diet from isotope evidence, and by the argument that the
  colony's collapse had more to do with the collapse of the walrus-ivory market
  (following the availability of African elephant ivory in Europe) than with
  soil erosion. **If that holds, Greenland becomes a trade-dependency case, not
  an ecocide case** — which would remove Diamond's best example from the
  ecocide column.

## 3. Turchin and cliodynamics — structural-demographic theory

Peter Turchin, building on Jack Goldstone's *Revolution and Rebellion in the
Early Modern World* (1991). Key works: *Historical Dynamics* (2003), *Secular
Cycles* (with Nefedov, 2009), *Ages of Discord* (2016), *End Times* (2023).

**The model has four interacting components:** general population, elites, the
state (fiscal health), and instability. The causal core:

1. Population grows against a relatively fixed resource base → labour surplus →
   **real wages fall, popular immiseration rises**.
2. Cheap labour and rising rents make elite status highly profitable → elites
   grow in number *faster than the positions available for them*.
3. **Elite overproduction** — more credentialed aspirants than slots — turns
   elites against each other. Factionalism, patronage inflation, radicalisation
   of frustrated elite aspirants ("counter-elites").
4. Elite consumption plus military competition drains state finances →
   **fiscal distress** → the state loses capacity to arbitrate.
5. Instability follows, kills or dispossesses enough elites and commoners to
   reset the cycle. Long "secular cycles" of ~200–300 years, with a shorter
   ~50-year "fathers-and-sons" oscillation of violence within them.

**Why it is strong:** it is the only framework here that makes quantitative,
out-of-sample predictions. Turchin published a forecast in 2010 (in a Nature
letter) that US instability would peak around 2020, on the basis of the model
rather than the news. **Whatever you think of the theory, that is how a science
behaves.** The elite-overproduction mechanism also has independent support: the
inflation of the late Roman senatorial order, the Ming glut of degree-holders
without office, the Ottoman *timar* holder squeeze, and the modern
overproduction of law and doctoral graduates relative to positions.

**Steelmanned criticism:**
- **Parameter fitting.** Cycles of "200–300 years" with adjustable phase can be
  fitted to almost any historical series. The degrees of freedom are large and
  the number of independent cases is small.
- Data quality. Pre-modern wage and elite series are reconstructions with
  enormous error bars, and Turchin's aggregation choices are contestable.
- The Seshat databank, built to test these models at scale, has been criticised
  on coding reliability — whether independent coders would assign the same
  values to the same societies. This is a live and unresolved dispute and it
  matters, because the quantitative claims rest on it.
- The 2020 prediction was arguably vague enough to be unfalsifiable; "elevated
  instability in a large country in a given decade" has a high base rate.
- **Non-independence of cases.** Later societies read about earlier ones.
  Statistical tests that assume independent observations are invalid here, and
  this problem is under-addressed across the whole quantitative-history field.

## 4. Ibn Khaldun — *asabiyyah*

*Muqaddimah* (1377). **The most under-credited work in collapse studies, and
its omission from Western surveys is a genuine failure of the field, not a
matter of taste.**

**The theory:** *asabiyyah* — usually rendered "group feeling," "social
cohesion," or "solidarity" — is the capacity of a group to act as one. It is
generated by shared hardship, kinship, and marginal environments where mutual
dependence is enforced by conditions. It is the real currency of political
power; military and fiscal resources follow from it, not the other way around.

**The cycle:**
1. A cohesive group from a harsh periphery (for Ibn Khaldun, typically Bedouin)
   possesses high *asabiyyah* and low material comfort.
2. It conquers a sedentary dynasty whose *asabiyyah* has decayed.
3. In power, it acquires cities, luxury, taxation, and a standing army of paid
   professionals rather than kinsmen.
4. **Comfort dissolves the very solidarity that produced the conquest.** By the
   fourth generation, the ruling family has no memory of the conditions that
   created its cohesion, rules through mercenaries and bureaucrats, and taxes
   heavily to fund a lifestyle its founders would not have recognised.
5. A new cohesive group from the periphery arrives. Roughly 3–4 generations,
   ~120 years.

**What makes it genuinely good, not merely early:**
- It is **mechanistic and non-teleological.** No destiny, no organic lifespan,
  no divine judgment — a specified social variable with specified inputs and a
  specified decay function.
- It contains a **fiscal sub-model that anticipates the Laffer curve by six
  centuries**: early dynasties take low rates from a wide base and grow revenue;
  late dynasties raise rates, suppress the activity being taxed, and collect
  less. He states this explicitly.
- It correctly identifies **legitimacy and cohesion as the load-bearing
  variables**, which is where the modern field has independently arrived (see
  mechanisms.md #4).
- It is essentially **elite-cohesion theory**, which makes it the direct
  ancestor of Turchin's elite dynamics. The lineage is rarely acknowledged.

**Steelmanned criticism:** the generational schedule is too neat and looks
retrofitted. The theory generalises from 14th-century Maghrebi dynastic
experience, and the nomad-conquers-sedentary pattern that grounds it is
characteristic of a particular Eurasian arid belt in a particular era — it does
not obviously apply to Mesoamerica, the Andes, or anywhere after gunpowder
ended the steppe military advantage (roughly the 16th–17th centuries). And
*asabiyyah* is not operationalised; it is diagnosed after the fact from the
outcome it is meant to explain, which is the same circularity charge levelled
at Tainter.

## 5. Climate forcing

The fastest-improving strand, because the proxies are getting good.

**The instruments:** ice cores (Greenland GISP2/GRIP, Antarctic) give volcanic
sulphate spikes and can date eruptions to within a year or two; tree rings give
annual growing-season temperature; speleothems (cave formations, e.g. Yok Balum
in Belize) give rainfall; lake sediment cores give regional hydrology.

**The strongest cases:**

| Event | Evidence | Associated collapse | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **536–660 CE Late Antique Little Ice Age** | Massive 536 and 539/540 volcanic signals in ice cores; a documented "dust veil" reported by Procopius and Cassiodorus; tree-ring minimum | Coincides with Justinianic plague, Sasanian–Roman exhaustion, Avar and Arab expansions | **Occurrence T1; causal role T2** |
| **Maya Terminal Classic droughts** | Chichancanab lake cores; Yok Balum speleothem showing multi-decadal drying c. 820–870 and later | Southern lowland abandonment | Occurrence T1; sole causation rejected |
| **4.2 ka BP event (~2200 BCE)** | Aridity proxies across the Near East and beyond | Akkadian empire's fall; Egyptian First Intermediate Period | **T2/T3 — the "global 4.2 ka event" framing is genuinely contested** |
| **Little Ice Age / General Crisis of the 17th century** | Solar minima, volcanic forcing, extensive documentary records | Ming fall 1644, Thirty Years' War, Fronde, English Civil War, Ottoman crisis | T2 — Geoffrey Parker's *Global Crisis* (2013) is the maximal statement |

**The central problem with all climate explanations:** *similar shocks
frequently caused nothing.* The same droughts hit regions that carried on. So
climate cannot be the whole explanation; it functions as a **stress test that
reveals pre-existing fragility.** Kyle Harper's *The Fate of Rome* (2017) is the
most ambitious application to Rome and has been criticised by John Haldon and
colleagues for exactly this — over-attributing to climate what requires a
political and institutional account.

**The right framing: climate supplies triggers; society supplies
vulnerability.** Triggers are common and cheap. Vulnerability is the
explanation.

## 6. Disease

Palaeogenomics has moved this from inference to fact.

- **Antonine Plague, 165–180 CE.** Probably smallpox. Reported to have killed
  Lucius Verus. Mortality estimates range from 2% to 25% of the empire — a
  range so wide it is barely an estimate.
- **Plague of Cyprian, ~249–262 CE.** Pathogen unidentified. Coincides with the
  onset of the third-century crisis.
- **Justinianic Plague, 541 CE onward.** *Yersinia pestis* **confirmed by
  ancient DNA from burials at Aschheim in Bavaria and elsewhere.** Pathogen
  identification is now Tier 1. **Demographic impact is Tier 2 and has been
  revised sharply downward** by a group of researchers (the "maximalist
  narrative" critique, arguing that the textual sources are rhetorical and that
  archaeological, numismatic and pollen evidence show no corresponding
  population crash). This is one of the sharpest active disputes in the field.
- **Black Death, 1347–1351.** ~30–60% of European mortality — the high end is
  now better supported than the older 25–33% figure. Well evidenced.
- **Columbian exchange, 1492 onward.** The largest demographic event in the
  record. **The size is unknowable within a factor of several** because
  pre-contact population estimates range from ~8 million to ~112 million for
  the Americas. Note also the growing argument that violence, enslavement and
  social disruption — not pathogens alone — drove much of the decline, and
  that the "virgin soil epidemic" framing has been used to make colonial
  depopulation sound like a natural disaster.

## 7. Systems fragility

The least historically grounded and most conceptually fashionable strand.

Core claim: **networks that are optimised for efficiency become fragile in a
characteristic way.** Tight coupling means failures propagate faster than they
can be contained; specialisation means no node can substitute for another;
just-in-time provisioning removes the buffers that absorb shocks.

Intellectual sources: Charles Perrow's *Normal Accidents* (1984) on tightly
coupled, complexly interactive systems; C.S. Holling's adaptive cycle and
panarchy (resilience ecology); Taleb on fragility and optionality; the
"critical transitions" literature (Scheffer, Carpenter and others) proposing
**early warning signals** — critical slowing down, rising variance and rising
autocorrelation as a system approaches a tipping point.

**The best historical case is the Late Bronze Age.** A palace-based
redistributive economy across the eastern Mediterranean depended on bronze, and
bronze required tin, and tin came from thousands of kilometres away (Central
Asia, possibly Cornwall). Every node needed the network. When enough nodes
failed within a few decades, the rest had no substitute.

**Steelmanned criticism:** "everything is connected and connection is risky" is
not a theory; it is a mood. Interconnection also confers *resilience* —
diversified supply, information sharing, mutual aid, and the ability to import
food during a local failure. The Late Bronze Age network was fragile because it
had **few nodes and no redundancy**, which is nearly the opposite of a modern
high-redundancy network. The early-warning-signal literature is promising but
has so far worked best in ecology and lake systems; applying it to societies
requires time series we do not have for the ancient cases.

## What the comparison shows

**These frameworks are not competitors for the same explanatory slot.** Sorted
by what they actually explain:

| Framework | Actually explains |
|---|---|
| Climate, disease | **Triggers.** Why *then* |
| Tainter, systems fragility | **Vulnerability.** Why the trigger propagated |
| Turchin, Ibn Khaldun | **Why elites failed to respond.** The coordination failure |
| Diamond | An attempt at all three, which is why it is both popular and criticised |

**A well-formed explanation of any specific collapse needs one from each row.**
Explanations offering only a trigger are the most common and the weakest.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Diamond showed civilisations destroy their environments" | He argued environment is one of five factors including response, and his headline case (Rapa Nui) is probably wrong |
| "Tainter proved complexity causes collapse" | He proposed a mechanism and illustrated it. Marginal returns on complexity have never been directly measured for an ancient society |
| "Turchin's model predicted 2020" | It made a genuine ex-ante forecast, which is more than any rival did — but "instability in the 2020s" has a high base rate and scoring it honestly is hard |
| "Cliodynamics is pseudoscience" | Too strong. It is a real research programme with real methodological problems, especially data coding and non-independence |
| "Ibn Khaldun was a precursor" | He was a full theorist with a mechanism, a fiscal model and a decay function. "Precursor" undersells him by a lot |
| "The Justinianic plague halved the population" | Increasingly doubted. Pathogen confirmed; demographic catastrophe substantially disputed |
| "Climate change caused X collapse" | Climate provides timing, not sufficiency. The same shocks hit societies that survived them |
| "The Sea Peoples destroyed the Bronze Age world" | The Egyptian sources are propaganda from the winning side, "Sea Peoples" is a modern collective label, and they are as plausibly a *symptom* of collapse as a cause |
| "Spengler and Toynbee are foundational" | Both are historically important and methodologically abandoned. Organicism and challenge-and-response are unfalsifiable |

## Open questions

- [ ] Can marginal returns on societal complexity be operationalised for any
      pre-modern case at all? If not, is Tainter a theory or a metaphor?
- [ ] Would independent coders reproduce the Seshat databank's values? Until
      that is settled, how much weight can cliodynamic results carry?
- [ ] Does the Norse Greenland ivory-market explanation displace the ecological
      one, and if so does Diamond retain any first-rank case?
- [ ] Is the Justinianic-plague minimalist revision correct? This is currently
      the most consequential live dispute about ancient demography
- [ ] Can early-warning signals (critical slowing down) be detected in any
      historical time series with adequate resolution?
- [ ] How would you test *asabiyyah* without diagnosing it from the outcome?
- [ ] Is China's repeated re-complexification a refutation of Tainter or an
      elaboration of him?
- [ ] Given non-independence of cases, what is the correct statistical
      framework for comparative history? Nobody has solved this

# Sleep — Solid Foundations, Overstated Superstructure

> **Status: TIER 1** for the acute functional effects of sleep deprivation and
> for CBT-I as an effective insomnia treatment. **TIER 2** for the long-term
> health associations, where reverse causation is severe. **TIER 3** for most of
> the mechanistic claims that circulate in popular sleep writing.

> **Not medical advice.** Persistent insomnia, loud snoring with daytime
> sleepiness, or sudden changes in sleep pattern warrant clinical assessment.
> **Undiagnosed obstructive sleep apnoea is common, treatable, and consequential
> — it is the single most valuable thing in this file to rule out.** No sleep
> medication, dose or supplement is recommended here.

> **Read this file with a specific caution: anxiety about sleep is itself a
> cause of insomnia. If reading about sleep risks makes you worry about your
> sleep, that worry is a mechanism, not a side effect. See the section on
> catastrophising below, which is deliberately placed before the risk material.**

## First, the thing that matters most in this file

**Do not catastrophise about individual bad nights.**

The dominant psychological model of chronic insomnia — Espie's
**attention–intention–effort** pathway, and the older **Spielman 3P model**
(predisposing, precipitating, perpetuating factors) — holds that acute insomnia
becomes chronic through the perpetuating factors the sufferer introduces:
monitoring sleep, trying to sleep, going to bed early to compensate, and
worrying about consequences.

**In that model, alarming health information about sleep is itself a
perpetuating factor.** A person who reads that poor sleep causes dementia and
then lies awake worrying about dementia has been made worse by accurate
information delivered badly.

| True | Also true |
|---|---|
| Chronic sleep restriction has measurable costs | **A bad night is normal, recoverable, and not a health event** |
| Sleep matters | **Trying hard to sleep reliably prevents sleep** |
| Sleep duration correlates with outcomes | Correlation includes people whose illness causes their sleep pattern |

**"Orthosomnia"** (Baron et al., 2017) names the clinical phenomenon: patients
presenting with insomnia caused by anxiety about tracker-reported sleep quality.
Consumer trackers have **poor sleep-stage classification accuracy** against
polysomnography, so a meaningful number of these patients are anxious about a
number that is wrong.

## What sleep does — by confidence

| Claim | Tier | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Sleep is required; total deprivation is fatal in animals | **1** | Rechtschaffen's rat studies (1983–89); fatal familial insomnia in humans |
| Acute restriction degrades attention, reaction time, working memory and mood | **1** | Many randomised lab studies |
| **People underestimate their own impairment when chronically restricted** | **1** | Van Dongen et al. (2003), *Sleep*: 14 days at 6h/night produced cognitive deficits comparable to 1–2 nights of total deprivation, while **subjective sleepiness plateaued.** The clearest and most practically important finding in the file |
| Sleep is involved in memory consolidation | **1–2** | Robust in lab paradigms; magnitude in real learning is debated |
| Sleep loss impairs glucose tolerance and appetite regulation acutely | **1–2** | Randomised short-term studies (Spiegel, Van Cauter); long-term consequence less clear |
| Sleep loss affects immune function | **2** | Vaccine-response and rhinovirus-challenge studies exist and are small |
| Short sleep duration predicts mortality | **2** | U-shaped, nadir ~7h. **Long sleep is almost certainly reverse causation.** Short sleep partly so |
| The brain clears metabolic waste more during sleep | **2, and now contested** | Xie et al. (*Science*, 2013) reported expanded interstitial space and increased clearance in sleeping mice. **A 2024 *Nature* study reported reduced clearance during sleep and anaesthesia**, directly challenging the glymphatic story.† Unresolved |
| Sleep loss causes Alzheimer's disease | **3** | Amyloid associations exist (Shokri-Kojori et al., PNAS, 2018, small n). **The causal direction is unresolved — Alzheimer's disrupts sleep years before diagnosis** |
| There is a "sleep loss epidemic" with population sleep declining | **Not well supported** | Time-use survey data (Youngstedt et al.'s reviews) show little consistent long-term decline in objectively or diary-measured sleep duration |

## Circadian rhythm

**The best-established mechanistic biology in this file, and worth separating
from sleep duration.**

- The suprachiasmatic nucleus is entrained principally by **light**, especially
  short-wavelength, especially in the morning.
- The 2017 Nobel Prize (Hall, Rosbash, Young) recognised the molecular clock
  mechanism — a genuine, mechanistically-understood system, unlike most of what
  appears in this notebook.
- **Regularity of timing appears to matter, possibly more than duration.**
  Several cohorts report that sleep-timing *variability* predicts outcomes after
  adjusting for duration. **Observational; the finding is recent and I hold it
  loosely.**
- **Shift work:** IARC re-evaluated night shift work as **Group 2A, probably
  carcinogenic to humans** (2019, revising the 2007 assessment). Confounding in
  shift-work cohorts is severe — shift workers differ systematically in class,
  smoking and diet — and the classification has been contested on those grounds.
- **Social jetlag** (Roenneberg): the mismatch between biological and social
  clock timing. Real construct; health consequences largely observational.

**Chronotype is substantially genetic** and only partially shiftable. The
"become a morning person" genre is selling something that biology only partly
permits.

## Insomnia — and the treatment nobody has heard of

**CBT-I (cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia) is the first-line
recommended treatment for chronic insomnia in adults**, per the **American
College of Physicians guideline (2016)**, the AASM, and European guidelines.

**This is under-known to the point of being a public health failure.**

| | CBT-I | Hypnotic medication |
|---|---|---|
| Short-term efficacy | Comparable | Comparable |
| **Long-term efficacy** | **Maintained after treatment ends** | Effects diminish; discontinuation often produces rebound |
| Guideline status | **First-line** | Second-line, for short-term or adjunctive use |
| Availability | **Poor — few trained providers** | Excellent |
| Typical duration | 4–8 sessions | Indefinite in practice |

**The components** (behavioural, not pharmacological): stimulus control,
**sleep restriction therapy** (counter-intuitively, deliberately *reducing* time
in bed to consolidate sleep — this must be done with guidance, as it transiently
increases sleepiness and is unsafe for drivers and shift workers), cognitive
restructuring of sleep-related beliefs, and relaxation training.

**Digital CBT-I** has genuine randomised evidence (SHUTi, Sleepio and others),
which matters because provider scarcity is the binding constraint. **Effect
sizes in digital trials are smaller than in-person and the trials are often
run by the product's owners.** Both facts are relevant.

**What this file will not do is tell you to try it.** Sleep restriction therapy
in particular is a clinical protocol with real short-term risks and it should be
supervised.

## Sleep apnoea — the most consequential thing to rule out

Obstructive sleep apnoea is **common, substantially undiagnosed, and
associated with hypertension, cardiovascular events, daytime sleepiness and
motor vehicle accidents.**

**The complication that must be stated:** CPAP reliably improves symptoms,
daytime function and blood pressure, but the large randomised trials for
**cardiovascular event reduction** — most notably **SAVE (2016)** — were
**null**. Adherence in those trials was low (~3–4 hours/night), which is the
standard explanation and is not a fully satisfying one.

**So: strong evidence for symptoms and function, weak evidence for hard
cardiovascular outcomes.** That is an honest summary and it differs from how the
condition is usually described.

## The *Why We Sleep* problem

**Matthew Walker's *Why We Sleep* (2017) is the most influential popular book in
this domain, and specific claims in it have been substantively challenged.**

**Alexey Guzey's December 2019 critique** documented a set of errors, including:

- The claim that **"the shorter your sleep, the shorter your lifespan"** stated
  as a simple monotonic relationship, when the underlying data is U-shaped —
  long sleep is also associated with mortality.
- A figure reproduced from a published paper with a data point or axis
  presented in a way that changed its apparent implication.†
- Characterisation of the WHO/IARC classification of *night shift work* as
  probably carcinogenic in terms that implied a broader declaration about sleep
  loss.
- The claim that no biological function is unaffected by sleep loss, and other
  categorical statements presented without the qualifications in the sources.

UC Berkeley conducted a review following the critique. Some corrections were
subsequently made to the text.†

**How to hold this fairly:**

| Not undermined | Undermined |
|---|---|
| That sleep is biologically essential | That the dose-response is simple and monotonic |
| That acute restriction impairs function | That specific dramatic mortality claims are supported |
| That circadian biology is real and important | That there is a documented population-wide "sleep loss epidemic" |
| That insomnia is treatable | That every stated mechanism is established |

**The general lesson is not about one author.** A single confident popular book
can install claims in millions of heads faster than the correction can travel,
and the correction never reaches the same audience. See
knowledge/how-theories-die.md.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| Everyone needs exactly 8 hours | Requirement varies; the mortality nadir sits near 7h and individual variation is wide. A rare genetic short-sleep phenotype exists (**DEC2**, **ADRB1** variants — Ying-Hui Fu's work) and is genuinely rare |
| A bad night is damaging | It is normal. **Worrying about it is the actual mechanism of harm** |
| You can't catch up on sleep | Partially you can. **Depner et al. (2019)** found weekend recovery sleep did not fully reverse metabolic effects — but "partial recovery" is not "no recovery," and the catastrophic framing is unsupported |
| Blue light from phones is a major sleep disruptor | The melatonin-suppression effect is real at high lab intensities. **At typical evening phone brightness the effect is small, and blue-blocking glasses trials are mixed to null.** The *content* keeping you awake is a better explanation than the wavelength |
| Sleep trackers measure your sleep | They estimate duration reasonably and **classify stages poorly.** Orthosomnia is a documented consequence |
| Melatonin is a sleeping pill | It is principally a **circadian phase-shifting** signal — useful for jet lag and delayed phase, weak as a hypnotic. Over-the-counter doses in the US are typically far above what phase-shifting requires, and regulation of content is poor. **Not a recommendation** |
| Alcohol helps you sleep | It shortens sleep onset and **degrades sleep architecture**, suppressing REM early and fragmenting the second half. Among the most reliable findings here |
| Napping is a sign of laziness or is harmful | Short naps improve alertness. Long/late naps disturb night sleep. Cohort associations between napping and mortality are heavily confounded by illness |
| Randy Gardner's 11 days proves humans can adapt | It is an anecdote from 1964 with no controls and disputed observation. **It proves nothing** |
| The glymphatic system definitively clears amyloid during sleep | The 2013 mouse finding was striking and the 2024 challenge is serious. **This is an open scientific question being reported as a settled fact** |
| Sleep hygiene advice fixes insomnia | **Sleep hygiene alone is not an effective treatment for chronic insomnia** and is often used as the control condition against which CBT-I is shown to be superior |
| More sleep is always better | Long sleep is consistently associated with worse outcomes. Almost certainly reverse causation — but it does mean "maximise sleep" is not supported |

## Open questions

- [ ] **Is short sleep causally related to mortality, or is it a marker for illness, shift work, poverty and stress?** Mendelian randomisation on sleep traits exists and is not decisive.
- [ ] Does the glymphatic clearance story survive? The 2024 challenge is direct and the field has not settled.
- [ ] Which direction does the sleep–dementia arrow point? Alzheimer's pathology disrupts sleep years before diagnosis, which is enough to generate the observed association without any causal contribution from sleep loss.
- [ ] Why did CPAP fail to reduce cardiovascular events in SAVE and similar trials? Adherence is the standard answer and it may be a rationalisation.
- [ ] Is sleep *regularity* more important than duration? Recent cohorts suggest yes. **Nobody has randomised it.**
- [ ] Has population sleep duration actually declined? The time-use data says largely no, which undercuts a premise of the entire popular genre.
- [ ] Can digital CBT-I close the provider gap at scale, and how much of its measured effect survives independent (non-vendor) evaluation?
- [ ] What is the actual harm profile of long-term hypnotic use? Observational associations with dementia and mortality are severely confounded by indication.

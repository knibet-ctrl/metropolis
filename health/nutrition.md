# Nutrition — The Weakest Evidence Base and the Loudest Claims

> **Status: TIER 2 at best for almost everything in this file, and TIER 3 for
> most specific claims.** A small number of items reach TIER 1 and I mark them.
> **This is the least reliable major field in the notebook**, and the confidence
> with which it is discussed is inversely proportional to the quality of its
> data.

> **Not medical advice.** Nothing here is a diet plan. If you have diabetes,
> kidney disease, an eating disorder history, are pregnant, or take medications
> with dietary interactions, dietary changes are a clinical matter.

> **Safety note, stated once and meant throughout:** this file contains no
> weight-loss instructions, no calorie targets and no restrictive protocols.
> Restriction-focused health messaging is a documented contributor to disordered
> eating, and the harm from that is not hypothetical.

> **The finding: the field's central problem is that almost every claim you have
> heard rests on people remembering what they ate. Nearly everything else
> follows from that.**

## Why nutrition science is unusually bad

| Problem | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Self-reported exposure** | Food frequency questionnaires. Correlations with weighed records often 0.4–0.6†. Under-reporting scales with body weight and health-consciousness — **differential error, the worst kind** |
| **No blinding possible** | You know what you are eating. Expectancy is unblinded straight into every soft outcome |
| **Decades-long timescales** | The diseases of interest take 30–40 years. Trials run 2–5. **Every diet trial is underpowered for the question it is asked about** |
| **Adherence collapses** | Long trials see arms converge. By year 3 you are comparing intentions, not diets |
| **Everything is confounded with class** | Diet quality correlates with income, education, smoking, activity, healthcare access, and neighbourhood. Adjustment cannot fully separate these |
| **Substitution is implicit and unstated** | "Reduce saturated fat" is meaningless without saying what replaces it. The replacement does most of the causal work and is usually unspecified |
| **Massive multiplicity** | 130 foods × 40 outcomes = thousands of comparisons, almost never pre-registered |
| **Industry funding** | Documented and consequential — see below |
| **Public appetite for answers** | Journals, media and researchers all face demand for definitive statements from data that cannot support them |

**Schoenfeld & Ioannidis (2013)** selected 50 ingredients from random cookbook
recipes. **Forty had published studies linking them to cancer risk — in both
directions.** That is the field, described in one experiment.

## The dietary fat story — how a consensus went wrong, and how the revision also went wrong

**The standard narrative:** Ancel Keys pushed a fat-heart hypothesis, cherry-
picked seven of twenty-two countries, dietary guidelines demonised fat, industry
replaced fat with sugar, and obesity followed.

**Most of this is half-right, which is worse than being wrong.**

| Claim | Assessment |
|---|---|
| "Keys cherry-picked 7 of 22 countries" | **Misrepresentation.** The 22-country graph was Yerushalmy & Hilleboe (1957), critiquing Keys. The Seven Countries Study selected cohorts prospectively for contrast and data availability, with stated methodology. This talking point is repeated constantly and is not accurate |
| Industry money shaped the consensus | **True and documented.** Kearns, Schmidt & Glantz (*JAMA Intern Med*, 2016) recovered correspondence showing the **Sugar Research Foundation funded the 1967 *NEJM* review by Hegsted, McGandy and Stare** that downplayed sucrose and emphasised fat. Funding was not disclosed — disclosure was not required then |
| Negative trial data was suppressed | **True.** Ramsden et al. recovered the **Minnesota Coronary Experiment** (*BMJ*, 2016; conducted 1968–73) and the **Sydney Diet Heart Study** (*BMJ*, 2013). Replacing saturated fat with linoleic acid **lowered cholesterol as predicted and did not reduce mortality**; in the Minnesota data, older participants who lowered cholesterol most fared worse. Small, incomplete, recovered decades later — but it existed and was not published |
| Guidelines caused the obesity epidemic | **An ecological inference, not a demonstration.** The timing is suggestive; the counterfactual does not exist. Taubes and Teicholz make the strongest version of this case and it remains unproven |
| LDL causes cardiovascular disease | **TIER 1, and this survives the whole revision.** Triangulated across RCTs, Mendelian randomisation and familial hypercholesterolaemia. **Note the distinction: the saturated-fat-to-heart-disease link is much weaker than the LDL-to-heart-disease link.** Conflating them is the error on both sides of this argument |

**Where the current evidence actually sits:** replacing saturated fat with
**polyunsaturated fat** has reasonable trial support for cardiovascular
endpoints. Replacing it with **refined carbohydrate** does not. Replacing it
with nothing in particular has no evidence at all. **The substitution is the
finding.**

## Cholesterol (dietary)

**Dietary cholesterol and blood cholesterol are only weakly related in most
people.** The 2015 US Dietary Guidelines Advisory Committee dropped the 300
mg/day limit, stating cholesterol was no longer "a nutrient of concern for
overconsumption."

**The eggs-are-bad era was a mechanistic inference that did not survive
measurement.** Hepatic cholesterol synthesis downregulates in response to
dietary intake in most people; "hyper-responders" exist and are a minority.

**This is a clean example of a reversal that was quiet, complete, and never
communicated to the public.** Large numbers of people still avoid egg yolks for
a reason the guidelines abandoned over a decade ago.

## Sugar

| Claim | Tier | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Sugar-sweetened beverages are associated with weight gain and type 2 diabetes | **1–2** | Consistent across designs; SSBs are the one dietary exposure where observational and trial evidence largely agree, plausibly because liquid calories are poorly compensated for |
| Added sugar is uniquely fattening beyond its calories | **3** | Not demonstrated. Isocaloric substitution trials generally fail to find a special effect |
| Sugar causes type 2 diabetes directly | **2–3** | Adiposity mediates most of the association. The direct-effect claim is weaker than the public discourse implies |
| Sugar is "addictive" like a drug | **3, and contested** | Animal models show bingeing under intermittent access; human neuroimaging analogies are weak. Avena's work versus Westwater/Fletcher's critique. The behavioural phenomenon is real; the addiction framing is not established |
| Fructose is metabolically special at dietary doses | **2–3** | The mechanism is real at high experimental doses. At realistic intakes, evidence is much weaker than Lustig's popularisation implies |

**The honest summary: sugar-sweetened drinks have a decent case. "Sugar is
poison" does not.**

## Salt — genuinely contested, presented from both sides

**This is one of the few live scientific disagreements in the file, as opposed
to a manufactured one.**

### The case for reduction

- Sodium raises blood pressure in a dose-dependent way — **randomised,
  replicated, uncontroversial mechanism.**
- **TOHP I and II** long-term follow-up (Cook et al.): participants randomised
  to sodium reduction showed **lower cardiovascular events 10–15 years later**,
  with a roughly linear relationship. This is the strongest trial-based evidence
  in the reduction camp.
- **SSaSS** — Salt Substitute and Stroke Study (Neal et al., *NEJM*, 2021):
  **~21,000 people in 600 Chinese villages**, cluster-randomised to a
  potassium-enriched salt substitute. **Stroke rate ratio ~0.86, major
  cardiovascular events ~0.87, all-cause death ~0.88.** Hard outcomes,
  randomised, large. **The single best trial in the entire salt literature.**
- Population-level reduction programmes (UK, Finland) correlate with falling
  blood pressure and stroke mortality.

**Critical safety note on SSaSS:** potassium-based salt substitutes are
**dangerous in chronic kidney disease and with certain medications**
(potassium-sparing diuretics, ACE inhibitors, ARBs). The trial excluded
high-risk participants. **This is not a product recommendation and it is exactly
the kind of finding that harms people when reported without the exclusion
criteria.**

### The case against aggressive reduction

- **PURE** (Mente, O'Donnell, Yusuf et al.): large international cohort finding a
  **J-shaped** relationship, with increased risk below roughly 3 g sodium/day —
  i.e. at intakes that guidelines recommend.
- **The methodological objection to PURE is serious:** it estimated 24-hour
  sodium from a single fasting **spot urine** using the Kawasaki formula, which
  is known to be biased and to produce regression-dilution artefacts. Critics
  argue the J-curve is a measurement artefact plus reverse causation.
- **The counter-objection is also serious:** almost no large study uses multiple
  24-hour urine collections, so this criticism applies broadly, and physiologic
  compensation (RAAS activation at very low sodium intakes) provides a mechanism
  for genuine harm at the bottom end.
- Salt sensitivity varies substantially between individuals and by ancestry, age
  and blood pressure status. **Population-level advice may be right on average
  and wrong for many individuals.**

**My reading, held loosely:** the mechanism is not in doubt, the SSaSS result is
the best available evidence and it favours reduction (or potassium
substitution), and the extreme low end remains genuinely unresolved. **Anyone
telling you this is settled in either direction is overstating.**

## Ultra-processed food

**The best single piece of evidence in this entire file:**

**Hall et al. (2019), *Cell Metabolism*.** 20 adults, inpatient metabolic ward,
randomised crossover, two weeks per arm. Diets matched for presented calories,
energy density, macronutrients, sugar, sodium and fibre. **Ad libitum eating.**

| Arm | Result |
|---|---|
| Ultra-processed | Ate **~500 kcal/day more**; gained **~0.9 kg** over 2 weeks |
| Unprocessed | Lost **~0.9 kg** |

**Why this matters so much:** it is randomised, inpatient (so intake was
measured, not recalled), crossover (each person is their own control), and
matched on the obvious nutrients. **It is the only study in the UPF debate that
escapes the field's central methodological problem.**

**Why it is not conclusive:** n=20. Two weeks. Novelty effects. Palatability and
eating rate differed and were not controlled. The matching was imperfect (the
unprocessed diet had less rapidly-available sugar).

**The NOVA classification problem is real.** NOVA sorts by processing, not
composition, and produces awkward results — wholemeal supermarket bread and
mass-produced confectionery both land in category 4. **Critics (including
sympathetic ones) argue NOVA is not a mechanism, it is a proxy for a bundle of
mechanisms: energy density, eating rate, palatability, fibre, and satiety per
calorie.** That may be fine for advice and is bad for science.

**Observational UPF studies (NutriNet-Santé, and others) report associations
with mortality and cardiovascular disease.** They rest on food questionnaires
and are confounded with class. Treat as consistent with Hall, not as
independent confirmation.

## Protein

| Claim | Tier | Note |
|---|---|---|
| The 0.8 g/kg/day RDA is a deficiency-avoidance floor, not an optimum | **1** | This is what the figure was derived to be. It is routinely misread as a ceiling |
| Higher intakes support lean mass retention with resistance training | **1–2** | Morton et al. (2018), *BJSM*: benefits from supplementation plateau around **1.6 g/kg/day**† |
| Older adults benefit from intakes above the RDA | **2** | Anabolic resistance is well described; the outcome evidence is weaker than the mechanistic story |
| High protein damages healthy kidneys | **Not supported** | No good evidence of harm to normal kidney function. **In existing kidney disease this is entirely different and protein intake is a clinical matter** |
| Protein timing / the "anabolic window" matters much | **3, mostly refuted** | Total daily intake dominates. The window is hours, not minutes |
| Plant protein is inadequate | **Refuted** | Complementation across a day is sufficient. The "incomplete protein" framing derives from a 1971 popular book, not from evidence |

## What actually holds up

| Claim | Tier | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Energy balance determines body weight** | **1** as thermodynamics | The contested part is what *regulates* intake and expenditure, not whether the accounting holds |
| **Named diets perform similarly when adherence is matched** | **1** | **DIETFITS** (Gardner, *JAMA*, 2018): n=609, healthy low-fat vs healthy low-carb, 12 months, difference **<1 kg and not significant.** No genotype or insulin-secretion interaction. **POUNDS LOST** (Sacks, *NEJM*, 2009): n=811, four macronutrient combinations, equivalent outcomes |
| **Adherence is the active ingredient** | **1** | Follows directly from the above. The best diet is the one you can sustain |
| **Fibre intake is associated with better outcomes, with dose-response** | **2** | Reynolds et al. (*Lancet*, 2019): 25–29 g/day, observational plus some trials. One of the more robust nutritional associations |
| **Sugar-sweetened beverages are worth avoiding** | **2** | Liquid calories are poorly compensated |
| **Minimising ultra-processed intake is a reasonable heuristic** | **2** | Hall 2019 plus consistent observational data. Heuristic, not mechanism |
| **Mediterranean-style eating has the best trial evidence of any named pattern** | **2** | **PREDIMED** (2013/2018): ~30% relative reduction in major CV events. **Retracted and republished in 2018** after randomisation irregularities at some sites; the revised analysis found similar effects but the design claim is weakened |
| **Supplements do not help people without deficiencies** | **1** | See below |

## Supplements — the clearest null in the notebook

| Supplement | Trial evidence |
|---|---|
| **Beta-carotene** | **CARET and ATBC: increased lung cancer incidence and mortality in smokers.** CARET was stopped early for harm. **The most important supplement result ever obtained and the least known** |
| **Vitamin E** | Multiple large trials null; some meta-analyses associate high doses with increased all-cause mortality |
| **Vitamin D** | **VITAL** (Manson et al., *NEJM*, 2019), n=25,871: no reduction in invasive cancer or major cardiovascular events. **D-Health** (Australia): no mortality reduction. Fracture evidence also largely disappointing outside deficiency and care-home settings |
| **Multivitamins** | USPSTF (2022): insufficient evidence for CVD or cancer prevention; **recommends against beta-carotene and vitamin E for this purpose** |
| **Fish oil** | Primary prevention largely null (ASCEND, VITAL). REDUCE-IT was positive but the mineral-oil placebo may have harmed the control arm; STRENGTH, with a different comparator, was null |
| **Antioxidants generally** | Bjelakovic et al. (Cochrane): several associated with **increased** mortality |

**Deficiencies are real and treating them matters** — B12 in vegans and older
adults, iron in menstruating people with documented deficiency, folate in
pregnancy (**the one supplement with unambiguous randomised evidence: the MRC
Vitamin Study, 1991, showed folic acid substantially reduced neural tube defect
recurrence**), vitamin D in genuine deficiency, iodine where intake is low.
**These are diagnoses, not lifestyle choices, and they need testing rather than
guessing.**

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| Nutrition science broadly knows what it is doing | It is the weakest major field in the notebook, and its practitioners increasingly say so |
| Some diet is optimal for everyone | Trials find equivalence when adherence matches. **DIETFITS specifically tested personalisation by genotype and insulin response and found nothing** |
| Dietary fat makes you fat | Refuted by isocaloric trials. Fat is energy-dense, which is a different claim |
| Dietary cholesterol raises blood cholesterol much | Weakly, in most people. Guidelines dropped the limit in 2015 |
| Sugar is uniquely fattening | Not shown isocalorically. Sugar-sweetened *beverages* are a better-supported target |
| Superfoods exist | No food has demonstrated an outcome effect in isolation. The category is a marketing term |
| Detoxes remove toxins | The liver and kidneys do this. No detox product has ever identified the toxin it removes |
| Breakfast is the most important meal | **Sievert et al. (*BMJ*, 2019):** randomised trials of adding breakfast found **no weight-loss benefit and slightly higher total intake.** The original claim is traceable to cereal marketing |
| Eating frequently "stokes metabolism" | Meal frequency has minimal effect on 24-hour energy expenditure at matched intake |
| You must drink eight glasses of water a day | No credible origin. Thirst is a reasonable regulator in healthy adults; requirements vary enormously |
| Organic food is more nutritious | Nutrient differences are small and inconsistent. Pesticide residue differences are real; the health consequence at typical exposures is not established |
| Gluten harms most people | Coeliac disease (~1%) and wheat allergy are real. **Non-coeliac gluten sensitivity largely failed blinded re-challenge trials**, with FODMAPs and nocebo explaining much of it |
| Red meat causes cancer, definitively | IARC classified processed meat Group 1 on **hazard**. The absolute effect is roughly one extra colorectal case per ~111 people at 50 g/day. **NutriRECS (Johnston et al., *Annals*, 2019)** assessed the evidence as low-certainty and recommended no change — triggering enormous backlash. **Both the recommendation and the backlash were about evidence standards, not about meat** |
| "Clean eating" is a health concept | It is a moral one. Orthorexic patterns are a documented harm of this framing |

## Open questions

- [ ] **Can nutritional epidemiology be fixed, or must it be replaced by trials and objective biomarkers?** Ioannidis versus the field. **The most consequential methodological dispute in this notebook.**
- [ ] Does the Hall UPF result replicate at larger n and longer duration? Follow-up work is ongoing and this is the highest-value pending result in the file.
- [ ] Is NOVA a real construct or a proxy bundle? If a proxy, which component does the work — energy density, eating rate, fibre, or palatability?
- [ ] Where does the sodium curve actually bottom out? PURE versus TOHP versus SSaSS. **Genuinely unresolved.**
- [ ] Is there any individual-level dietary personalisation that works? Zoe/PREDICT-style glycaemic-response personalisation is the leading candidate and has commercial incentives attached to its own evaluation.
- [ ] Does the timing of eating matter independently of what and how much? Time-restricted eating trials have been mostly null for weight once intake is matched, but circadian mechanisms are plausible.
- [ ] What replaced saturated fat in the actual food supply after the guidelines, and what did that do? **The most important unanswered historical question in the diet-heart debate.**
- [ ] How much of the observed diet–health association is diet, and how much is socioeconomic position wearing a diet costume?

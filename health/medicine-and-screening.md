## What medicine does extremely well

**The sceptical material below is worth nothing without this section, and it is
routinely omitted from popular criticism.**

| Domain | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Acute trauma and surgery** | Survival from major trauma has transformed. Appendicitis, obstruction, fracture fixation, caesarean section |
| **Infection** | Antibiotics, antiretrovirals — HIV converted from a death sentence to a managed condition within twenty years |
| **Obstetrics and neonatal care** | Maternal and infant mortality reductions are among the largest health gains ever achieved |
| **Acute cardiovascular care** | Primary PCI in ST-elevation MI, thrombolysis and thrombectomy in stroke. **Note this contrasts sharply with stenting for stable angina, which failed — see the-control-group.md** |
| **Anaesthesia** | Perioperative mortality has fallen by orders of magnitude |
| **Vaccination and public health** | The largest mortality reductions in history |
| **Several cancers** | Testicular cancer, childhood leukaemia, Hodgkin lymphoma, CML with imatinib. Real cures, not statistical artefacts |
| **Transplantation, dialysis, insulin, thyroid replacement** | Conditions converted from fatal to chronic |

**And the scale caveat:** medical care is estimated to account for perhaps
**10–20% of variance in population health outcomes**†; behaviour, environment,
income and education dominate (McGinnis, Williams-Russo & Knickman, *Health
Affairs*, 2002). **Medicine is extraordinarily good at the acute and quite
limited against the social determinants of chronic disease.**

## The three biases that make screening look better than it is

### 1. Lead-time bias

**Screening moves the diagnosis date earlier. It does not necessarily move the
death date later.**

Diagnosed at 67 by symptoms, dies at 70: **3-year survival**.
Screened at 60, same disease, same death at 70: **10-year survival**.

**Survival tripled. The patient gained nothing and lost seven years of knowing.**

### 2. Length bias

**Screening preferentially detects slow-growing disease**, because slow disease
spends longer in the detectable-but-asymptomatic window. Fast, aggressive
disease appears and kills between screening rounds.

**So the screen-detected population is enriched for indolent cancers with good
prognoses regardless of treatment**, and screening looks effective because of
who it catches, not what it does.

### 3. Overdiagnosis — the extreme of length bias

**Detection of disease that would never have caused symptoms or death.** These
patients are diagnosed, treated, and counted as cured — of something that was
never going to hurt them. **They cannot benefit, and they absorb the full harms
of treatment.**

**The cleanest demonstration in medicine: South Korea and thyroid cancer.**

| | |
|---|---|
| From 1999 | Thyroid ultrasound offered opportunistically alongside a national screening programme |
| By 2011 | **Thyroid cancer incidence rose roughly fifteen-fold** — it became the most commonly diagnosed cancer in the country |
| Mortality | **Essentially flat** |
| Consequence | Tens of thousands of thyroidectomies, with lifelong hormone replacement, and complications including hypoparathyroidism and vocal cord paralysis |
| Source | Ahn, Kim & Welch, *NEJM*, 2014 |

**A fifteen-fold increase in a disease, with no change in deaths, is not an
epidemic. It is a measurement artefact with surgical consequences.**

A parallel case: **neuroblastoma screening of infants in Japan and Germany**
doubled incidence with **no mortality reduction.** Japan discontinued the
programme in 2004.

## Why 5-year survival is a corrupted metric

**Welch, Schwartz & Woloshin (*JAMA*, 2000)** compared changes in 5-year
survival with changes in mortality across **20 tumour types** in the US.

**The correlation between improvement in 5-year survival and reduction in
mortality was essentially absent.**

$$\text{5-year survival} \uparrow \;\;\not\Rightarrow\;\; \text{Deaths} \downarrow$$

**All three biases above inflate survival without saving anyone.** Mortality per
100,000 population does not have this problem — it counts deaths in everyone,
diagnosed or not.

**Practical consequence:** international comparisons of cancer "survival rates"
are substantially comparisons of **diagnostic intensity**. A country that
screens more aggressively will report better survival and may have identical
mortality. **This has been used repeatedly in health-system political arguments
in both directions, and it is a statistical artefact in both.**

## Specific programmes — the honest state of play

### Mammography

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Cochrane (Gøtzsche & Jørgensen, 2013)** | ~15% relative breast-cancer mortality reduction; ~30% overdiagnosis. Their summary: for **2,000 women screened over 10 years, 1 death avoided, 10 healthy women treated unnecessarily**, and ~200 experience significant false-alarm distress |
| **Canadian National Breast Screening Study (Miller et al., *BMJ*, 2014)** | 89,835 women, 25-year follow-up: **no breast-cancer mortality reduction**; 22% of screen-detected cancers overdiagnosed |
| **Criticism of the Canadian trial** | Serious — allegations of compromised randomisation and substandard mammographic technique. The trial is disputed rather than dismissed |
| **Current guidelines** | Moved in different directions in different countries; USPSTF (2024) recommends biennial screening from 40 |

**This is genuinely contested by competent people, and both a pro- and
anti-screening reader can find honest support.** The area of agreement:
**overdiagnosis is real and non-trivial, and the harms are not zero.**

### PSA for prostate cancer

| Trial | Result |
|---|---|
| **PLCO (US)** | No mortality benefit — **but roughly 90% of the control arm underwent PSA testing anyway.** The trial largely compared screening to screening |
| **ERSPC (Europe)** | ~20% relative reduction in prostate-cancer mortality; **NNS around 781 men invited and 27 diagnosed to prevent one death at 13 years**† |
| **Overdiagnosis** | Substantial. Many detected cancers would never have caused symptoms |
| **Harms** | Biopsy complications; incontinence and erectile dysfunction after treatment are common and permanent |

**Richard Ablin, who discovered PSA in 1970, publicly criticised its use as a
population screening test**, calling the resulting programme a "profit-driven
public health disaster." That is one man's polemic, not a finding — but it is
the discoverer's.

**The modern resolution is partial:** active surveillance rather than immediate
treatment for low-risk disease, and MRI before biopsy, both of which reduce
overtreatment without abandoning detection. **This is the right shape of
solution: change what happens after the positive test, rather than arguing about
the test.**

### Colonoscopy

**NordICC (Bretthauer et al., *NEJM*, 2022)** — the first large randomised trial
of colonoscopy invitation:

- **Intention-to-screen:** ~18% reduction in colorectal cancer incidence, **no
  statistically significant reduction in colorectal cancer mortality at 10
  years.**
- Only ~42% of those invited actually attended.
- **Per-protocol (adjusted) estimates were considerably more favourable** and
  are, by construction, less reliable.

**The result was widely reported as "colonoscopy doesn't work" and widely
defended as "attendance was low and follow-up short."** Both readings are
partial. It remains the best randomised evidence available and it is less
impressive than the observational literature promised — **which is the recurring
pattern of this entire notebook.**

### Lung cancer screening — a success

**NLST (2011):** low-dose CT versus chest X-ray in heavy smokers — **~20%
relative reduction in lung cancer mortality and a ~6.7% reduction in all-cause
mortality.** **NELSON** (Netherlands/Belgium) broadly confirmed it.

**Why this one works when others don't:** a genuinely high-risk target
population, a lethal disease with a short indolent phase (so less length bias),
and an effective intervention for early-stage disease. **The conditions for
screening to succeed are specific, and most programmes do not meet them.**

### The broader picture

**Saquib, Saquib & Ioannidis (*BMJ*, 2015)** examined randomised evidence for
**39 screening tests across 19 diseases**: disease-specific mortality reductions
were found for a minority, and **reductions in all-cause mortality were
essentially absent.**

**The charitable reading:** screening trials are almost never powered for
all-cause mortality, so absence of evidence is expected. **The uncharitable
reading:** if a screening programme genuinely saved lives at the claimed
magnitude, it should eventually show up in total deaths. **Both readings are
defensible and the question is open.**

## Treating a disease versus treating a risk factor

**A distinction that explains most disagreements about medicalisation.**

| | Treating disease | Treating a risk factor |
|---|---|---|
| Patient status | Symptomatic, unwell | **Well, by definition** |
| Benefit | Often large, often immediate | Probabilistic, deferred, invisible |
| Harm | Accepted against present suffering | **Paid immediately by someone who felt fine** |
| Typical NNT | Low | **High** |
| Example | Antibiotics for pneumonia | Statin in low-risk primary prevention |
| Failure mode | Under-treatment | **Over-treatment, and the manufacture of patients** |

**Diagnostic threshold creep is a real and under-scrutinised mechanism.**
Definitions of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, osteoporosis and obesity
have all been widened over time. Each widening converts healthy people into
patients overnight. **Some widenings were evidence-driven; guideline panels have
also repeatedly been found to contain members with industry ties.** Both facts
are true and neither settles any individual case.

**"Pre-disease" categories** — prediabetes, prehypertension, osteopenia — are
the clearest instance. Many people so labelled will never progress. The label
has psychological, insurance and treatment consequences regardless.

## Antibiotic resistance

| Point | Detail |
|---|---|
| **The mechanism is not in doubt** | Selection pressure. This is evolution operating on a timescale we can watch |
| **The scale** | The **GRAM study** (*Lancet*, 2022) estimated **1.27 million deaths directly attributable** to bacterial AMR in 2019, and ~4.95 million associated |
| **The famous projection is weaker than the citation implies** | The O'Neill Review (2016) figure of **10 million deaths/year by 2050** is a linear extrapolation with very wide uncertainty, widely criticised, and **cited far more often than its methods section warrants** |
| **Where the antibiotics go** | A very large share of global antibiotic use is agricultural. Clinical stewardship is necessary and not sufficient |
| **The pipeline problem** | Antibiotics are economically unattractive: short courses, curative, and the best new drug should be **used as little as possible.** The market structure actively punishes the product we need |
| **What individuals can do** | Complete the diagnostic process rather than demanding antibiotics for viral illness. **Do not stop a prescribed course on your own judgement** — course-length questions are an active clinical debate and not a patient decision |

## Iatrogenic harm — real, and badly measured

| Estimate | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **IOM, *To Err Is Human* (1999)**: 44,000–98,000 US deaths/year from medical error | Landmark, transformative, and **methodologically criticised** — extrapolated from chart reviews with subjective attribution |
| **Makary & Daniel (*BMJ*, 2016)**: ~250,000 US deaths/year, "third leading cause of death" | **Heavily criticised.** Extrapolated from small studies with severe selection, attributing deaths in very sick patients to error. **The figure is widely repeated and poorly supported** |
| **The defensible core** | Preventable harm in hospitals is common and consequential. **Precise magnitude is unknown, and confident large numbers should be treated with suspicion in both directions** |

**The counterfactual problem is fundamental:** deciding whether a death in a
critically ill patient was "caused by" an error requires a judgement about what
would have happened otherwise, and reviewers disagree with each other
substantially.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| Catching it early is always better | Only if early treatment changes the outcome. For overdiagnosed disease, early detection is pure harm |
| More screening is better healthcare | Screening has harms — overdiagnosis, false positives, biopsy complications, anxiety, and treatment injury in people who were never at risk |
| 5-year survival measures how good the care is | It measures diagnostic intensity as much as anything. **Welch (2000) found no correlation with mortality across 20 cancers** |
| Country A has better cancer care because survival is higher | Frequently a screening-intensity artefact. Compare mortality per population instead |
| A cancer diagnosis means a life at risk | Not for the overdiagnosed fraction, which in some cancers is very large. **We currently cannot reliably tell which is which, and that is the real problem** |
| Doctors know the numbers | Gigerenzer's studies repeatedly find clinicians misinterpreting positive predictive value and relative risk. **Not a competence failure — a statistics-education failure** |
| Guidelines are neutral distillations of evidence | Often good; panels have documented industry ties, and threshold changes have expanded treated populations enormously |
| "Prediabetes" is a disease | It is a risk category. Many labelled with it never progress |
| A negative test means you're fine | It means the test was negative. Sensitivity is never 100%, and interval cancers are the reason screening's benefit is smaller than the detection rate suggests |
| Medical error is the third leading cause of death | The 250,000 figure is a poorly-supported extrapolation. Preventable harm is real; that number is not a measurement |
| Medicine is why we live longer | Sanitation, nutrition, housing, income and vaccination did most of the historical work. **Medicine's contribution is real, later, and smaller than assumed** |

## Open questions

- [ ] **Can overdiagnosed cancers be distinguished from lethal ones at diagnosis?** Molecular classification is the leading hope. **Solving this would resolve most of the screening debate at once.**
- [ ] What is the true net effect of mammography with modern imaging and modern treatment? The trials are decades old and both technologies have changed.
- [ ] Does NordICC look different at 15 years, and does attendance explain the null? The follow-up is pending.
- [ ] Why do so few screening programmes show all-cause mortality benefit? Power, or the absence of a real effect? **The most important methodological question in screening.**
- [ ] How much of guideline threshold creep is evidence-driven and how much is expansion of a market? Nobody has done the systematic accounting.
- [ ] What is the actual rate of preventable in-hospital death? Existing estimates span an order of magnitude and use incompatible methods.
- [ ] Can the antibiotic market be restructured (subscription/pull incentives) fast enough? The UK and US have pilots and no adequate evaluation.
- [ ] Does communicating overdiagnosis risk change patient decisions, or just reduce uptake indiscriminately? **The ethics of informed screening choice depend on this and it is barely studied.**

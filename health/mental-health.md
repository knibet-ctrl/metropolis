## What the evidence supports

| Intervention | Condition | Tier | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CBT** | Depression, anxiety disorders, PTSD, OCD, insomnia | **1** | Largest evidence base of any psychotherapy. Effect sizes inflated by publication bias but survive correction |
| **Behavioural activation** | Depression | **1** | **COBRA** (Richards et al., *Lancet*, 2016): non-inferior to CBT, delivered by less-specialised staff at lower cost. Under-used |
| **Exposure-based therapies** | Anxiety, phobia, PTSD, OCD | **1** | Among the strongest effects in clinical psychology |
| **Antidepressants** | Moderate to severe depression | **1** for superiority over placebo; **2** for magnitude | Cipriani et al. (*Lancet*, 2018): 522 trials, 116,477 participants, **all 21 drugs superior to placebo**, ORs ~1.37–2.13 |
| **Interpersonal therapy, psychodynamic therapy (short-term)** | Depression | **2** | Evidence weaker than CBT largely because fewer trials, not because of demonstrated inferiority |
| **Exercise** | Depression, anxiety | **2** | Effects shrink substantially in low-risk-of-bias trials. See below |
| **Sleep treatment (CBT-I)** | Depression comorbid with insomnia | **2** | Improving sleep improves mood; the causal chain is plausible and partly randomised |
| **Social connection** | Everything | **2** | Large observational effects; **loneliness intervention trials have been mostly disappointing**, which matters |
| **Mindfulness-based programmes** | Depression relapse prevention, anxiety | **2** | Goyal et al. (*JAMA Intern Med*, 2014): moderate evidence versus non-specific controls, **no evidence of superiority to active controls** |
| **Ketamine / esketamine, psilocybin** | Treatment-resistant depression | **2–3** | Real short-term signals; **blinding is essentially impossible** (functional unblinding), long-term and safety data thin |

## The efficacy debate, presented honestly

### Antidepressants: what is actually contested

**Not contested:** that antidepressants separate from placebo in randomised
trials. Cipriani's network meta-analysis is the largest synthesis ever
performed and every drug beat placebo.

**Contested:** whether the average separation is clinically meaningful.

| Position | Argument |
|---|---|
| **Kirsch and colleagues** | The mean drug–placebo difference on the Hamilton scale is around **2 points** — below NICE's former 3-point "clinical significance" threshold. Most of the response is placebo. Separation increases with baseline severity, suggesting the effect is confined to the severely ill |
| **The rebuttal** | The 3-point threshold was **arbitrary and NICE later abandoned it.** Averages conceal heterogeneity: a mixture of strong responders and non-responders produces a small mean. Response-rate analyses (rather than mean change) show larger differences. And symptom-scale points are not the outcome patients care about |
| **Both sides agree on** | **Turner et al. (*NEJM*, 2008)**, which compared FDA registration data to publication: **of 74 trials, 38 were positive and 37 of those were published; of 36 negative or questionable trials, 22 were unpublished and 11 were published as positive.** Effect sizes in the published literature were inflated by roughly one third |

**Turner (2008) is the most important paper in this section.** It establishes
that the published literature overstated the effect, and — crucially — that
**even the complete FDA dataset still showed superiority over placebo.**

### Psychotherapy: the same problem

**Cuijpers and colleagues** have repeatedly shown that psychotherapy trials for
depression suffer from publication bias and small-study effects of similar
magnitude. Adjusting for them reduces the pooled effect size for depression
from roughly **0.67 to around 0.42**† — still a real effect, substantially
smaller than advertised.

**A design problem specific to therapy trials:** the common comparator is a
**waitlist control**, which is not a neutral condition. Being told you will be
treated in three months may actively suppress spontaneous improvement. Trials
against active controls show smaller effects. **Anyone comparing "therapy works
better than drugs" or vice versa across trials with different comparator types
is comparing nothing.**

### Exercise for depression

Widely promoted, and the honest picture is messier than the promotion:

- **Cochrane (2013):** pooled SMD around **−0.62**; restricted to trials with
  adequate allocation concealment, blinded outcome assessment and
  intention-to-treat analysis, it fell to about **−0.18**† — small and of
  uncertain clinical relevance.
- **Noetel et al. (*BMJ*, 2024)** network meta-analysis reported larger effects
  across modalities, while noting **most included trials were at high risk of
  bias.**
- Blinding is impossible. Expectancy, therapist contact, and structured activity
  are all confounded with the exercise itself.

**My reading: exercise probably helps mood, the effect is smaller than the
headlines, and it is not a substitute for treatment in moderate to severe
illness.** Recommending exercise to someone with severe depression as though it
were sufficient is a common and harmful error — the condition itself removes the
capacity to do it, which converts advice into another failure.

## The serotonin controversy — both sides, at length

**This is the most misreported story in recent mental health coverage, and the
misreporting is dangerous.**

### The paper

**Moncrieff, Cooper, Stockmann, Amendola, Hengartner & Horowitz (2022),
*Molecular Psychiatry*** — an umbrella review of systematic reviews and
meta-analyses across six areas of serotonin research (serotonin metabolites,
receptor binding, transporter levels, tryptophan depletion, SERT gene studies,
and gene–environment interactions).

**Conclusion:** "no consistent evidence of an association between serotonin and
depression, and no support for the hypothesis that depression is caused by
lowered serotonin activity or concentrations."

### The rebuttals

**Jauhar, Cowen, Nutt and others responded, and their objections are
substantive:**

| Objection | Detail |
|---|---|
| **The target was a straw man** | The "chemical imbalance" model was a **marketing simplification, not the professional consensus.** Academic psychiatry abandoned simple monoamine-deficiency theories decades ago. Critics reply, fairly, that clinicians and public health materials repeated it anyway |
| **Umbrella reviews inherit their inputs' flaws** | Aggregating heterogeneous, small, old literatures and reporting an absence of consistency is close to guaranteed by the method |
| **Specific evidence was mischaracterised** | Notably tryptophan depletion, which does reliably lower mood in **recovered** depressed patients and those with family history — arguably the strongest serotonin-relevant experimental evidence |
| **PET receptor findings were disputed** | Interpretation of transporter and receptor binding studies is contested on technical grounds |
| **The inference does not follow** | **The core objection.** Even if serotonin is not the cause, that says nothing about whether serotonergic drugs work |

### The point that must not be lost

**Mechanism and efficacy are independent questions.**

- Aspirin worked for decades before prostaglandin inhibition was understood.
- Lithium works and its mechanism remains unclear.
- General anaesthetics work and their mechanism is not settled.
- **Efficacy is established by randomised controlled trials, not by mechanistic
  stories.** Cipriani (2018) is unaffected by anything in the Moncrieff paper.

**Much of the press coverage collapsed "the serotonin theory is unsupported"
into "antidepressants don't work."** Those are different claims and only the
first was argued. **People stopped medication on the basis of the second.**

**What the controversy genuinely establishes:** that a simplified causal story
was communicated to patients for decades with more confidence than the evidence
supported, and that this was an institutional failure of honesty. That is a real
and serious finding. It is not the finding that got reported.

## Withdrawal and discontinuation — a real issue, badly handled by everyone

| Position | Claim |
|---|---|
| **Historically minimised** | Guidelines long described discontinuation symptoms as mild and self-limiting over 1–2 weeks. **This was under-evidenced and, for many patients, wrong** |
| **Davies & Read (2019)** | Systematic review reporting roughly **56% incidence and 46% describing symptoms as severe**† |
| **Criticism of that figure** | Several included surveys recruited from online withdrawal support communities — a maximally selected sample. The true population incidence is very likely lower |
| **Horowitz & Taylor (*Lancet Psychiatry*, 2019)** | Argued for **hyperbolic tapering** based on receptor-occupancy pharmacology: linear dose reductions produce non-linear occupancy changes, so the last small doses matter most. Influential; guidelines have moved |
| **Current position** | Withdrawal is real, varies enormously between drugs and people, and **requires a supervised plan.** UK guidance (NICE, RCPsych) has been revised in this direction |

**This is exactly the topic where a research notebook can cause harm.** The
correct action on reading it is to discuss tapering with a prescriber, not to
stop.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Chemical imbalance" is the scientific theory of depression | It was a simplification popularised partly by pharmaceutical marketing. Academic psychiatry moved on long ago and **should have said so publicly much sooner** |
| The serotonin review showed antidepressants don't work | **It made no such claim and could not.** Mechanism ≠ efficacy. Cipriani (2018) stands |
| Antidepressants are placebos | They separate from placebo in the largest analyses of the complete regulatory dataset. **The honest criticism is about magnitude, not existence** |
| Therapy is uniformly effective | Effect sizes are real and inflated by publication bias and waitlist controls. Roughly a third to a half of patients do not respond adequately to first-line treatment |
| Talking therapy has no side effects | Adverse effects occur in perhaps 3–10% of psychotherapy patients† and are systematically under-measured. Some trauma-focused work transiently worsens symptoms by design |
| Depression is one disease | It is a syndrome defined by a symptom checklist. Two people can meet criteria sharing almost no symptoms. **This heterogeneity is a strong candidate explanation for small average effect sizes** |
| Exercise is as good as medication | Not established. Effects shrink in high-quality trials, and severe depression removes the capacity to exercise |
| Mindfulness is universally safe | Adverse experiences are documented and under-reported (Farias, Van Dam et al., 2018). Not appropriate for everyone, particularly some trauma presentations |
| Antidepressants cause suicide | The 2004 FDA black-box warning reflects a real signal for **increased suicidal ideation in under-25s.** Completed suicides did not increase in the trial data, and **prescribing fell after the warning while youth suicide rates rose** — the net effect of the warning itself is debated and may have been harmful |
| Psychedelics are a breakthrough treatment | Early results are genuinely interesting. **Blinding fails almost completely** — participants know — and expectancy in a highly hyped field is exactly the condition that produces later disappointment |
| Mental illness is caused by low resilience or poor choices | Heritability estimates for major depression are typically ~30–40% and for bipolar and schizophrenia substantially higher. **This is not a character question** |
| More mental health awareness is straightforwardly good | Contested. The **"prevalence inflation" hypothesis** (Foulkes & Andrews, 2023) proposes that broad awareness campaigns may lead some people to interpret ordinary distress as disorder, potentially worsening outcomes. **TIER 3, speculative, and taken seriously by serious people** |

## Open questions

- [ ] **Why are average treatment effects small when clinical experience reports strong individual responses?** Diagnostic heterogeneity is the leading hypothesis and remains unproven. **The most important question in the field.**
- [ ] Can depression be carved into biologically distinct subtypes that predict treatment response? Decades of attempts; no reliable biomarker exists.
- [ ] What is the true population incidence and severity distribution of antidepressant withdrawal? Existing estimates come from selected samples in both directions.
- [ ] Does hyperbolic tapering reduce withdrawal severity in randomised comparison? The pharmacological argument is strong; the trial evidence is thin.
- [ ] Do psychedelic effects survive adequate control for expectancy? **Nobody has solved the blinding problem** and until someone does, effect sizes should be discounted heavily.
- [ ] Is the rise in reported youth mental illness a real increase, better detection, changed thresholds, or prevalence inflation? **Genuinely unresolved and politically charged in both directions.**
- [ ] Does social media cause adolescent mental illness? Haidt argues yes; Odgers, Przybylski and others argue the effect sizes are tiny and the natural experiments are weak. **Contested, high-stakes, and the data does not currently settle it.**
- [ ] Why has psychiatry produced no genuinely novel mechanism-of-action antidepressant class for decades, and does that reflect the science or the economics of drug development?

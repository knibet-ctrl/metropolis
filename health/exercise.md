# Exercise — The Best-Evidenced Intervention, and What Is Oversold About It

> **Status: TIER 1** that physical activity is associated with substantially
> lower all-cause mortality and improves a wide range of randomised intermediate
> outcomes. **TIER 2** on the causal magnitude for mortality — no RCT is powered
> for it and none will be. **TIER 3** for essentially every specific protocol.

> **Not medical advice.** If you have cardiac disease, uncontrolled
> hypertension, are pregnant, are recovering from injury or surgery, or are
> significantly deconditioned, exercise prescription is a clinical matter. Talk
> to a clinician first.

> **The core finding: the dose-response curve is steep at the bottom and flat at
> the top. Almost everything the industry sells concerns the flat part.**

---

## What the evidence supports, by strength

| Claim | Tier | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Physical activity is associated with 20–35% lower all-cause mortality at guideline volumes | **1** | Dozens of cohorts, millions of participants, consistent dose-response |
| Cardiorespiratory fitness is among the strongest mortality predictors measurable | **1** (as association) | Mandsager et al. (2018), n=122,007 treadmill tests; Blair's Aerobics Center Longitudinal Study |
| Exercise improves blood pressure, insulin sensitivity, lipids, VO2max, strength, bone density, and mood | **1** | Hundreds of RCTs on intermediate outcomes |
| Resistance training reduces fall and fracture risk in older adults | **1** | RCTs of progressive resistance and balance training |
| Exercise improves function and reduces symptoms in chronic conditions (heart failure, COPD, osteoarthritis, depression) | **1–2** | Cardiac and pulmonary rehab RCTs are unusually strong |
| Increasing activity *causes* lower mortality | **2** | Plausible, triangulated, never randomised on mortality |
| Any particular modality, split, or protocol is superior | **3** | Head-to-head trials mostly find equivalence when work is matched |
| Exercise is an effective weight-loss strategy | **Largely refuted** | Modest effect alone; compensation is substantial. See below |

## The dose-response curve — the central finding

**Arem et al. (2015), *JAMA Internal Medicine*,** pooled six cohorts (~660,000
adults):

| Activity relative to guideline minimum (150 min/wk moderate) | Approximate mortality reduction vs inactive |
|---|---|
| None | reference |
| Less than half the minimum | **~20%** |
| 1–2× minimum | **~31%** |
| 2–3× minimum | ~37% |
| 3–5× minimum | **~39%** |
| 5–10× minimum | ~39% — **no further gain, no harm** |

**Read the first two rows again.** Doing *less than the recommended minimum* —
roughly a brisk 10-minute walk most days — captures **about two-thirds of the
total available association.** Tripling from there adds about 8 percentage
points.

$$\frac{\text{benefit from 0} \to \text{75 min/wk}}{\text{benefit from 0} \to \text{750 min/wk}} \approx \frac{20}{39} \approx 51\%$$

**The marginal value of exercise is highest for the person doing none, and
approaches zero for the person already training hard.** Almost all exercise
content is produced for and consumed by people on the flat part of the curve.

**Steps data agrees.** Paluch et al. (2022), *Lancet Public Health*, harmonised
meta-analysis: mortality benefit plateaus around **6,000–8,000 steps/day for
adults over 60** and **8,000–10,000 for under 60.** The famous 10,000 figure has
no scientific origin — it comes from the name of a 1965 Japanese pedometer,
*manpo-kei* ("ten-thousand step meter"), chosen because the character 万 looks
like a walking person.

## VO2max and cardiorespiratory fitness

**Mandsager et al. (2018), *JAMA Network Open*:** 122,007 patients undergoing
treadmill testing at the Cleveland Clinic, median follow-up 8.4 years.

| Fitness category | Adjusted mortality hazard ratio vs low fitness |
|---|---|
| Below average | ~0.61† |
| Above average | ~0.41† |
| High | ~0.30† |
| **Elite** | **~0.20**† |

The authors reported **no observed upper limit of benefit** and that the
mortality risk associated with low fitness exceeded that associated with
smoking, diabetes and coronary artery disease in this sample.

**Now the caveats, because this study is quoted constantly without them:**

1. **It is observational.** Fitness is measured, not assigned.
2. **Reverse causation is severe.** People with undiagnosed disease perform
   badly on a treadmill. That is what treadmill tests are for.
3. **The sample is patients referred for stress testing** — not the general
   population.
4. **VO2max is substantially heritable**, with trainability itself heritable
   (the HERITAGE Family Study found large individual variation in response to an
   identical 20-week programme, including non-responders). **Being fit and
   getting fitter are not the same variable.**
5. Nobody has randomised people to elite fitness. The training required is
   confounded with everything else about the people who can sustain it.

**The honest statement:** VO2max is an excellent *prognostic marker*. Whether
raising your VO2max by training moves your risk to match the people who already
have that VO2max is **not established** — and the parallel with HDL cholesterol,
where exactly this inference failed catastrophically, should be kept in mind.

## Aerobic versus resistance training

**They are not substitutes, and the resistance evidence is under-rated.**

**Momma et al. (2022), *British Journal of Sports Medicine*,** systematic review
and meta-analysis of muscle-strengthening activity:

- **30–60 minutes per week** of muscle-strengthening activity associated with
  approximately **10–20% lower all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease,
  total cancer, diabetes and lung cancer.**
- **J-shaped:** the association attenuated and reversed direction beyond roughly
  **130–140 minutes per week.†** Interpret cautiously — this is sparse data at
  the extremes and confounded by who trains that much.
- **The combination of aerobic and strengthening activity outperformed either
  alone.**

**Thirty to sixty minutes a week is two short sessions.** That is a strikingly
low dose for a strikingly large association, and it is the finding most likely
to be news to a general reader.

| | Aerobic | Resistance |
|---|---|---|
| Strongest evidence for | Cardiovascular outcomes, VO2max, mortality association | Strength, muscle mass, bone density, fall prevention, glucose disposal, functional independence |
| Under-recognised for | — | **All-cause mortality; independence in old age; the single best-evidenced fall-prevention component** |
| Where it is oversold | HIIT-specific claims | Hormonal and "metabolic afterburn" claims |
| Who neglects it | People who lift | **Almost everyone over 50, and women disproportionately** |

**The strongest practical case for resistance training is not longevity. It is
that sarcopenia — age-related muscle loss — is a principal mechanism by which
old people lose independence, and it is one of the few age-related processes
with a demonstrated, cheap, non-pharmacological countermeasure.** Grip strength
and gait speed are among the best functional predictors of outcomes in older
adults, and both are trainable.

## Sedentary behaviour — separable from exercise

**Ekelund et al. (2016), *The Lancet*,** harmonised meta-analysis of >1 million
adults:

- High sitting time (>8h/day) was associated with increased mortality — **but
  the association was largely eliminated in those achieving roughly 60–75
  minutes/day of moderate activity.**
- **High television time remained associated with mortality even in the active
  group**, which is either evidence that TV specifically matters or, more
  plausibly, that TV time is a marker for something else (socioeconomic
  position, snacking, isolation).

**The honest reading:** "sitting is the new smoking" is a slogan, not a finding —
the effect sizes are not remotely comparable and the confounding is severe.
"Prolonged sitting is bad and exercise substantially offsets it" is closer, and
even that is observational.

**The "breaking up sitting" literature** (short walking breaks every 30 minutes)
has genuine randomised evidence for **postprandial glucose and insulin
responses**. It has no hard-outcome evidence. TIER 2 on the mechanism, TIER 3 on
whether it matters for anything you care about.

## Exercise and weight — the honest version

**Exercise is an excellent health intervention and a poor weight-loss
intervention.** These are both true and stating only one of them is how the
field misleads people.

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| Exercise alone produces modest weight loss | Typically 1–3 kg in trials of a year or less†, far below what the energy arithmetic predicts |
| Compensation is the reason | Increased intake, decreased non-exercise movement, and possibly metabolic adaptation |
| **Constrained total energy expenditure** | Pontzer's work on the Hadza found total daily energy expenditure similar to sedentary Westerners despite far higher activity. **TIER 2 and genuinely contested**, but it explains the trial results better than the naive model does |
| Exercise is strongly associated with weight *maintenance* | National Weight Control Registry — observational, self-selected, and often over-read |
| Exercise preserves lean mass during weight loss | This is well supported and matters more than the scale figure |

**The practical implication is not "don't exercise."** It is that the health
benefits of exercise are largely independent of whether you lose weight, that
fitness improves outcomes at any body size, and that **treating exercise as
calorie-burning sets people up to quit when the scale doesn't move.**

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| 10,000 steps is a scientific target | It is the marketing name of a 1965 Japanese pedometer. Mortality benefit plateaus around 6,000–8,000 for older adults |
| More exercise is always better | The curve flattens above roughly 2–3× the guideline minimum. Beyond that you are training for performance, not longevity |
| The "fat-burning zone" burns more fat | At low intensity a higher *proportion* of energy comes from fat, but total energy expenditure is lower and 24-hour substrate use equalises. **For fat loss, total energy deficit dominates and the zone is irrelevant** |
| HIIT is dramatically superior | It is time-efficient and produces comparable VO2max gains per unit of work. Head-to-head trials with matched work generally find equivalence. Adherence, not physiology, is the differentiator |
| You need a specific programme | Trials comparing protocols overwhelmingly find that adherence beats programming. **The best programme is the one you will still be doing in a year** |
| Cardio kills your gains | Interference effects exist and are small at recreational volumes |
| Stretching prevents injury | Static stretching before exercise has poor evidence for injury prevention and can transiently reduce force output. Warm-up has better evidence than stretching does |
| Lactic acid causes soreness | Lactate clears within an hour. DOMS is mechanical damage and inflammation. **This has been known since the 1980s and is still repeated** |
| You must exercise fasted / at a particular time | Effects on fat oxidation are real, small, and not shown to affect body composition outcomes |
| Exercise is how you lose weight | It is how you improve health at whatever weight you are. The weight-loss effect is disappointing and consistently so |
| Sitting is the new smoking | Smoking costs about ten years. Sitting's association is much smaller, heavily confounded, and largely offset by activity |
| Older people should be careful about lifting | **Backwards.** Progressive resistance training is one of the best-evidenced interventions in geriatrics. The risk of not training exceeds the risk of training, under supervision |
| Runner's high is endorphins | Endorphins are too large to cross the blood-brain barrier in relevant quantity. The endocannabinoid explanation has better support. **A minor point that illustrates how long a plausible mechanism survives without evidence** |

## What genuinely remains uncertain

- **Whether raising fitness moves risk to match the already-fit.** The HDL
  precedent argues for caution.
- **The upper end of the curve.** Very high endurance volumes are associated
  with coronary artery calcification and atrial fibrillation in some cohorts.
  Whether this translates to harm is unresolved; the mortality data does not
  show it.
- **Individual response variation.** HERITAGE found large differences in
  trainability. **Non-responders to a given stimulus exist**, and the popular
  framing of universal response is wrong.
- **Whether exercise "counts" if it's occupational.** Some cohorts find
  occupational physical activity associated with *worse* outcomes — the
  "physical activity paradox." Confounding by socioeconomic position is the
  obvious explanation and has not been fully excluded.

## Open questions

- [ ] **Is the fitness–mortality association causal at the magnitude observed?** The most important unanswerable question in this file. Requires an RCT nobody will run.
- [ ] Does resistance training reduce mortality causally, or is muscle mass a marker for health and affluence? Momma's meta-analysis is observational throughout.
- [ ] What explains the physical activity paradox — why leisure activity looks protective and occupational activity does not?
- [ ] Is the constrained-total-energy-expenditure model right? Pontzer's data is striking and the mechanism is unresolved. **If correct, it reframes the entire exercise-for-weight-loss literature.**
- [ ] Is there a real upper limit where endurance volume becomes harmful? Coronary calcification findings in masters athletes are real; their prognostic meaning is not established.
- [ ] Why do non-responders exist, and can the stimulus be varied to reach them? HERITAGE identified the phenomenon in the 1990s and it remains largely unexplained.
- [ ] How much of the sedentary-behaviour association survives adjustment for what people are doing while sedentary? The TV-time residual suggests: not much.

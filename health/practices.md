# Practices — What To Actually Do

> **Status: mixed and marked per item.** Ranked strictly by evidence strength,
> not by how good it sounds or how much it costs. Several widely promoted
> practices here are **TIER 3** and I say so rather than smuggling them in beside
> the solid ones.

> **NOT MEDICAL ADVICE. Stated here again because this is the file people will
> act on.**
> - **No drug, dose, supplement or protocol is recommended in this notebook.**
> - **Nothing here is a reason to start, stop, reduce or skip a prescribed
>   treatment.** Abrupt discontinuation of some medications is dangerous. That
>   decision belongs with your prescriber.
> - **Population evidence does not tell you what happens to you.** Your
>   baseline risk, comorbidities, medications and values change every
>   calculation in this notebook.
> - **If you are in immediate danger — chest pain, stroke symptoms, thoughts of
>   suicide, an overdose — call your local emergency number.** Crisis lines: 988
>   (US/Canada), 116 123 Samaritans (UK/Ireland), 13 11 14 (Australia).

> **The honest summary before you start: four things carry most of the value —
> don't smoke, move regularly, know your blood pressure, and sleep enough.
> Everything else on this page is a rounding error by comparison, and everything
> below rank 12 is arguably noise.**

## Ranked by evidence strength

| Rank | Practice | Tier | Cost | Why it's ranked here |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Do not smoke. If you smoke, get help to stop** | **1** | Negative — it saves money | ~10 years of life. Nothing else on this list is within an order of magnitude. Cessation support roughly doubles or triples success rates versus willpower alone |
| 2 | **Know your blood pressure** | **1** | Free at a pharmacy | Silent, common, treatable, and the treatment prevents strokes. **The highest return on a single measurement in medicine** |
| 3 | **Move most days, at any intensity, including walking** | **1** | Free | Most of the available benefit is captured below the guideline minimum. **The first ten minutes are worth more than the hundredth** |
| 4 | **Keep vaccinations current for your age and risk group** | **1** | Usually free | The largest mortality reductions in history. Includes the ones adults forget |
| 5 | **Sleep enough, at regular times** | **1** for function, **2** for mortality | Free | And **do not catastrophise a bad night** — sleep anxiety is itself the mechanism that makes insomnia chronic |
| 6 | **Reduce or eliminate alcohol** | **1** for cancer, **2** for cardiovascular | Saves money | The J-curve was largely artefactual. There is no established protective dose |
| 7 | **Wear the seatbelt, the helmet, don't drive impaired or tired** | **1** | Free | Injury dominates years-of-life-lost in younger adults and is almost entirely absent from health discourse |
| 8 | **Do resistance training twice a week, especially past 40** | **1** for function, **2** for mortality | Free to cheap | 30–60 min/week is associated with 10–20% lower all-cause mortality†. **Sarcopenia is a principal route to losing independence and it is trainable** |
| 9 | **Maintain real relationships and see people in person** | **2** — observational only | Free | Effect size rivals smoking cessation. On the list because the evidence is large and consistent and **the intervention cannot hurt you** |
| 10 | **Have a clinician you can actually see, and go** | **1** by construction | Varies enormously by country | Every actionable TIER 1 item routes through this. **For many people this is the single highest-value item on the page** |
| 11 | **Eat mostly food you'd recognise as food; get enough fibre and protein; drink fewer sugary drinks** | **2** | Neutral to cheaper | The strongest defensible summary of a weak field. **Note how little it says** |
| 12 | **Treat your teeth and eyes** | **1–2** | Varies | Boring, cheap, and neglected in every wellness discussion |
| 13 | **Sun protection** | **2** | Cheap | Good evidence for skin cancer prevention, minimal all-cause mortality effect |
| 14 | **Get insomnia assessed rather than self-treating** | **1** | Varies | CBT-I is first-line, works, and outlasts treatment. **Almost nobody knows it exists** |
| 15 | **Get loud snoring with daytime sleepiness assessed** | **2** | Varies | Sleep apnoea is common, undiagnosed, and treatable for symptoms |
| 16 | **Discuss age- and risk-appropriate screening — including the harms** | **2** | Varies | **Genuinely two-sided.** Ask about overdiagnosis and false positives, not just detection rates |
| 17 | **Learn to read a health claim** | **1** as a skill | An hour | evidence-hierarchy.md. **Compounds over a lifetime and protects you from everything below** |

## What NOT to do, and what not to buy

**This is the higher-value half of the file.**

| Don't | Why |
|---|---|
| **Take supplements without a diagnosed deficiency** | Repeatedly tested, repeatedly null, **occasionally harmful.** Beta-carotene increased lung cancer in smokers. "Probably useless" is not "harmless" |
| **Take antioxidant supplements** | Several are associated with **increased** mortality in Cochrane analyses |
| **Buy a biological age or telomere test** | Poor test–retest reliability, no validated responsiveness to intervention. **You will pay for a number you cannot act on** |
| **Buy NMN, NR, resveratrol or other "longevity" compounds** | Trials raise a biomarker and show no clinical benefit. The sirtuin assay that started it was an artefact |
| **Buy senolytics outside a trial** | Excellent mouse data, human pilots measuring feasibility. **Not the same thing** |
| **Take prescription drugs off-label for longevity** | Rapamycin and metformin have no human lifespan RCT. Both have real side effects. This is a clinical decision and, in my reading, a poorly justified one |
| **Buy a detox, cleanse, or alkaline anything** | No mechanism, no evidence, no identified toxin |
| **Chase a named diet** | Trials find equivalence at matched adherence. **You are choosing a diet; the diets are not choosing you** |
| **Optimise the flat part of the exercise curve** | If you already train, more training buys performance, not longevity |
| **Buy a wearable expecting health improvement** | Some help with activity adherence. Sleep-stage data is unreliable and **orthosomnia is a documented consequence** |
| **Screen for everything you can pay for** | Full-body scans, whole-genome panels and executive health packages generate incidentalomas, cascades and overtreatment with no demonstrated mortality benefit |
| **Catastrophise a bad night's sleep** | It is normal. Worry is the mechanism that makes it chronic |
| **Assume a health headline is informative** | Ask: randomised or observational, hard outcome or surrogate, and what is the absolute risk? |
| **Stop a prescribed medication on the basis of reading** | **Including on the basis of this notebook.** Discuss it with the prescriber |
| **Use weight as your primary health target** | Fitness improves outcomes at any body size, weight-focused messaging contributes to disordered eating, and Look AHEAD found real weight loss without cardiovascular event reduction |
| **Take "natural" to mean "safe"** | Tobacco, arsenic, aristolochic acid, hepatotoxic herbal preparations. Supplement contamination and mislabelling are documented and regulation is weak in most jurisdictions |
| **Export the scepticism in this notebook to vaccination or blood pressure** | Those are the strongest evidence in the file. **Uniform scepticism is not calibration; it is a different bias** |

## The money point, stated plainly

| Item | Approximate cost | Evidence tier |
|---|---|---|
| Not smoking | **Saves money** | **1** |
| Walking | **£0** | **1** |
| Blood pressure check | **£0 at a pharmacy** | **1** |
| Sleeping | **£0** | **1** |
| Seeing your friends | **£0** | **2** |
| Bodyweight resistance training | **£0** | **1–2** |
| Vaccination | **£0 in most systems** | **1** |
| Longevity supplement stack | **£1,000+/year** | **3** |
| Biological age testing | **£200–500 each** | **3** |
| Executive full-body screening | **£1,000–3,000** | **2, plausibly negative** |
| Longevity clinic membership | **£5,000–100,000/year** | **3** |

**The correlation between price and evidence quality in this field is roughly
inverse, and the mechanism is not mysterious.** Interventions that are free,
unpatentable and already known cannot support an industry. Interventions that
are uncertain, expensive and novel can. **The market is not selling you health;
it is selling you the part of health that can be sold.**

**This is inconvenient for everyone with something to sell, including
publishers, influencers, clinics, supplement companies, and — in a smaller
way — anyone writing a health notebook. Discount accordingly, including this
one.**

## Questions worth asking a clinician

**Not to challenge them — to get better information than a consultation
normally produces under time pressure.**

| Question | What it surfaces |
|---|---|
| **"What's my absolute risk, not my relative risk?"** | Converts a scary percentage into a number you can reason about |
| **"What's the number needed to treat, roughly?"** | Reveals whether you are one of many treated for one to benefit |
| **"What happens if I do nothing?"** | The natural history. Frequently omitted, frequently the relevant comparator |
| **"Is this treating a disease or a risk factor?"** | Changes the harm calculus completely |
| **"What's the evidence — a trial, or observation?"** | Rarely asked; usually answerable |
| **"What are the harms of this test, including a false positive?"** | The half of screening that never gets mentioned |
| **"If this comes back abnormal, what happens next?"** | **Ask before the test, not after.** The cascade is the risk |
| **"Is there a less invasive option with similar evidence?"** | Often yes, especially for procedures |

**A clinician who welcomes these is a good sign. Time pressure, not
defensiveness, is the usual obstacle.**

## A note for anyone with an eating disorder history

**This notebook contains material about weight, food and restriction that is
unsafe to act on alone.** Restriction-focused health information is a documented
contributor to disordered eating, and the reasoning in this file — quantifying,
optimising, discounting expert reassurance — is exactly the cognitive style that
eating disorders exploit.

**Nutrition and weight are clinical territory for you.** That is not a smaller
version of the general advice; it is a different category. Please work with
someone.

## What I would actually tell someone who asked

**In order, and it is very short:**

1. If you smoke, that is the whole conversation. Everything else is rounding.
2. Go for a walk today, and most days. It does not have to be long.
3. Find out what your blood pressure is.
4. Sleep. Do not lie awake worrying about sleep.
5. Lift something heavy twice a week, especially if you are over 40.
6. Drink less, or none.
7. See people.
8. Have a doctor and go when something changes.
9. Do not buy anything.
10. When you read a health claim, ask whether it was randomised and what the
    absolute risk is.

**That is the entire evidence-based intervention list, it costs nothing, and
nobody will make money telling it to you. Which is precisely why you have heard
so much else instead.**

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular belief about *what to do* | Reality |
|---|---|
| There is an optimisation stack to assemble | There is a short list of mostly-free basics and a large market selling everything else |
| Doing more of the right things gives proportionally more benefit | Dose-response curves flatten hard. The first increment dominates |
| Health requires discipline and sacrifice | The TIER 1 list requires almost none. **The sacrifice narrative is what makes the expensive alternatives feel serious** |
| A personalised plan beats a generic one | DIETFITS tested personalisation by genotype and insulin response and found nothing. Consumer personalisation is mostly marketing |
| Tracking improves outcomes | Activity tracking modestly aids adherence. Sleep and biomarker tracking mostly produce anxiety and orthosomnia |
| Knowing more makes you healthier | Past the basics, more information produces more testing, more false positives, and more overtreatment |
| The right practice list is long | If your list has thirty items, twenty-five are TIER 3 and you have diluted the five that matter |
| Self-experimentation is a reasonable substitute for evidence | n=1, unblinded, uncontrolled, with regression to the mean and natural history unaccounted for. **It is not weak evidence; it is not evidence** |
| If you can afford better health, buy it | The purchasable interventions are the weakly-evidenced ones. **What money reliably buys is access to a clinician and time to exercise and sleep** — which is real, and is not a product |
| Doing nothing is the safe default | For smoking, blood pressure and vaccination it is not. **Scepticism is a tool, not a lifestyle** |

## Open questions

- [ ] Does knowing any of this change behaviour? **The health-behaviour literature says information alone changes very little**, which is an uncomfortable finding for a research notebook.
- [ ] What is the marginal value of health information past the TIER 1 list? Plausibly zero and possibly negative through anxiety and overtesting. **Nobody has measured it.**
- [ ] How much of the boring list do people already know and simply not do? If most of it, the binding constraint is not knowledge and this file is aimed at the wrong problem.
- [ ] Do decision aids that present absolute risk and harms actually improve screening decisions, or just reduce uptake indiscriminately?
- [ ] Is there a version of this ranking that survives the next twenty years of reversals? Prasad's base rate says at least one item will move. **I do not know which.**
- [ ] Does sceptical health writing cause net harm by reducing trust in the well-evidenced core? **The most serious objection to this entire notebook and I do not have an answer to it.**

# Ageing — What Is Known, and the Industry Built on What Isn't

> **Status: TIER 1** for the descriptive biology of ageing and for the failure
> of every longevity supplement tested with hard outcomes. **TIER 2** for the
> hallmarks framework as a causal account. **TIER 3** for essentially every
> intervention marketed to extend human lifespan.

> **Not medical advice.** No supplement, drug or protocol described here is
> recommended. Several are prescription medications being used off-label by
> people outside trials; that is a decision requiring a clinician, and in some
> cases a decision I would characterise as poorly justified given the evidence.

> **The finding: there is a large, well-funded industry selling interventions
> for which no human randomised trial with a hard outcome exists. Not weak
> evidence — no such evidence at all. Meanwhile the interventions with proven
> mortality benefit are the boring ones in what-actually-works.md.**

## The hallmarks framework

**López-Otín, Blasco, Partridge, Serrano & Kroemer, "The Hallmarks of Aging,"
*Cell*, 2013** — nine hallmarks; expanded to **twelve in the 2023 update.**

| Hallmark | Status |
|---|---|
| Genomic instability | Well established |
| Telomere attrition | Established in cells; **causal role in human ageing overstated** — see below |
| Epigenetic alterations | Well established descriptively; basis of the ageing clocks |
| Loss of proteostasis | Well established |
| Deregulated nutrient sensing | mTOR, IGF-1, AMPK. **The strongest interventional target in animals** |
| Mitochondrial dysfunction | Established; the "mitochondrial free radical theory" as the primary driver is largely abandoned |
| Cellular senescence | Established; causally demonstrated in mice |
| Stem cell exhaustion | Established |
| Altered intercellular communication | Established; includes "inflammaging" |
| *(2023)* Disabled macroautophagy | Newer |
| *(2023)* Chronic inflammation | Newer |
| *(2023)* Dysbiosis | **Weakest of the twelve** |

**The essential caveat about the whole framework:** the hallmarks are a
*descriptive taxonomy of things that change with age*. Which are **drivers**,
which are **consequences**, and which are **compensatory responses** is largely
unresolved. **A list of correlates of ageing is not a theory of ageing**, and
treating each hallmark as a drug target has produced a lot of activity and no
demonstrated human lifespan extension.

## Cellular senescence — the best animal evidence

Senescent cells stop dividing, resist apoptosis, and secrete inflammatory
factors (the **senescence-associated secretory phenotype**, SASP).

**Baker, van Deursen and colleagues (*Nature*, 2011 and 2016)** used the
INK-ATTAC transgenic mouse to clear p16-positive senescent cells. Clearance
**extended median lifespan by roughly 25%** in one strain and improved
healthspan measures.†

**Senolytics** — drugs that selectively kill senescent cells — followed:
dasatinib plus quercetin, fisetin, navitoclax and others.

**Where human evidence actually stands:**

| | Status |
|---|---|
| Mouse lifespan and healthspan | **Genuinely impressive** |
| Human trials | **Tiny pilots** — a handful of patients in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and diabetic kidney disease, measuring biomarkers and feasibility |
| Human hard outcomes | **None.** No mortality data, no long-term safety |
| Being sold today | **Yes, extensively** |

**The gap between "extended lifespan in a transgenic mouse" and "you should take
this" is enormous, and the supplement market has priced it at zero.**

## Telomeres — the clearest case of an overstated popular story

The 2009 Nobel Prize (Blackburn, Greider, Szostak) recognised real and important
biology. **What happened to the public story is a case study in mechanistic
over-extrapolation.**

| Popular claim | Reality |
|---|---|
| Telomere length is your "biological age" | It is a **weak** predictor of mortality — measurably worse than grip strength, gait speed or VO2max |
| Lengthening telomeres would slow ageing | **Mendelian randomisation (Haycock et al., *JAMA Oncology*, 2017) found genetically longer telomeres associated with substantially increased risk of several cancers** — lung adenocarcinoma, glioma, melanoma — alongside modestly reduced risk of some cardiovascular conditions. **Longer is not simply better** |
| Stress shortens telomeres and meditation lengthens them | The underlying studies are small, use variable measurement methods, and have not replicated robustly. **A high-profile literature with weak foundations** |
| Commercial telomere tests are informative | Measurement reliability is poor; **repeat measurements on the same sample can vary more than a decade of ageing.** These tests are not clinically actionable |

**The deep problem:** telomerase reactivation is a feature of **85–90% of
cancers.** A cell that can divide indefinitely is what a tumour is. Any
intervention that lengthens telomeres systemically is playing directly against
this.

## Caloric restriction — the best-studied intervention, and its human problem

**In short-lived organisms — yeast, worms, flies, rodents — caloric restriction
extends lifespan robustly and reproducibly.** It is the most replicated
intervention in gerontology.

**In primates, the picture broke down:**

| Study | Result |
|---|---|
| **Wisconsin (2009)** | CR monkeys showed improved survival from age-related causes |
| **NIA (2012)** | **No significant survival benefit** |
| **Joint reanalysis (Mattison et al., *Nature Communications*, 2017)** | Differences largely attributable to **diet composition and control-group feeding**: NIA controls were fed a healthier diet and were not truly ad libitum, while Wisconsin controls ate a high-sucrose diet freely. **The Wisconsin "CR effect" may partly be a "control group ate badly" effect** |

**This reconciliation is the most instructive result in the file**, because it
generalises: much of what looks like intervention benefit in animal work is
control-group harm.

**In humans:**

**CALERIE-2** (2015–2019) randomised healthy non-obese adults to two years of
caloric restriction; participants achieved roughly **12% restriction** against a
25% target.

| Found | Not found |
|---|---|
| Improved cardiometabolic risk markers | Any hard outcome — the trial was not designed for one |
| Reduced markers of inflammation | Any demonstration of slowed ageing |
| A signal on thymic function | Any mortality data, which would take decades |
| **Also found:** reductions in bone mineral density, lean mass, and (in some analyses) aspects of mood and libido | |

**Caloric restriction in humans is TIER 3 for longevity, has documented
downsides, and is genuinely risky for older adults and anyone with an eating
disorder history — where restriction is not a neutral experiment.**

**Intermittent fasting and time-restricted eating** are frequently marketed as
CR-equivalent. In trials, they mostly produce weight outcomes similar to
continuous restriction at matched intake, with no demonstrated independent
longevity effect. TIER 3.

## Pharmacological candidates — what the evidence actually is

| Candidate | Animal evidence | Human evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Rapamycin** | **The strongest.** NIA Interventions Testing Program (Harrison et al., *Nature*, 2009): lifespan extension in mice **started at 600 days of age** — late-life intervention worked. Replicated across strains and sexes | **None for lifespan.** Immunosuppression, metabolic effects, and mouth ulcers are real. The **Dog Aging Project (TRIAD)** is the most serious ongoing test |
| **Metformin** | Mixed; ITP results unimpressive for lifespan in healthy mice | **No RCT.** The observational claim that diabetics on metformin outlive non-diabetic controls is confounded by indication, prevalent-user bias and BMI. **Mendelian randomisation does not support it.** **TAME** was proposed to test this and has struggled for funding. Also: metformin may **blunt exercise training adaptations** (Konopka et al., 2019) — an underappreciated trade-off |
| **NAD+ precursors (NMN, NR)** | Effects in mice, contested magnitude | Human trials **raise NAD+ levels** and show **no clinical outcome benefit.** The endpoint that is measured is the one that is easy, not the one that matters |
| **Resveratrol** | The origin of the sirtuin story | **Largely collapsed.** Pacholec et al. (*JBC*, 2010) showed the original sirtuin-activation assays were an artefact of the fluorophore used. GSK acquired Sirtris for ~$720m in 2008 and discontinued the programme. **A complete cycle: mechanism, hype, acquisition, failure, and a supplement market that continues regardless** |
| **Senolytics** | Strong in mice | Tiny pilots only |
| **GLP-1 receptor agonists** | — | **The one place with real hard-outcome data** (SELECT, 2023: ~20% MACE reduction in overweight/obese patients with CVD). **Not marketed as longevity drugs and the best current candidate to actually be ones** |

**Note the pattern: the compounds with the loudest longevity marketing have the
least human evidence, and the compound with real outcome data is sold for
something else.**

## Blue Zones — and the serious challenge

**Dan Buettner's Blue Zones** (Okinawa, Sardinia, Ikaria, Nicoya, Loma Linda)
became one of the most influential ideas in popular health, generating books, a
Netflix series, certified "Blue Zone" municipalities, and a large body of
dietary inference.

**The challenge, principally from Saul Newman (Oxford/ANU), is serious:**

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Extreme-age regions correlate with absent birth records** | Newman's analyses report that regions with high rates of reported supercentenarians correlate with **later introduction of birth registration, higher poverty, lower literacy, and shorter average lifespans** — the opposite of what a genuine longevity cluster should look like |
| **Pension fraud is a live incentive** | Greece: a 2012 audit reportedly found a very large share of centenarian pension recipients were dead or non-existent.† Italy and Japan have had comparable scandals |
| **Japan's missing centenarians (2010)** | A national audit found **hundreds of thousands of registered centenarians unaccounted for**†, some dead for decades with pensions still collected. **Okinawa's pre-war records were substantially destroyed in 1945**, making age verification for the relevant cohorts extremely difficult |
| **Record-keeping improvements reduce the effect** | Where registration improved, reported extreme-age rates fell |
| **Newman received an Ig Nobel (2024)** | Which is a joke award with a serious point, and the work has drawn genuine engagement from demographers |

**How to hold this fairly:**

- **Newman's critique is partly preprint, and demographers dispute the strength
  of some of his claims.** It is not a settled refutation.
- **It does not follow that Okinawans or Sardinians are unhealthy.** Regional
  differences in cardiovascular mortality at ordinary ages are separately
  documented.
- **What it does undermine is the specific inference chain**: extreme
  longevity → identify a shared diet → recommend that diet. **If the extreme
  longevity numbers are inflated by administrative artefact, the entire
  dietary inference has no foundation.**
- The dietary characterisations were also constructed retrospectively from
  regions selected on the outcome — **selection on the dependent variable**, the
  same error catalogued in leaders/failure-modes.md.

**Verdict: TIER 3 and falling.** The Blue Zones literature should now be treated
as a hypothesis under active challenge, not as established fact.

## Biological age clocks

**Horvath's epigenetic clock (2013)** and successors (PhenoAge, GrimAge,
DunedinPACE) predict mortality better than chronological age alone. That is a
real result.

**The problems for consumer use:**

- **Test–retest reliability of some clocks is poor** (Higgins-Chen et al., 2022
  addressed this with principal-component versions), so a single reading has
  wide uncertainty.
- **No clock has been validated as a surrogate endpoint.** Changing your clock
  reading has never been shown to change your outcome. **This is precisely the
  surrogate-endpoint error that CAST should have permanently inoculated
  medicine against** — see the-control-group.md.
- The industry selling clock-based interventions is selling a measurement whose
  responsiveness to intervention is unestablished.

## Maximum lifespan — an open scientific dispute

| Position | Argument |
|---|---|
| **There is a hard limit near ~115** | Dong, Milholland & Vijg (*Nature*, 2016): reported age at death of the world's oldest person plateaued after the 1990s |
| **Mortality plateaus after ~105** | Barbi et al. (*Science*, 2018), Italian data: hazard flattens rather than rising, implying no fixed wall |
| **Methodological objections to both** | Small numbers, age-validation problems, and — per Newman — systematic age misreporting in exactly the population being analysed |

**Jeanne Calment (d. 1997, aged 122)** remains the record. A 2018 challenge by
Zak and Novoselov proposed an identity substitution; demographers have largely
rejected it, and the case illustrates how thin the data at the extreme is.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| Ageing research is close to extending human lifespan | **No intervention has ever been shown to extend human lifespan in a randomised trial.** Not one |
| Telomeres are the ageing clock | Weak predictor; longer telomeres genetically increase several cancer risks |
| Resveratrol/NMN/NAD+ boosters slow ageing | The sirtuin assay was an artefact; NAD+ trials raise a biomarker and show no outcome benefit |
| Blue Zones prove a longevity diet | The extreme-age data is under serious challenge on record-reliability grounds, and the dietary inference selected on the outcome |
| Caloric restriction extends human life | The primate results conflicted and were partly explained by control-group diet. Human data shows biomarker changes and real costs |
| Metformin is a proven longevity drug | No RCT. The observational case is confounded, MR does not support it, and TAME has not run |
| Biological age tests tell you how you're ageing | They tell you a number with poor reliability and no demonstrated responsiveness to intervention |
| Ageing is a disease that will be cured | Whether ageing is a disease is partly a definitional question. **Framing it as curable is a fundraising position, not a scientific finding** |
| Rich biohackers have found something | An n=1 protocol with no control arm, biomarker endpoints, and enormous confounding by wealth, healthcare access and selection. **It is not evidence and cannot become evidence** |
| Free radicals cause ageing, so take antioxidants | The free-radical theory is largely abandoned as a primary driver, and antioxidant supplement trials have been null to **harmful** |
| Anti-ageing is expensive | The interventions with actual mortality evidence — not smoking, activity, blood pressure control — are free or cheap |

## Open questions

- [ ] **Are any hallmarks causal drivers rather than consequences?** Senescence has the best mouse evidence. For most of the rest, nobody knows.
- [ ] Does rapamycin extend healthspan in a large mammal? **The Dog Aging Project is the most informative trial currently running in the field.**
- [ ] Can a validated healthspan or biological-age endpoint be constructed? Without one, no longevity drug can be trialled on anything but mortality, requiring decades.
- [ ] How much of the Blue Zones literature survives rigorous age validation? **The highest-value replication question in the file, and it is administrative rather than biological work.**
- [ ] Is there a fixed maximum human lifespan? Dong versus Barbi, unresolved, and confounded by the same age-verification problem.
- [ ] Do senolytics do anything in humans? Pilots exist; the outcome trials do not.
- [ ] Why does caloric restriction work so reliably in short-lived species and so ambiguously in primates? A satisfying answer would reshape the field.
- [ ] Is the longevity industry producing any science, or is it producing a market? **Both, in a ratio nobody has measured.**

# What Actually Works — The TIER 1 List

> **Status: TIER 1 throughout.** This file is deliberately restricted to
> interventions with either randomised evidence on hard outcomes, or
> observational evidence so large, consistent and dose-responsive across designs
> that the alternative explanations have been exhausted. Where an item rests on
> observational evidence alone, **I say so in the row.**

> **Not medical advice.** Blood pressure and lipid treatment in particular are
> clinical decisions that depend on your individual risk, and nothing here
> should be used to start, stop, or alter a prescribed treatment. Talk to a
> clinician who knows your history.

> **The finding of this file is its length.** Everything with strong evidence
> fits on one page. It is unexciting, mostly free, and almost entirely
> unmonetisable. **That is not a coincidence and it is the most useful thing in
> the notebook.**

## The list, ranked by expected magnitude

| # | Intervention | Best evidence | Rough effect size | Design |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Do not smoke; if you smoke, stop** | Doll & Peto British Doctors Study (1951–2001), n≈34,000 | **~10 years of life lost by lifelong smokers.** Quitting at 30 recovers nearly all; at 40, ~9 years; at 50, ~6 | Observational, but with dose-response, temporality, mechanism, and a natural experiment in every quitter |
| 2 | **Treat high blood pressure** | SPRINT (2015), HYVET, decades of RCTs | SPRINT: **~25% reduction in the primary CV composite, ~27% in all-cause mortality** vs standard target. Stopped early for benefit | **RCT, hard outcomes** |
| 3 | **Be physically active** | Arem et al. (2015) pooled cohorts; Ekelund et al. (2016) | **~20–35% lower all-cause mortality** at guideline levels vs sedentary | Observational at scale; RCTs on intermediate outcomes |
| 4 | **Get vaccinated per schedule** | Smallpox eradication (1980); measles, polio, pneumococcal, influenza, HPV trials | Smallpox: **from ~300 million deaths in the 20th century to zero.** HPV vaccination programmes: near-elimination of vaccine-type infection and precancer in cohorts | **RCT plus population elimination data** |
| 5 | **Lower LDL cholesterol where individual risk warrants it** | Cholesterol Treatment Trialists' meta-analyses; PCSK9 trials; Mendelian randomisation | **~21–22% reduction in major vascular events per 1 mmol/L LDL reduction per year of treatment**† | **RCT, hard outcomes, triangulated with MR** |
| 6 | **Do not get injured** — seatbelts, helmets, no drink-driving, fall prevention in older adults | Crash and trauma data; fall-prevention RCTs | Seatbelts: **~45% reduction in fatality for front-seat occupants**†. Injury is a leading cause of years-of-life-lost under 45 | Quasi-experimental (seatbelt laws), RCT (falls) |
| 7 | **Sleep adequately and regularly** | Van Dongen et al. (2003); large cohort mortality data | U-shaped mortality association with a nadir near 7h; **large, reproducible cognitive and metabolic effects of restriction in lab conditions** | RCT for short-term function; observational for mortality, with reverse causation live |
| 8 | **Drink little or no alcohol** | Stockwell et al. (2016); ALDH2 Mendelian randomisation; IARC Group 1 | The **J-curve is largely artefactual.** No demonstrated cardioprotection; monotonic dose-response for several cancers | MR plus corrected meta-analysis |
| 9 | **Maintain a body composition that isn't at either extreme** | Global BMI Mortality Collaboration; bariatric surgery cohorts; GLP-1 outcome trials (e.g. SELECT, 2023) | J-shaped mortality curve; **SELECT reported ~20% reduction in MACE** in overweight/obese patients with existing CVD | Mixed: observational for BMI, **RCT for pharmacological weight reduction** |
| 10 | **Have people in your life** | Holt-Lunstad et al. (2010), 148 studies | **~50% greater odds of survival** with stronger social relationships | **Observational only.** Reverse causation is entirely plausible. Included because the effect is large, consistent, and the intervention cannot hurt you |

## Why each item is on the list — and what would knock it off

### 1. Smoking

The strongest causal claim in the notebook that was never established by a
randomised trial. **It qualifies as TIER 1 on triangulation, not design:**
dose-response by pack-year, temporality, biological mechanism, an enormous
effect size that no plausible confounder can generate, a consistent picture
across dozens of countries and both sexes, and a natural experiment — every
quitter is their own before-and-after.

**Bradford Hill's 1965 criteria were formulated for exactly this argument.** The
tobacco industry's "correlation is not causation" defence was technically
correct and substantively fraudulent, and it is the reason that phrase carries
the weight it does today.

**Vaping is not on this list.** It is almost certainly far less harmful than
combustion, plausibly effective as a cessation aid, and has no long-term outcome
data. That combination is TIER 2, and it belongs in what-we-know.md, not here.

### 2. Blood pressure

**The best return on measurement in all of medicine.** Hypertension is common,
silent, cheaply detected, and treatable, and treating it prevents stroke — an
outcome that destroys function rather than just ending life.

**Caveats that matter and are usually omitted:** SPRINT used unattended
automated office measurement, which reads lower than standard clinic
measurement, so its "120" is not your clinic's "120." Intensive control produced
**more** hypotension, syncope, electrolyte abnormalities and acute kidney
injury. Targets are contested and are individualised for a reason. **This is a
clinician's decision, not a reader's.**

### 3. Physical activity

See exercise.md in full. The headline: the curve is **steep at the bottom and
flat at the top.** Most of the benefit is bought moving from nothing to
something. Evidence for hard outcomes is observational, and healthy-user bias is
live — but the dose-response, the concordance with randomised intermediate
outcomes, and the plausibility of the mechanism make this as strong as
observational evidence gets.

### 4. Vaccination

**The largest documented mortality reductions in the history of the species, and
the item on this list most subject to organised misinformation.** Smallpox
eradication is not a study result; it is the absence of a disease that killed
hundreds of millions.

The strongest honest criticisms are narrow: individual vaccine-year efficacy
varies (influenza notably), rare adverse events are real and must be counted,
and post-licensure surveillance is imperfect. **None of these is an argument
against the schedule, and treating them as one is how a legitimate safety
discussion gets weaponised.**

### 5. Lipids

**The best-triangulated causal claim in chronic disease.** Three independent
lines converge: RCTs of statins, ezetimibe and PCSK9 inhibitors; Mendelian
randomisation on LDL-raising and LDL-lowering variants; and the natural
experiments of familial hypercholesterolaemia and PCSK9 loss-of-function.

**The honest complications:** benefit scales with baseline risk, so primary
prevention in low-risk people has a large NNT; side effects in trials are less
common than in practice, where the nocebo effect is substantial (the SAMSON
trial, 2020, is the elegant demonstration — n=60, and symptoms on statin and on
placebo were near-identical); and **HDL turned out to be a marker, not a
target**, which should induce humility about anything not yet trial-tested.

### 6. Injury

**Systematically underweighted because it isn't a disease.** Under 45, external
causes dominate years-of-life-lost in most rich countries. Seatbelts, helmets,
not driving impaired, smoke alarms, swimming ability, and — in older adults —
fall prevention through strength and balance work, medication review and home
hazards, all have better evidence than most of the wellness industry combined.

### 7. Sleep

See sleep.md. **TIER 1 for short-term function, TIER 2 for the mortality
claims**, because the observational literature has severe reverse-causation
problems and some widely repeated popular claims are overstated. Listed here
because the functional effects are randomised, replicated and large.

### 8. Alcohol

**This is the item where the consensus moved most in the last decade.** The
J-curve is largely explained by the "sick quitter" problem — abstainer reference
groups in old cohorts contained people who had stopped drinking because they
were already ill. Correct for that and the protective effect mostly vanishes.
Mendelian randomisation using ALDH2 variants points the same way.

**Steelman:** a residual small cardiovascular benefit at low intakes has not
been definitively excluded, the MR studies are mostly East Asian and the variant
has other effects, and no long-term RCT exists (the NIH's attempt, MACH15, was
terminated in 2018 over industry funding involvement). **But the burden of proof
has shifted, and the cancer dose-response is not in doubt.**

### 9. Body composition

**Handled carefully, because this is where health advice does the most harm.**

The mortality curve against BMI is J-shaped, with the nadir in the overweight
range in several analyses — the "obesity paradox," largely explained by reverse
causation (illness causes weight loss) and smoking. BMI is a poor individual
measure: it misclassifies muscular people and misses visceral distribution.

**What is genuinely TIER 1:** severe obesity carries substantial risk; visceral
adiposity is more predictive than weight; and pharmacological weight reduction
now has randomised hard-outcome evidence (SELECT, 2023) which the diet
literature never produced.

**What is not:** that weight is under simple voluntary control, that willpower
is the mechanism, or that intentional weight loss in the mildly overweight
improves mortality. **Look AHEAD** (2013) randomised 5,145 people with type 2
diabetes to intensive lifestyle intervention, achieved real weight loss, and
found **no reduction in cardiovascular events** over roughly a decade. It was
stopped for futility.

**Safety note:** weight-focused health messaging is a documented contributor to
disordered eating. If you have a history of an eating disorder, this domain
warrants clinical support, not self-directed optimisation.

### 10. Social connection

**The only purely observational item on the list.** It is here because the
effect size rivals smoking cessation, it replicates across designs, countries
and outcome measures, the plausible mechanisms are multiple, and — decisively —
**the intervention has no downside.** The asymmetry justifies acting on weaker
evidence than the other items required.

## What is conspicuously absent, and why

| Not on the list | Why not |
|---|---|
| Any supplement, for anyone without a diagnosed deficiency | Repeatedly tested, repeatedly null, occasionally harmful. Beta-carotene *increased* lung cancer in smokers (CARET, ATBC) |
| Any specific named diet | Trials find them equivalent when adherence is matched. See nutrition.md |
| Fasting protocols, cold exposure, sauna, breathwork | Interesting, mostly TIER 2–3, no hard-outcome RCTs. Sauna has the best observational data (Finnish cohorts) and no trial |
| "Detoxing," alkaline anything, cleanses | No mechanism, no evidence, occasional harm |
| Most screening tests | See medicine-and-screening.md. Some are valuable; several have negative expected value |
| Antioxidants generally | Cochrane analyses associate several with **increased** mortality |
| Stress reduction as a mortality intervention | Real for wellbeing, unproven for lifespan |
| Sunscreen | Actually decent evidence for skin cancer prevention — omitted only because it barely moves all-cause mortality. Include it in your own version |

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| There is a long list of things that extend life | There are about ten, and two of them are "don't smoke" and "don't crash" |
| The list is being expanded by research | The list has been **shortened** by research. HRT, antioxidants, vitamin D, moderate alcohol and several drugs were all removed |
| Diet is the biggest lever | Smoking, blood pressure and physical activity all have better evidence and larger effects. **Diet gets the most attention and has the weakest data** |
| Optimising further gives more benefit | Dose-response curves flatten hard. The first increment is worth many multiples of the last |
| Expensive interventions are better | The correlation is close to inverse. The top of this list costs nothing |
| Genetics dominates | Twin studies typically attribute roughly 20–30% of lifespan variance to genetics†, and recent work using assortative-mating corrections suggests even less. **Most of it is not inherited** |
| Doctors have a longevity toolkit they aren't sharing | They have this list. It is boring, they say it constantly, and nobody wants to hear it |
| Health is mostly healthcare | Medical care is estimated to account for perhaps 10–20% of variance in population health outcomes†; behaviour, environment and socioeconomic position dominate |

## Open questions

- [ ] **How much of the physical-activity mortality association is causal?** No RCT is powered for mortality and none will be. This is the largest unresolved question about the largest item on the list.
- [ ] What is the optimal blood pressure target for whom? SPRINT versus ACCORD-BP versus real-world measurement gives different answers. **Actively contested.**
- [ ] Does intentional weight loss reduce mortality in people who are not severely obese? Look AHEAD says no for events in diabetics. The GLP-1 outcome trials are changing this and the picture is incomplete.
- [ ] Is there any safe level of alcohol? The cancer curve looks monotonic; the cardiovascular question is not fully closed.
- [ ] Is social connection causal, and if so, is loneliness intervenable at scale? **Trials of loneliness interventions have been mostly disappointing**, which is evidence against the naive causal reading.
- [ ] What is the correct primary-prevention threshold for lipid treatment? A values question dressed as an evidence question, and it will not be settled by data.
- [ ] Which of these ten will be on the list in twenty years? Prasad's reversal rate says to expect at least one to move.

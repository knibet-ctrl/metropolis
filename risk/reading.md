# Reading — Primary Sources Worth The Time

> **Status: TIER 2 on the recommendations** — these are judgements about value,
> not findings. **Every entry carries the caveat it deserves**, including the
> ones I rate highest.

> **Compiled from general knowledge without verification.** Titles, dates and

## Read these four first

If you read nothing else, read one from each row. They cover the four axes:
forecasting skill, tail risk, judgement error, and system failure.

| Book | Author | Year | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Superforecasting** | Philip Tetlock & Dan Gardner | 2015 | The only book here whose central claims come from a large, preregistered-ish, adversarially-scored tournament |
| **The Black Swan** | Nassim Nicholas Taleb | 2007 | The tail-risk argument, argued hard. Abrasive and correct about the thing that matters |
| **Thinking, Fast and Slow** | Daniel Kahneman | 2011 | **With the replication caveat below.** Still the best single map of the terrain |
| **Normal Accidents** | Charles Perrow | 1984 | The structural theory of why complex tightly-coupled systems fail. Unmatched |

## Forecasting

**Philip Tetlock, *Expert Political Judgment: How Good Is It? How Can We Know?*
(2005).** The academic book, and the one to read if you want the evidence rather
than the summary. Twenty years of scored forecasts from 284 experts. **The
foxes/hedgehogs finding and the negative fame–accuracy correlation are here in
full.** Dry. Worth it.

**Philip Tetlock & Dan Gardner, *Superforecasting* (2015).** The popular
account of the Good Judgment Project. **Strength: the underlying research is
genuinely good — real tournament, real scoring, replicated across seasons.
Weakness: the narrative smooths the results, and the frequently-repeated claim
that superforecasters beat intelligence analysts with classified access by ~30%
rests on data that is classified and cannot be checked.**

**Nate Silver, *The Signal and the Noise* (2012).** The best available
introduction to why some domains are forecastable and others are not — weather,
baseball, earthquakes, poker, elections, each treated separately. **Strength:
domain-by-domain rather than one grand theory. Weakness: written before the
replication crisis and before 2016, and it is more optimistic about model-driven
forecasting than the last decade supports.** Silver's later *On the Edge* (2024)
covers risk-taking cultures and is more uneven.

**Sherman Kent, "Words of Estimative Probability" (1964).** Short, free, and one
of the highest value-per-page items in this file. **The 1951 "serious
possibility" episode — readers interpreting the same phrase as anywhere from 20%
to 80% — is the entire case for numeric probability in one anecdote.**

**Douglas Hubbard, *How to Measure Anything* (2007).** Unglamorous and
practically the most useful book here for working professionals. Calibration
training exercises, the value of information calculation, and a sustained
argument that "immeasurable" almost always means "not yet decomposed."

## Tail risk and uncertainty

**Nassim Nicholas Taleb, *Incerto* — *Fooled by Randomness* (2001), *The Black
Swan* (2007), *Antifragile* (2012), *Skin in the Game* (2018), *Statistical
Consequences of Fat Tails* (2020).**

| Volume | Read it for | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| *Fooled by Randomness* | Survivorship bias and the role of luck in track records. **Probably the best of the five** | Loose in places |
| *The Black Swan* | The core argument. Mediocristan/Extremistan | Repetitive; the score-settling is tiresome and gets in the way |
| *Antifragile* | The convexity idea | The concept is real; the term does more rhetorical than analytical work outside biology and options |
| *Skin in the Game* | The ethical argument about asymmetric exposure | Least rigorous of the set |
| *Statistical Consequences of Fat Tails* | **The actual mathematics.** Free online | Technical. This is where the claims are cashed out, and critics who haven't read it are arguing with the wrong book |

**Honest note: Taleb is right about the central thing and the presentation
costs him readers who need to hear it.** Read *Fooled by Randomness* and
*Statistical Consequences*; the middle three are the same argument at different
lengths.

**Benoit Mandelbrot & Richard Hudson, *The (Mis)Behavior of Markets* (2004).**
Mandelbrot published on non-Gaussian price behaviour in **1963** and was ignored
for four decades. **This is the priority claim, from the source, and it is more
temperate than Taleb.** Read it if you want the fat-tails argument without the
polemic.

**Frank Knight, *Risk, Uncertainty and Profit* (1921).** The origin of the
distinction. Genuinely hard going and mostly of historical interest; read a
summary unless you have a reason.

**Ole Peters, ergodicity economics — papers and lecture notes, mostly post-2010,
much of it freely available.** *(There is a Peters & Adamou lecture-note series
online; I do not have a reliable citation for a single canonical book.)* **The
coin-flip example in fat-tails.md comes from here and it is the highest
insight-per-minute item in the whole notebook.** Contested in scope, not in
mathematics.

## Judgement, biases, and the replication problem

**Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011).**

**Read it. And read it knowing the following, which the book does not tell you
because it could not:**

- **Kahneman himself wrote in 2017** that he had placed too much faith in
  underpowered studies, **specifically naming chapter 4** (the priming
  material). He had warned social priming researchers of "a train wreck looming"
  in an open letter in **2012**.
- **Findings cited in the book that have since failed:** ego depletion (23 labs,
  n≈2,141, d≈0.04), social priming (Doyen et al. 2012 traced the elderly-walking
  effect to experimenter expectancy), facial feedback (17-lab null, 2016).
- **Findings that hold:** anchoring, overconfidence in intervals, scope
  insensitivity, hindsight bias, availability (directionally), the conjunction
  fallacy as an effect.
- **The book has not been revised, and the dead findings continue to propagate
  through management training and journalism.**

**It remains the best single map of the territory. Treat it as a map with
regions marked "here be dragons" that are not marked on the map.**

**Kahneman, Sibony & Sunstein, *Noise* (2021).** **Better evidenced than
*Thinking, Fast and Slow* and much less read.** Noise is measured by giving
professionals identical cases and observing the spread — this is measurement,
not a psychology experiment, so it does not have a replication problem in the
same way. Underwriters differing by a median 55%; judges likewise. **The
practical recommendations — decision hygiene, structured protocols, independent
judgements before discussion — are the most actionable in the behavioural
literature.**

**Gerd Gigerenzer — *Calculated Risks* / *Reckoning with Risk* (2002),
*Gut Feelings* (2007), *Risk Savvy* (2014).** The serious counter-programme.
**Read *Calculated Risks* specifically for the natural-frequencies argument and
the medical screening chapters** — it is the most immediately life-useful book
in this file. **The Gigerenzer/Kahneman dispute is real and neither side is
foolish. Reading only one of them gives you a distorted picture of the field.**

**Gigerenzer, Todd & the ABC Research Group, *Simple Heuristics That Make Us
Smart* (1999).** The technical version: take-the-best, recognition heuristic,
fast-and-frugal trees, and the demonstration that simple rules beat regression
out of sample. **This is the less-is-more result with the data attached.**

**Paul Meehl, *Clinical versus Statistical Prediction* (1954).** Short, seventy
years old, and still ignored. **If you make repeated judgements of the same
kind, this is the most consequential 150 pages you can read.** Grove et al.
(2000) is the modern meta-analysis if you want the updated numbers.

**Robyn Dawes, "The Robust Beauty of Improper Linear Models in Decision Making"
(*American Psychologist*, 1979).** A paper, not a book. **Unit-weighted models
with no fitted coefficients beat expert judgement.** Fifteen pages that should
have changed several professions and didn't.

**Simmons, Nelson & Simonsohn, "False-Positive Psychology" (2011).** The paper
that made the replication crisis undeniable by demonstrating that undisclosed
analytic flexibility can produce p<0.05 for arbitrary false hypotheses. **Short.
Read it if you ever cite a psychology study.**

## Decisions

**Gary Klein, *Sources of Power* (1998)** and **"Performing a Project
Premortem" (*Harvard Business Review*, September 2007).** Klein's naturalistic
decision-making tradition studied experts making *good* decisions under time
pressure — the opposite research programme to Kahneman's, and a necessary
corrective to reading only the error literature. **The 2009 Kahneman–Klein joint
paper, "Conditions for Intuitive Expertise: A Failure to Disagree," is the best
single statement of when to trust intuition and is freely available.**

**Annie Duke, *Thinking in Bets* (2018).** Poker player turned decision
researcher. **The concept of *resulting* — judging decision quality by outcome —
is worth the price alone, and it is the error most organisations institutionalise.**
Light on evidence, heavy on framing, and the framing is good.

**Richard Zeckhauser, "Investing in the Unknown and Unknowable" (2006).** A
paper. The argument that the best returns are in situations that are not merely
uncertain but *unknowable*, where competition is thin because most participants
require estimable probabilities. Under-read.

## Systems and failure

**Charles Perrow, *Normal Accidents* (1984).** **The most important book in this
section.** Interactive complexity and tight coupling as structural predictors of
accidents. **Read it as a permanent corrective to "we just need better
procedures."**

**Karl Weick & Kathleen Sutcliffe, *Managing the Unexpected* (2001).** The High
Reliability Organisation counter-argument, with the five practices. **Read it
alongside Perrow, not instead of him. The dispute between them is unresolved and
is the most useful unresolved dispute in the notebook.**

**James Reason, *Human Error* (1990)** and *Managing the Risks of
Organizational Accidents* (1997). The Swiss cheese model, and the
active-failure/latent-condition distinction that should be applied to every
incident review you ever attend.

**Diane Vaughan, *The Challenger Launch Decision* (1996).** Normalization of
deviance, documented in exhaustive archival detail. **Published seven years
before Columbia demonstrated the mechanism again at the same agency.** Long and
worth it.

**Richard Feynman, Appendix F to the Rogers Commission Report (1986).** Free,
about ten pages, and the clearest short statement of the difference between an
engineering estimate and a management estimate ever written. **1 in 100 versus
1 in 100,000; the actual record was 1 in 67.5.**

**The Columbia Accident Investigation Board Report (2003)**, particularly
chapters 7 and 8 on organisational causes. Free. **A government body writing
plainly that it had failed to learn from its own previous investigation is rare
and instructive.**

**Bent Flyvbjerg & Dan Gardner, *How Big Things Get Done* (2023)**, and
Flyvbjerg's 2002 JAPA paper *"Underestimating Costs in Public Works Projects:
Error or Lie?"* **The paper is the evidence; the book is the argument.** The
modularity finding — that repeatable modular projects deliver and bespoke
mega-projects don't — is the most actionable result in the project literature.

## What I would skip, and why

| Item | Why |
|---|---|
| Most "decision-making" business books | Recycled Kahneman, often citing findings that are now dead, with no replication caveat |
| Popular treatments of nudge theory | The field-deployed effect is ~1/6 of the published effect. Read DellaVigna & Linos (2022) instead |
| Anything asserting a "10,000 hour rule" | Gladwell's framing, not Ericsson's finding. Deliberate practice explains ~1% of variance in professions |
| Anything citing the Stanford Prison Experiment as evidence | It is a demonstration with n=24, no control, and coached guards. Not evidence |
| Books promising to eliminate cognitive bias | General debiasing training has weak evidence. **Environmental redesign — formats, checklists, algorithms — has much better evidence and is less marketable** |

## Open questions

- [ ] Is there a good modern textbook treatment that integrates Tetlock's forecasting results with Taleb's tail-risk argument rather than treating them as rival camps? I don't know of one, and it is the obvious missing book.
- [ ] Has anyone written a revised, replication-aware edition of the popular behavioural-economics canon? *Noise* is the closest thing and it is a different book, not a correction.
- [ ] Is there an accessible empirical treatment of ergodicity economics for non-specialists? The lecture notes are good and the popular explanations almost all omit the arithmetic, which is why the idea doesn't land.
- [ ] Which of the books above will look worst in ten years? My guess is *Antifragile*, on grounds of imprecision, and the optimistic parts of *The Signal and the Noise*. Recorded so it can be checked.
- [ ] Is the Perrow/HRO dispute adjudicated anywhere I haven't seen? If someone has done the cross-industry quantitative comparison, it would be the most valuable paper in the field.

# Biases — With the Replication Caveat First

> **Status: TIER 1 that the replication crisis is real and severe. TIER 2 on
> which specific findings survive. TIER 3 or dead for a substantial fraction of
> the famous ones.**

> **Read this section before any other part of this file.** Nothing below the
> caveat should be read as settled science, and several famous findings in the
> popular literature are now believed to be false.

---

## THE CAVEAT — read this first

**A substantial portion of the heuristics-and-biases and social-psychology
literature that reached the public between 1970 and 2015 has failed to
replicate.** This is not a fringe complaint. It is the mainstream position of
the field.

| Evidence | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Open Science Collaboration (2015)**, *Science* | Attempted replication of **100** psychology studies. Roughly **36%** produced statistically significant results in the same direction. Mean effect size roughly **half** the original |
| Social psychology subset | Replicated at a notably **lower** rate than cognitive psychology |
| **Camerer et al. (2018)**, *Nature Human Behaviour* | 21 social science studies from *Nature* and *Science*, 2010–2015. **13 of 21 (62%)** replicated, with effect sizes ~50% of original |
| **Many Labs** projects (Klein et al., 2014 onward) | Some classic effects replicated robustly and repeatedly; others failed everywhere |

**Daniel Kahneman himself, in 2012, wrote an open letter to social priming
researchers warning of "a train wreck looming."** In 2017 he publicly stated
that he had placed too much faith in underpowered studies, specifically
including chapter 4 of *Thinking, Fast and Slow*.

**Why it happened — the mechanisms, none of which require fraud:**

| Mechanism | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Underpowered samples** | n=20 per cell was normal. Such studies can only detect enormous effects; any significant result is therefore likely inflated or spurious |
| **Publication bias** | Null results were unpublishable. The literature is a filtered sample |
| **p-hacking / researcher degrees of freedom** | Simmons, Nelson & Simonsohn (2011) demonstrated that undisclosed flexibility in analysis can produce p<0.05 for false hypotheses at will |
| **HARKing** | Hypothesising after results are known, presented as a priori |
| **No preregistration** | Standard until roughly 2013 |

**How to read the rest of this file:** every finding is marked **HOLDS**,
**WEAKENED**, or **DEAD**. Where I am unsure, I say so. **The popular
literature — including bestsellers — has not been updated and continues to
transmit findings that are known to be false.**

## The programme, briefly

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman, beginning ~1971. The claim: people use
**heuristics** — fast rules — that work well in many environments and produce
**systematic, predictable errors** in others. Kahneman's Nobel, 2002 (Tversky
died in 1996 and Nobels are not awarded posthumously).

The dual-process framing (System 1 / System 2, terminology from Stanovich and
West) is a **useful metaphor with weak neural grounding**. It is not a claim
about two brain systems.

## What holds up

| Finding | Status | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Overconfidence in interval estimates** | **HOLDS — strongly** | Alpert & Raiffa (1982): people asked for 98% confidence intervals produced intervals containing the truth ~60% of the time. Expected surprise rate 2%; observed ~40%. **Replicated for decades across domains and cultures.** The most robust finding in the file |
| **Base rate neglect** | **HOLDS, format-dependent** | Real, but Gigerenzer showed natural frequencies substantially dissolve it. See probability-basics.md. Both facts are true |
| **Anchoring** | **HOLDS** | Many Labs 1 (2014) found anchoring among the most robust effects tested. Real-world versions (initial price offers, sentencing recommendations) replicate |
| **Scope insensitivity** | **HOLDS** | Desvousges et al. (1992): willingness to pay to save **2,000 / 20,000 / 200,000** birds was roughly **$80 / $78 / $88**. A 100-fold change in stakes produced no change in valuation. Replicated across contingent-valuation studies |
| **Hindsight bias** | **HOLDS** | Fischhoff (1975). Once you know the outcome you cannot recover your prior uncertainty. Robust |
| **Availability** | **HOLDS directionally** | Probability judgements track ease of recall. Some specific original demonstrations are shaky; the phenomenon is not |
| **Noise (inconsistency)** | **HOLDS — and is the strongest of all** | Kahneman/Sibony/Sunstein (2021). Underwriters differed from each other by a median ~55% on identical cases. Judges given identical case files gave wildly different sentences. **This is measurement, not a psychology experiment, which is why it is bulletproof** |
| **Confirmation / positive-test strategy** | **HOLDS** | Wason (1960, 1968). People test hypotheses by seeking confirming instances |
| **Sunk cost effects** | **HOLDS, moderated** | Real in field data (Arkes & Blumer 1985). Effect sizes vary; the phenomenon survives |
| **Conjunction fallacy (Linda)** | **HOLDS, but** | Tversky & Kahneman (1983): ~85% ranked "bank teller and feminist" above "bank teller." **Hertwig & Gigerenzer (1999) showed frequency framing cuts it dramatically.** The error is real; the interpretation is contested |

## What is weakened

| Finding | Status | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| **Loss aversion (λ ≈ 2)** | **WEAKENED** | The *phenomenon* survives; the claimed universality and the coefficient do not. **Gal & Rucker (2018)**, "The Loss of Loss Aversion," argues much of the evidence is better explained by status-quo bias and inertia. Loss aversion in *risky* choice is better supported than in riskless choice |
| **Endowment effect** | **WEAKENED / bounded** | **John List (2003)**: experienced sports-card traders showed **no endowment effect**. Market experience eliminates it. It is a novice effect, not a human universal |
| **Nudges** | **HEAVILY WEAKENED** | **DellaVigna & Linos (2022)**: academic nudge literature reports average effects ~**8.7 percentage points**; two large US nudge units running trials on **~23 million people** achieved ~**1.4 percentage points**. **Roughly a 6x gap, attributable mainly to publication bias.** Nudges work; they work about one-sixth as well as advertised |
| **Growth mindset** | **WEAKENED** | Yeager et al. (2019), National Study of Learning Mindsets, n≈12,490 US students: overall GPA effect ~**0.03–0.05 points**, concentrated in lower-achieving students. Real, tiny, and a fraction of popular claims |
| **Marshmallow test** | **HEAVILY WEAKENED** | Watts, Duncan & Quan (2018), n≈918 with controls: predictive effect roughly **halved** and largely accounted for by family SES and home environment. The "willpower predicts life outcomes" story does not survive |
| **Choice overload (jam study)** | **WEAKENED** | Iyengar & Lepper (2000). Scheibehenne et al. (2010) meta-analysis found a **mean effect near zero** with large heterogeneity. Sometimes real, conditions poorly understood |
| **Implicit bias / IAT predictive validity** | **WEAKENED** | Test-retest reliability is low (r ≈ 0.5), and meta-analyses find weak prediction of individual discriminatory behaviour. The existence of implicit associations is not the issue; the *instrument's* individual-level validity is |

## What is dead

| Finding | Status | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| **Ego depletion** | **DEAD** | Baumeister et al. (1998) radish-and-cookie study spawned hundreds of papers. **Hagger et al. (2016)**: preregistered multi-lab replication, **23 labs, n≈2,141**, effect size **d ≈ 0.04**, confidence interval spanning zero. Subsequent registered replications also null. There is no glucose-depletion account of willpower |
| **Social priming (elderly-walking)** | **DEAD** | Bargh, Chen & Burrows (1996): subjects primed with age-related words walked more slowly. **Doyen et al. (2012)** failed to replicate and showed the effect appeared only when experimenters *knew the condition* — it was experimenter expectancy |
| **Power posing (hormonal effects)** | **DEAD** | Carney, Cuddy & Yap (2010) claimed testosterone up, cortisol down, risk tolerance up. **Ranehill et al. (2015)**, n≈200, found **no hormonal or behavioural effects**. **Dana Carney, a lead author, publicly stated in 2016 that she does not believe the effects are real.** A "felt power" self-report effect may survive |
| **Facial feedback (pen-in-mouth)** | **DEAD as originally reported** | Strack et al. (1988). **Wagenmakers et al. (2016)**: 17-lab registered replication, n≈1,894, null. Later work with different protocols has found small effects; the original claim does not stand |
| **Stanford Prison Experiment** | **NOT EVIDENCE** | Zimbardo, 1971. Archival work shows guards were coached toward brutality, one key "breakdown" was later described by the participant as feigned, n=24, no control, no hypothesis test. **It is a demonstration, not an experiment, and should not be cited as evidence of anything** |
| **"10,000 hours"** | **NOT AS POPULARISED** | Ericsson's deliberate-practice research is real; the "10,000 hours" rule is Gladwell's. Macnamara et al. (2014) meta-analysis: deliberate practice explains ~**26%** of variance in games, ~**21%** in music, ~**18%** in sport, and **~1%** in professions |

## Gigerenzer's critique — take it seriously

Gerd Gigerenzer (Max Planck Institute for Human Development) has mounted the
most serious sustained attack on the heuristics-and-biases interpretation. **He
does not deny the experimental results. He denies that they show
irrationality.**

### The four arguments

**1. Format, not cognition.** Many "biases" are artefacts of presenting
probabilities as single-event percentages, a format that appeared in human life
around 1650 and does not match how we encounter frequency information.
Gigerenzer & Hoffrage (1995): physicians' correct diagnostic inferences rose
from roughly **16% to ~46%** when the identical problem was stated in natural
frequencies rather than probabilities. **Same doctors. Same information.
Different representation.** *(Percentages vary by study; the direction and
magnitude are robust.)*

Hertwig & Gigerenzer (1999) did the same to the Linda problem: conjunction
errors fell sharply under "how many out of 100" framing.

**2. Ecological rationality.** A heuristic is not good or bad in itself; it is
good or bad **relative to an environment**. Judging heuristics against a
normative model derived from a different environment is not a demonstration of
irrationality. **This is a philosophical point and it is a strong one.**

**3. Less-is-more effects are real and demonstrable.**

| Result | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Recognition heuristic** | Goldstein & Gigerenzer (2002): German students outperformed American students at judging which of two US cities was larger. **Knowing less produced better inference**, because recognition itself carried the signal |
| **Take-the-best** | A one-cue lexicographic rule matched or beat multiple regression on out-of-sample prediction across ~20 datasets |
| **1/N portfolios** | DeMiguel, Garlappi & Uppal (2009): naive equal weighting **beat 14 sophisticated mean-variance optimisation models** out of sample across multiple datasets. Optimisation overfits estimation error |
| **Fast-and-frugal trees** | Green & Mehr (1997): a 3-question decision tree for coronary care unit admission was **more accurate than the logistic regression model** and than physicians |

**Why less-is-more works: under uncertainty, complex models fit noise.** In a
low-signal environment a simple rule has higher bias and much lower variance,
and variance dominates. **This is not a psychological claim, it is a statistical
one, and it is correct.**

**4. Real-world constraint.** Under time pressure, incomplete information and
computational limits, "optimal" Bayesian updating is not available. Simon's
bounded rationality (1955) and satisficing. **Rationality that cannot be
executed is not a normative standard.**

### Steelman the other side

Kahneman's camp's best responses:

- **The overconfidence and noise findings do not depend on format at all**, and
  they are the most damaging in practice.
- **Format effects reduce base-rate neglect; they do not eliminate it.** A
  residue persists.
- **Ecological rationality can excuse anything post hoc** — for any error you
  can construct an environment where it would be adaptive. That risks
  unfalsifiability, symmetrically to the charge against Kahneman.
- **In modern environments the mismatch is what matters.** If our heuristics are
  well-adapted to Pleistocene frequency information and badly adapted to
  mortgage-backed securities, the practical conclusion is identical to
  Kahneman's.

**My read: Gigerenzer wins on interpretation and on the practical fix
(redesign the representation), Kahneman's camp wins on the persistence of
overconfidence and noise. Both agree the errors occur. The dispute is about
what to call them and what to do about them, and the "what to do" answer —
change the format, use checklists, use algorithms — comes mostly from
Gigerenzer.**

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular claim | What's actually the case |
|---|---|
| "*Thinking, Fast and Slow* is a summary of settled science" | Kahneman himself has said parts of it — particularly the priming chapter — rested on studies he should not have trusted. The book has not been revised |
| "Willpower is a depletable resource" | Ego depletion is dead. **d ≈ 0.04** across 23 preregistered labs. The glucose version is worse |
| "Power posing changes your hormones" | An original author says she doesn't believe it. The replication was null |
| "The Stanford Prison Experiment showed situations make people evil" | It showed that if you tell people to act like brutal guards, some will. It is not evidence |
| "The marshmallow test predicts life success" | Largely a proxy for family socioeconomic status. Effect roughly halves with controls |
| "Nudges are a cheap policy win" | They produce roughly **1.4pp** in large real-world deployment, not the **8.7pp** in the published literature. Still often cost-effective; not transformative |
| "Humans are irrational" | Overstated and probably wrong as framed. Humans use fast rules that are well-adapted to some environments and badly adapted to others. **"Irrational" is a verdict, not a finding** |
| "Biases can be trained away" | Weak evidence for general debiasing. **Much stronger evidence for changing the environment**: natural frequencies, checklists, forced consideration of base rates, algorithms |
| "Loss aversion is the most robust finding in behavioural economics" | It was said for years. It is now among the more contested, especially outside risky choice |
| "The replication crisis was about fraud" | Overwhelmingly not. It was underpowered designs, publication bias, and analytic flexibility — all of which were normal practice and none of which required bad faith |
| "Behavioural economics is discredited" | Overcorrection. Anchoring, overconfidence, scope insensitivity and noise are solid, and noise may be the most practically important finding the field has produced |

## Open questions

- [ ] What is the honest current effect size for base-rate neglect under natural-frequency presentation? Both camps cite favourable numbers and I do not know of a clean adjudicating meta-analysis.
- [ ] Does general debiasing training work at all? The forecasting-training result (~10% improvement from one hour, Good Judgment Project) is the strongest positive evidence and it is domain-specific.
- [ ] How much of the surviving canon will survive the *next* decade of preregistered replication? Base rate from 2015–2025 says: be pessimistic.
- [ ] Is "ecological rationality" falsifiable, or is it a post-hoc licence? Gigerenzer's specific models (take-the-best, recognition) are falsifiable and have been tested. The general framework is looser.
- [ ] Why did the field's self-correction take until ~2011–2015 when the statistical critiques (Meehl, Cohen, Ioannidis 2005) were decades old? Same structural question as Mandelbrot in fat-tails.md, and probably the same answer.
- [ ] Should popular books with known-dead findings carry corrections? No mechanism exists, and the findings continue to propagate through management training, HR practice and journalism.

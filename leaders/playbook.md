# The Playbook — Mechanisms for People Without Armies

The case studies are useless unless translated. This file is the translation:
each mechanism as something a normal person can run *this month*, with no
inherited kingdom, no fortune, and no war.

---

## Tier 1 — Start here (highest return, lowest cost)

### 1. Pick one problem and refuse to leave it for 5+ years
This is mechanism #2, and it is the closest thing to a cheat code available.

- Choose a problem that is **real, narrow, and compounding**.
- Compounding means: year 4 is easier than year 1 because of what year 1–3 built (reputation, relationships, a codebase, a body of data, a skill tree).
- **Anti-pattern:** rotating to whatever is currently exciting. Ten one-year efforts produce nothing; one ten-year effort produces an unassailable position.
- **Test:** "Will the work I do this year still be worth something in year five?"

### 2. Get direct contact with reality, weekly
Mechanism #1 and #10.

- Go where the actual work happens. Talk to the actual user, patient, customer, operator.
- Write down one thing you learned that contradicts what the room believes.
- Do this 50 times a year and you will know things nobody else in your organisation knows. That *is* the edge. It's not glamorous and it's available to everyone, which is precisely why almost nobody does it.

### 3. Classify every decision as reversible or not
Mechanism #4.

- Reversible → decide within 24 hours, at ~70% confidence, and set a date to check.
- Irreversible → slow down, write a page, argue against yourself, sleep on it.
- Most people's lives are slowed to a crawl by treating reversible decisions as if they were permanent. Fixing this alone can double your effective speed.

### 4. Write. Publicly, and in your own words.
Mechanism #5.

- Caesar wrote his own dispatches. Ashoka carved edicts. FDR broadcast.
- The modern equivalent costs nothing: publish your thinking on the narrow problem from #1, consistently, for years.
- This does three things at once: forces clarity, compounds reputation, and makes the right people find *you* instead of you chasing them.
- **Whoever names the problem owns the conversation about it.**

## Tier 2 — Structural moves

### 5. Find the binding constraint before working hard
Mechanism #10.

- Before any big effort: "If this succeeds perfectly, does the overall outcome actually change?"
- If no, you've found a non-constraint. Working harder there produces zero.
- The real constraint is usually boring, structural, and mislabelled as someone's personal failing.

### 6. Build the machine, not the output
Mechanism #7.

- Every time you do something a second time, write down how. Third time, automate or delegate it.
- Ask monthly: *"What would keep running if I disappeared for six months?"*
- Personal heroics are linear and end when you do. Systems compound and are inheritable.

### 7. Spend outrageous effort on who you work with
Mechanism #6.

- Choosing your co-founder, your first hires, your manager, your peer group — these decisions dominate almost everything else you can control.
- Selecting well is a one-time cost with permanent returns. Managing badly-selected people is a permanent cost with diminishing returns.

### 8. Send one costly signal
Mechanism #9.

- Pick something that proves commitment because it would be irrational to fake: leave the safe job, publish the unpopular but defensible position under your real name, ship the thing before you feel ready, put your own money in.
- One credible costly signal is worth more than three years of stated intentions.
- **Calibrate:** costly ≠ reckless. The signal should be survivable if it fails. Crossing the Rubicon works; crossing it with no army is just drowning.

## Tier 3 — Long game

### 9. Delete aggressively
Jobs cutting 350 products to 4. Subtraction is the underused operation.
- Quarterly: list your commitments. Cut the bottom 30%. Not the bad ones — the *merely good* ones, which are what actually block the great one.

### 10. Position for the shock you expect
Mechanism #8. Churchill was right about one thing, publicly, and it was enough.
- Identify one high-consequence change you believe is coming that consensus underrates.
- Build capability *now* that pays off enormously if you're right and costs little if you're wrong.
- Cheap option, expensive payoff. Then wait — possibly for years — without spending your resources in the meantime.

### 11. Argue from your opponent's stated values
King's method. Far harder to dismiss than external critique. Instead of "your standard is wrong," say "you are failing your own standard."

### 12. Train your replacement
Mechanism #12. Influence measured over decades is dominated by transmission.
- Teach openly. Give away your best material. The person who taught you was more consequential than the person who out-competed you.

## The 30-Day Version

If you do nothing else:

- **Week 1:** Write down the one problem you'll still be working on in five years.
- **Week 2:** Go and talk to five people at the coalface of that problem. Write down what surprised you.
- **Week 3:** Identify the real bottleneck. Kill one project that isn't it.
- **Week 4:** Publish something specific about your problem, under your name. Then do it again next month, and the month after, for five years.

## How to tell whether any of this is working

The playbook is useless without a feedback loop, and every item above is slow
enough that you will not notice failure for years. These are the leading
indicators — things observable in weeks rather than decades.

| Mechanism | Lagging measure (years) | **Leading indicator (weeks)** |
|---|---|---|
| #1 Information | You knew something the market didn't | **Count of surprises per month.** If nothing surprised you, you weren't in contact with reality — you were in contact with reports |
| #2 Time horizon | A compounding position | **Is this year's work built on last year's, or a restart?** |
| #10 Real constraint | The outcome moved | **Did killing this project change anything?** If not, it was never the constraint |
| #4 Decision velocity | Fewer regrets | **Ratio of decisions made in a day vs a month.** Then check how many of the slow ones were actually reversible |
| #5 Narrative | People use your framing | **Are you using your competitor's vocabulary, or are they using yours?** |
| #6 Selection | The team outperforms | **Hours spent choosing people vs correcting them** |
| #7 Institutions | It runs without you | **What broke during your last week off?** That list is your dependency map |
| #13 Error correction | You caught the error | **Who told you that you were wrong this month, and what happened to them?** |

**The three worth actually tracking**, because they're cheap and they move:

1. **Surprises per month.** Proxy for mechanism #1. Zero is a failure state, not a success one.
2. **Who told you you were wrong.** Proxy for #13. The single best early-warning signal in the notebook.
3. **What broke while you were away.** Proxy for #7. Also the only one that is measured for you, whether you like it or not.

**The trap:** all three are uncomfortable, and the comfortable version of each
("I'm well-informed," "I'm approachable," "the team is self-sufficient") is
available at zero cost and tells you nothing.

## The Honest Warning

Every case in these files paid a **named, specific price** their peers refused to pay:
obscurity (Borlaug), prison (Mandela), unpopularity (Churchill in the 30s),
physical danger (King), monotony (Curie), financial risk (Graham),
or three decades on one problem (Lee Kuan Yew).

The mechanisms above are not hacks. They are descriptions of what it looks
like to accept a cost that other people, with equal talent, decline.

**If a strategy in this file feels comfortable, you've misunderstood it.**

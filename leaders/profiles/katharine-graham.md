# Katharine Graham (1917–2001)

*Publisher of The Washington Post. The notebook's case that institutions are defined by two or three decisions made when the safe option was obvious.*

---

## What she inherited

A newspaper, and almost no confidence.

Her father **Eugene Meyer**, a financier, bought the *Washington Post* at a
bankruptcy auction in 1933 when it was the weakest of Washington's papers. Her
mother Agnes was a formidable, intellectual and emotionally cold woman.

Katharine was raised in enormous wealth and considerable neglect. She worked
briefly as a reporter, then married **Philip Graham**, a brilliant and charming
lawyer. Her father handed control of the paper **to Phil, not to her** — and
Katharine agreed with the decision. In her memoir she records thinking it
natural that a man should run it.

Phil was a talented publisher who bought *Newsweek* and expanded the company.
He also suffered from severe **bipolar disorder**, became abusive, humiliated
her publicly, and had an affair he was open about. In **1963 he killed himself**
at their farm.

**She became publisher at 46, having never held a serious executive job, with
four children, in a state of profound grief, and with a self-confidence that
was — by her own extensive account — close to non-existent.**

Her memoir *Personal History* is remarkable precisely because she does not
retouch this. She describes shaking before meetings, being unable to speak in
groups of men, and assuming she was an interim caretaker.

## Stories worth knowing

**The Pentagon Papers (June 1971).** The first of the two decisions.

Daniel Ellsberg leaked a classified Defense Department history of the Vietnam
War showing that successive administrations had misled the public. The *New York
Times* published first and was **restrained by a federal court injunction** —
the first prior restraint on a newspaper in American history.

The *Post* then obtained a portion of the papers. The decision landed on Graham
in a phone call, at a party at her house, with executives and lawyers on the
line.

The case against publishing was overwhelming:
- The company was **days into its initial public offering.** The underwriting agreement could be voided if criminal prosecution loomed, potentially destroying the offering.
- The *Post* company owned **television licences**, which the government could refuse to renew. This was a substantial share of revenue and a genuine existential threat.
- The *Times* was already under injunction; the legal risk was demonstrated, not theoretical.
- Her lawyers advised against it. Her business executives advised against it.
- The editors — **Ben Bradlee** and the newsroom — threatened mass resignation if she refused.

She asked questions, listened, and said: let's go, let's publish.

*(Reported wording varies; the substance is well attested.)*

The Supreme Court ruled for the newspapers within weeks. But **on the night, she
did not know that.** She made the decision with the downside fully explained to
her and the upside uncertain.

**Watergate (1972–74).** The second decision, and the harder one because it was
not a single night — it was **two years of sustained pressure**.

Two junior metro reporters, **Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein**, followed a
burglary at Democratic headquarters. For long stretches the *Post* was
**effectively alone** on the story. Other major papers did not follow. The White
House denied everything and attacked the paper by name.

The pressure was real and specific:
- Attorney General **John Mitchell**, told of a story about to run, made a crude and threatening remark about Graham personally. Bradlee ran the story with the threat included, minus the crudest word.
- **Challenges were filed against the Post company's Florida television licences** by parties with Nixon connections. The stock fell substantially.
- The administration socially and professionally froze out the paper in a city that runs on access.
- After the 1972 landslide re-election, the story looked, for a period, as though it might simply die and leave the *Post* discredited.

She backed the newsroom throughout. She also, importantly, **asked hard
questions and demanded sourcing** — she was not a rubber stamp. Bradlee's
account is of a publisher who wanted to know exactly how solid each story was
and then took the consequences.

**The business side — the part usually omitted.** She was not only a
custodian.

She took the company public in 1971, professionalised its management, and
recruited seriously. **Warren Buffett** bought a stake in 1973 and became a
mentor and board member; she in turn taught him about newspapers and Washington.
Under her leadership the company's share price performance over the following
decades was, by conventional measures, outstanding.

**The 1975 pressmen's strike** is the counterweight to the heroic narrative. The
pressmen struck, and some sabotaged the presses and assaulted a foreman on the
way out. Graham broke the strike — running the paper with managers and
non-union labour, and ultimately replacing the pressmen's union. It was a
bruising, hard-nosed fight that badly damaged her standing with organised labour.

She was, by then, entirely capable of ruthlessness. **The frightened widow of
1963 is not the person who ran the 1975 strike.**

**Learning in public.** She joined a consciousness-raising conversation with
**Gloria Steinem**; she asked Buffett to teach her to read a balance sheet, and
he wrote her long tutorial letters. In her fifties, running a major corporation,
**she was still openly and systematically learning the job.**

Her memoir won the Pulitzer Prize in 1998, largely because of its honesty about
her own inadequacy.

## How she differed from her contemporaries

Compare with the **New York Times**, which published the Pentagon Papers first
and deserves the primary credit there. On Watergate, however, the *Times* and
most of the national press were slow, and the *Post* was exposed alone for
months. Institutional courage is easier when everyone moves together.

Compare with the **other proprietors of the era**, who largely maintained
comfortable relationships with the administrations they covered. Access is
valuable; antagonism is expensive. Most chose access.

Compare with the **expected behaviour of a caretaker**. Everyone — including
Graham herself, initially — assumed she was holding the seat. A caretaker's
rational choice in June 1971 is unambiguously *don't publish*. **The entire
point is that the safe option was obvious and she took the other one.**

Compare with her father and her husband, both of whom had been handed the paper
on the assumption of competence. She was handed it on the assumption of
incompetence and produced its two defining moments.

## The price

Her name and reputation attacked at the highest level of government. Real
financial jeopardy — the licence challenges and the share price fall were not
symbolic. Social ostracism in a city built on invitations.

Beneath that: decades of a diminished sense of her own capability, a marriage
that ended in suicide, and — in her own telling — years of being treated as
decorative by men who worked for her.

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"Woodward and Bernstein brought down Nixon."** The reporting mattered; so did the Senate committee, Judge Sirica, the FBI, the tapes, and Congress. The *Post*'s role was to keep the story alive when it could easily have died.
- **"She was a passive owner who let Bradlee run wild."** She interrogated the sourcing and carried the consequences. Bradlee was emphatic on this point.
- **"A gentle liberal figure."** The 1975 strike says otherwise. She was a tough proprietor.
- ***The Post*** (2017) compresses and dramatises the timeline considerably.
- **"She was a natural leader who always had it in her."** Her own memoir refuses this reading, which is why it is worth reading.

## The pattern

**Graham is the notebook's clearest evidence that institutions are defined by
two or three decisions made when the safe option was obvious — and that almost
everything else is noise.**

She ran the *Post* for over two decades. The overwhelming majority of that time
was budgets, hiring, printing contracts and circulation. Her legacy rests
almost entirely on a phone call in June 1971 and a sustained refusal to fold
between 1972 and 1974.

Two implications, both practical:

1. **Those moments are recognisable in advance**, because they have a
   characteristic shape: the safe option is obvious, the lawyers advise against,
   the downside is concrete and the upside is a principle. If you are waiting
   for certainty, you will identify them only in hindsight.
2. **Competence is not a prerequisite for the decision.** She had no executive
   training and profound self-doubt. What she had was a clear sense of what the
   institution was *for*, and the willingness to spend the institution's money
   defending it.

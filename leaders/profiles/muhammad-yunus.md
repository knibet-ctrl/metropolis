# Muhammad Yunus (b. 1940)

*Founder of Grameen Bank. The notebook's case for finding the binding constraint when everyone has mislabelled it as a character flaw.*

*Living figure. He has since held political office in Bangladesh, and both microfinance and his own record are actively contested.*

---

## What he inherited

A comfortable background in Chittagong, East Pakistan — his father was a
jeweller. He was academically successful, won a **Fulbright**, took a PhD in
economics at Vanderbilt, and taught at Middle Tennessee State University.

He returned to become head of the economics department at **Chittagong
University** in newly independent Bangladesh — a country that had just emerged
from a war of independence and, in **1974, a devastating famine**.

**That famine is the origin point.** He described the experience of teaching
elegant economic theory in a classroom while people were dying of hunger in the
streets outside as producing a kind of intellectual revulsion — that the
theories he taught had nothing to say about what was happening a hundred metres
away.

**He walked out of the classroom and into the neighbouring village.** That is
mechanism #1, executed literally.

---

## Stories worth knowing

**Jobra, 1976 — the $27.** In the village of Jobra he met a woman making bamboo
stools. She was skilled, worked hard, and remained trapped in poverty.

Investigating why, he found the actual mechanism: she had no capital to buy
bamboo, so she borrowed from a trader who required her to sell the finished
stools back to him at a price he set. Her profit was a few pennies a day. She
was not lazy, unskilled, or badly motivated. **She was short of working capital
and had no access to any lender.**

He and his students surveyed the village and found **42 people** in the same
trap. The total sum required to free all of them was, by his account, about
**US$27**.

He lent it from his own pocket.

**Everyone repaid.**

*(Source note: this is Yunus's own account, told many times. It is well known
and the figures vary slightly across retellings. Treat the origin story as
self-reported.)*

**The insight, stated precisely:** poverty here was not caused by any deficiency
in the poor. It was caused by a **structural absence** — no institution would
lend small sums to people without collateral, because the administrative cost
per loan exceeded the interest, and because bankers believed the poor would not
repay.

The problem had been misdiagnosed as a character flaw for as long as anyone had
bothered to think about it.

**The banks said no.** He then tried to persuade commercial banks to lend to the
poor. They refused, on the grounds that the poor were not creditworthy and could
not fill in forms.

He offered to act as **personal guarantor** — putting his own name behind the
loans. The banks eventually accepted that. Loans were made; they were repaid.

He then had to argue the case for years, expanding district by district, before
**Grameen Bank was established by ordinance in 1983**.

**Replacing collateral with social structure.** The core design innovation. With
no collateral available, Grameen used **group lending**:

- Borrowers form small groups, typically of five, who choose each other.
- Loans go to individuals, but the group's continued access depends on the members' repayment behaviour.
- Weekly meetings, in public, in the village, where repayments are made openly.
- Loans are disbursed in a sequence within the group, so early borrowers demonstrate reliability.

**Social accountability substituted for physical collateral.** People who could
not pledge assets could pledge their standing among neighbours they saw every
week.

**Lending overwhelmingly to women.** Grameen's borrowers became predominantly
women — eventually the great majority. This was initially very difficult in
conservative rural Bangladesh; male family members and religious leaders
objected, and staff had to negotiate access.

The reasoning was empirical rather than ideological: repayment rates were higher,
and money reaching women was more reliably spent on food, health and children's
schooling.

It also had effects beyond finance — women holding capital and attending weekly
public meetings changed household and village dynamics in ways that were not
part of the original design.

**Inverting the branch model.** Conventional banking requires customers to come
to the bank, during working hours, with documentation. Grameen sent staff to the
villages, weekly, on foot and by bicycle. **It moved the institution to the
customer**, which is the same structural move as Ohno's *genchi genbutsu* and
Curie's willingness to go where the work was.

**The Nobel Peace Prize, 2006**, shared between Yunus and Grameen Bank.

## The criticisms — which are substantial

The notebook should be careful here, because microfinance has been through a
full cycle of enthusiasm and correction.

- **Interest rates.** Microfinance rates are high in absolute terms — necessarily, given the cost of servicing tiny loans — but this has been used to fund exploitative lending by other institutions operating under the same label.
- **The Andhra Pradesh crisis (2010).** Aggressive commercial microlenders in India were associated with over-indebtedness and a reported wave of borrower suicides, prompting state intervention. This was not Grameen, but it happened under the banner microfinance had built.
- **Coercive group pressure.** The mechanism that replaces collateral is social shame. Researchers have documented cases where that pressure became harsh, and where women were used as conduits for loans controlled by male relatives.
- **The evidence base.** Randomised controlled trials from the 2010s onwards generally found **modest** effects: some increase in business activity and household autonomy, but **little consistent evidence that microcredit lifts households out of poverty**. This substantially deflated the strongest claims made for it in the 1990s and 2000s.
- **Political conflict.** Yunus was removed from Grameen Bank's leadership in 2011 by the Bangladeshi government, then led by Sheikh Hasina, and subsequently faced a long series of legal cases widely regarded by international observers as politically motivated. He later became head of an interim government in 2024. His record is now entangled with active Bangladeshi politics, and sources on all sides should be read with care.

**The fair summary: he correctly identified a real structural constraint and
built a working institution around it. The claim that microcredit ends poverty
was overstated — by the field, and at times by him — and the evidence has
corrected it.**

## How he differed from his contemporaries

Compare with the **development economics establishment** of the 1970s, which
worked on macro aggregates, industrial policy and aid flows. Yunus went to a
village and asked individual people why they were poor.

**He got a different answer because he asked a different question in a different
place.**

Compare with **commercial banks**, who had concluded the poor were not
creditworthy without ever testing it. Grameen's repayment rates — consistently
reported above 95% — demonstrated that the assumption was simply false. **An
entire industry held a belief for a century that nobody had bothered to check.**

Compare with **conventional charity**, which transfers resources and creates
dependence without changing the structure. Yunus's insistence was that the poor
needed access to capital markets, not gifts — a position that made him
enemies in the aid establishment.

## The price

Years of institutional refusal and personal financial guarantee. Sustained
opposition from banks, from religious conservatives objecting to lending to
women, and later from his own government.

Removal from the institution he founded, followed by prolonged legal
proceedings. And — professionally painful in a different way — watching the
evidence base deflate the largest claims made for his life's work.

---

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"$27 lifted 42 families out of poverty."** It freed them from a specific exploitative arrangement. The origin story is self-reported and has been polished by repetition.
- **"Microcredit ends poverty."** Not supported by the rigorous evidence. It provides useful financial access with modest effects. This overclaim did real damage to the field's credibility.
- **"He invented microcredit."** Credit cooperatives, rotating savings groups and similar structures long predate him. His contribution was a scalable, replicable institutional design and the demonstration that repayment rates would hold.
- **"Grameen is a charity."** It is a bank, majority-owned by its borrowers, that charges interest and expects repayment. That was the entire point.

## The pattern

**Yunus contributes the notebook's clearest instance of mechanism #10: the
binding constraint is often absurdly small, entirely structural, and has been
misdiagnosed by everyone as a personal failing.**

The generalisable procedure:

1. **Go and ask the person with the problem why they have it.** Not a
   representative, not a report — the person.
2. **Distrust any explanation that reduces to character.** "They're lazy,"
   "they don't care," "they lack ambition" are almost always the shape of an
   unexamined structural constraint.
3. **Quantify the constraint.** $27 is a very different problem from "poverty."
4. **Then design a mechanism** that removes it at scale — and make it an
   institution rather than an act of generosity.

And the honest counterweight, which the notebook should keep attached: he ran
the procedure correctly and the result was **real but smaller than claimed**.
Finding a genuine constraint and removing it is necessary. It does not
guarantee the effect will be as large as everyone, including you, wants to
believe.

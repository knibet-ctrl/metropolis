# Mao Zedong (1893–1976)

*Founder of the People's Republic of China. The notebook's extreme case: every mechanism, run at maximum intensity, with the error-correction channel structurally inverted.*

*Studied here as case data. The human cost described below is among the largest attributable to any leader in history.*

---

## Why he is in the notebook

Because he is the strongest available evidence for its most reliable finding.

Mao ran **mechanism #2** (long horizon), **#3** (concentrated bet), **#5**
(narrative control), **#7** (institution-building) and **#12** (successors) with
more intensity and more totality than almost anyone in this collection.

He also **destroyed every channel through which he could be told he was wrong.**

`failure-modes.md` lists information starvation at the top as the most common
killer of previously effective leaders. Mao is that failure mode at the scale of
a fifth of humanity.

**And the control case sits in the same country: Deng ran comparable ambitions
with sandboxing and feedback. Same nation, same party, same century. The
variable is containment and error correction.**

---

## What he inherited

A moderately prosperous peasant family in Hunan and a China in disintegration —
the Qing dynasty collapsing, warlordism, foreign concessions, and a national
humiliation narrative that shaped his entire generation.

He worked as an assistant librarian at Peking University, where he was, by his
own later account, ignored by the intellectuals he admired. That resentment
toward educated elites recurs across his career with considerable consequences.

He was a founding member of the Chinese Communist Party in 1921.

## The achievements, stated plainly

The notebook should not pretend these did not happen.

**The Long March (1934–35).** Encircled by Nationalist forces, the Communists
retreated roughly 6,000 miles over about a year, across mountains and grassland,
with enormous losses. Mao emerged as the dominant leader during it. As an act of
narrative construction it is unmatched — a catastrophic retreat converted into
the founding epic of a state.

**Peasant revolution as a strategic innovation.** Orthodox Marxism located
revolution in the urban industrial proletariat. Mao argued it would come from
the **peasantry**, and organised accordingly — land redistribution, rural base
areas, guerrilla warfare. Against Chinese conditions this was correct, and it
was heterodox enough to be dangerous to say.

**Yan'an and guerrilla doctrine.** His writing on protracted people's war
became genuinely influential worldwide. The discipline he imposed — that the
army must not abuse peasants — was in stark contrast to warlord and Nationalist
behaviour and won substantial rural support.

**Unification (1949).** He ended roughly a century of foreign intrusion,
warlordism and civil war, and established a centralised state.

**The early gains.** Land reform (accompanied by very large numbers of
executions of landlords), literacy campaigns, mass basic healthcare, and
substantial improvements in life expectancy over the period as a whole. Women's
legal status improved sharply — the 1950 Marriage Law banned arranged marriage
and concubinage.

**These are real. They are also why the rest is so instructive: this was not an
incompetent man.**

## The Hundred Flowers Campaign (1956–57) — the hinge

He invited intellectuals to criticise the Party, using the formula about letting
a hundred flowers bloom and a hundred schools of thought contend.

Criticism came — more, and sharper, than expected.

The campaign was then reversed into the **Anti-Rightist Campaign**. Hundreds of
thousands of people who had spoken were labelled rightists, purged, sent to
labour camps, or driven to suicide.

**Whether it was a deliberate trap from the outset or a genuine invitation that
he could not tolerate is debated. The effect is not in dispute.**

From that point, **honest criticism in China carried a demonstrated, personal,
catastrophic cost.** Everyone learned it. The error-correction channel was not
merely closed — it was inverted, so that telling the truth was the dangerous
act and flattery was the safe one.

**Everything that follows is the consequence.**

---

## The Great Leap Forward (1958–1962)

A concentrated national bet: overtake British steel production, collectivise
agriculture totally, industrialise the countryside, all at once.

**The mechanisms, all present:**
- Long horizon — a transformed China within a generation.
- Concentration — the entire nation, one plan, simultaneously.
- Narrative control — total.
- Institution-building — the People's Communes.
- Mass mobilisation — hundreds of millions of people.

**And no containment and no feedback.**

**Backyard furnaces.** Communes were required to produce steel. Peasants melted
down usable farm tools, cooking pots and metal fittings in small clay furnaces.
The output was largely **useless pig iron**. Meanwhile the labour was diverted
from the harvest, and timber was cut for fuel.

**Agricultural pseudoscience.** Practices derived from **Lysenkoist** doctrine
were mandated: close planting (on the theory that plants of the same class do
not compete), deep ploughing to implausible depths, and sowing rates that
destroyed yields.

**The Four Pests campaign.** Sparrows were designated a pest for eating grain,
and the population was mobilised to kill them — banging pots so the birds could
not land until they died of exhaustion. **Sparrows also eat locusts.** Locust
populations exploded. It is one of the purest documented cases of an
intervention on a system nobody had modelled.

**The fatal feedback loop.** Local officials, having seen what happened to
critics in 1957, reported **fictitious harvest figures** — each competing to
report more than the next.

The state then **requisitioned grain based on the false figures**, taking a
proportion of a harvest that did not exist. Villages were stripped of the food
they actually had. Grain was **exported** while people starved, because the
reported surplus said there was plenty.

Peasants were prevented from leaving their communes to look for food.

**When Marshal Peng Dehuai — a Long March veteran and Defence Minister — wrote
Mao a private letter at the Lushan Conference (1959) describing the real
conditions, he was denounced, purged, and later died after years of
persecution.**

That is the moment the notebook keeps returning to. **The last functioning
error-correction channel was a man who had marched 6,000 miles with him, and it
was destroyed for using it.**

**The famine that followed is estimated to have killed tens of millions of
people.** Scholarly estimates vary widely — commonly cited ranges run from
roughly 15 million to over 40 million — and the methodology is contested. It is
among the deadliest famines in recorded history, and it was caused by policy.

## The Cultural Revolution (1966–1976)

Having lost standing after the Great Leap, Mao mobilised **students — the Red
Guards** — against the Party apparatus itself, calling on them to attack the
"four olds" and to bombard the headquarters.

Consequences: schools and universities closed for years; teachers, doctors,
engineers and officials were publicly humiliated in "struggle sessions," beaten,
imprisoned, sent to the countryside, or killed. Temples, libraries, artworks and
historical sites were destroyed. Families were made to denounce each other.
Factional violence approached civil war in some provinces.

**Deng Xiaoping was purged and sent to a tractor factory; his son was thrown
from a window and paralysed.** The man who would rebuild China spent this period
doing manual labour.

**Liu Shaoqi**, the head of state and Mao's designated successor, was denounced,
imprisoned and died in detention, reportedly without adequate medical care.

**Mechanism #12 in its most destructive form: he destroyed his own successors
because they had become a threat.**

## How he differed from Deng — the decisive comparison

This is the most valuable pairing in the notebook, because almost every variable
is held constant: same country, same party, same ideology, same century,
overlapping personnel.

| | Mao | Deng |
|---|---|---|
| Scale of experiment | Entire nation, simultaneously | Four coastal zones, contained |
| If it failed | National catastrophe | Quarantined; abandon and move on |
| Source of policy | Derived from ideology | Ratified from what already worked (Xiaogang) |
| Response to bad news | Purge the messenger (Peng, Liu) | Absorb it; adjust |
| Test of success | Doctrinal correctness | Whether the cat catches mice |
| Succession | Destroyed every heir | Term limits; designated two generations |

**Deng ran the same ambition with a sandbox and a feedback loop. That is the
entire difference, and it is measured in tens of millions of lives.**

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **Casualty figures are contested.** Ranges for the Great Leap famine vary substantially across scholarship (Frank Dikötter, Yang Jisheng, Judith Banister and others use different methods and reach different totals). The magnitude is catastrophic under every serious estimate; specific numbers should be cited with their source.
- **"He intended the famine."** Most historians describe a leader who created conditions making catastrophe near-inevitable, then refused to acknowledge it and continued requisitioning and exporting grain once it was underway. Whether that constitutes intent is a genuine historiographical dispute; the culpability is not seriously contested.
- **"He achieved nothing."** Unification, land reform, literacy, basic healthcare and improved life expectancy over his rule as a whole are real. The notebook is more useful, not less, for holding both facts.
- **"The Cultural Revolution was chaos he lost control of."** He initiated it deliberately, and deployed and later demobilised the Red Guards, sending millions of them to the countryside.
- **The official Chinese assessment** — the 1981 Party resolution, which judged him substantially correct and partly mistaken — is a political document produced by the institution he founded.

## The pattern

**Mao is `failure-modes.md` at maximum scale, and he establishes the notebook's
single most reliable finding.**

Three conclusions:

1. **An error-correction channel is not a management nicety. It is the
   difference between the success sample and the failure sample.** Lincoln kept
   rivals in the room. Toyota hung a cord above every workstation. Amazon
   legitimised disagreement before the decision. Genghis promoted the man who
   had shot at him. Mao purged Peng Dehuai, and the result was a famine.

2. **The mechanisms are amplifiers with no sign attached.** Narrative control,
   concentration, long horizons and institution-building are as available to
   catastrophe as to achievement. **What determines the sign is whether
   mechanism #1 — contact with the truth — is intact.** Mao's was not merely
   weak; it was structurally inverted, so that the system generated confident
   falsehood at scale.

3. **Success is what destroys the information supply.** He was not deluded in
   1935. Deference accumulated with victory until, by 1958, no one in China
   could safely tell him a harvest figure was wrong. **The failure mode arrives
   *after* the achievements, which is precisely why it is so hard to see coming
   — and why every durable leader in this collection built a deliberate
   counter-mechanism.**

**If nobody has told you that you are wrong this month, your information supply
is broken.** Mao is what that looks like when the person is running a country
and it has been broken for twenty years.

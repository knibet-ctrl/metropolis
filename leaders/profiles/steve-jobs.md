# Steve Jobs (1955–2011)

*Co-founder of Apple, Pixar, NeXT. The notebook's case for subtraction as a strategic operation.*

---

## What he inherited

Adopted at birth in California. His biological mother required that his adoptive
parents be college graduates; the Jobses were not, and had to promise he would
attend college. He did — **Reed College for one semester** — then dropped out
and audited classes that interested him, including a **calligraphy** course he
later credited for the Mac's typography.

He grew up in what became Silicon Valley, with a father who restored cars in the
garage and — by Jobs's account — insisted that the unseen back of a cabinet be
finished as carefully as the front. That story is a family anecdote and does
real work in his self-mythology; treat it accordingly.

He was not the engineer. **Steve Wozniak** designed the Apple I and II. Jobs's
contribution was seeing that the machine could be a **product** — a thing with a
case, a price, a market, and a story — rather than a hobbyist board.

## Stories worth knowing

**Getting fired (1985).** After the Macintosh underperformed commercially and a
power struggle with CEO **John Sculley** — whom Jobs had personally recruited
from PepsiCo — the board sided with Sculley and Jobs was stripped of operational
responsibility. He left.

He described it later as the best thing that could have happened to him, on the
grounds that being thrown out replaced the heaviness of success with the
lightness of being a beginner again.

**The wilderness was the most productive period of his life**, which is the part
usually rushed past:
- He founded **NeXT**. Commercially it largely failed. Its **NeXTSTEP** operating system, however, became the foundation of macOS and iOS. **Tim Berners-Lee built the first web browser and server on a NeXT machine.**
- He bought the computer graphics division of Lucasfilm for a few million dollars and turned it into **Pixar**. He funded it personally for years while it lost money. *Toy Story* (1995) made him a billionaire.

He was gone from Apple for twelve years. **He came back with an operating
system, a fortune, and — by most accounts — a substantially improved ability to
work with people.**

**The return and the deletion (1997).** Apple was reportedly weeks from
insolvency. Jobs returned as interim CEO and made a series of subtractive moves:

- **Killed the Newton**, the printer line, and a large share of peripherals.
- **Ended the Macintosh clone licensing programme**, which was cannibalising Apple's own margins.
- Reduced a sprawling and confusing product line to a **four-quadrant grid**: consumer and professional, desktop and portable. Four products.
- Took a **$150 million investment from Microsoft** and settled the patent disputes — announcing it on stage at Macworld to audible booing from Apple loyalists, and telling them directly that the notion Apple had to lose for Microsoft to win was over.

*Claims note: the widely cited "350 products down to 10" figure is unreliable.
The four-quadrant grid is well documented; the surrounding numbers vary by
source.*

**The principle he articulated:** that focus means saying no to the hundred
other good ideas.

**The distinction matters.** Saying no to bad ideas is trivial. The hard,
scarce act is killing *good* projects with real advocates and real potential,
because they dilute the great one. **Subtraction is the underused operation in
almost every organisation.**

**"Think Different" (1997).** Before shipping any new product, he ran a brand
campaign. The reasoning: Apple's most valuable asset in 1997 was not technology
but what the name meant, and that had to be repaired before anything could be
sold.

**The iPod and "1,000 songs in your pocket" (2001).** MP3 players existed and
were bad. Apple's contribution was the integration — hardware, the iTunes
software, and later the store with negotiated label deals — plus a
specification stated as a **user benefit rather than a technical figure**.
Nobody remembers "5GB hard drive."

**The iPhone (2007).** The strategically significant part is that it
**cannibalised the iPod**, then Apple's most profitable product, at its peak.

Most companies cannot do this. Kodak could not. Nokia could not. The willingness
to destroy your own most successful product before a competitor does is rare
because it requires overruling everyone whose position depends on it.

**The reality distortion field.** Coined by colleagues at Apple in the early
1980s, only half-affectionately. He would insist that something impossible was
possible, with total conviction, until people did it.

**Bud Tribble** and others describe engineers achieving things they had proved
were impossible. **Larry Kenyon** was reportedly told that the Mac's boot time
mattered because, multiplied across millions of users, seconds saved equalled
lifetimes.

It cut both ways. The same field produced impossible deadlines, publicly
humiliated staff, and — most seriously — his own **nine-month delay in accepting
surgery** for pancreatic cancer in 2003 while pursuing alternative treatments.
His biographer records his own later regret. **The trait that made him
extraordinary appears to have shortened his life.**

**Pixar and the other model.** Worth noting because it complicates the
caricature. At Pixar he largely stayed out of the creative process and left
**John Lasseter** and **Ed Catmull** to run it. Catmull built a genuine
error-correction system — the **"Braintrust,"** a peer review body with no
authority to mandate changes, designed so that candid criticism could be given
without threat.

**Pixar had the feedback mechanism that `failure-modes.md` identifies as
decisive, and Apple under Jobs largely did not.** He was capable of both modes.

## How he differed from his contemporaries

Compare with **Bill Gates**, whose strategy was to be everywhere: licence the OS
to every manufacturer, maximise share, accept variable quality. It won the PC
era decisively. Jobs insisted on controlling hardware and software together,
which nearly killed Apple in the 1990s and then won completely in mobile, where
integration mattered more.

**Neither was wrong. The environment changed and one bet paid off later.** That
is a luck-and-timing point the notebook should not gloss over.

Compare with **John Sculley**, a highly competent marketing executive who ran
Apple by market research and product proliferation, and presided over its
decline. Jobs's stated contempt for asking customers what they wanted is a real
methodological difference, and it is only correct in specific circumstances —
when the customer cannot imagine the option.

Compare with **Wozniak**, the better engineer, who has said plainly that he
would not have made the company happen. **Technology and product are different
skills, and the notebook's builder section keeps demonstrating that the second
is rarer.**

## The price

Paid substantially by people around him. Well-documented cruelty to employees;
public humiliations; taking credit for others' work. He initially **denied
paternity of his daughter Lisa**, including under oath, while Apple was going
public, and later acknowledged and regretted it. His relationship with Wozniak
was damaged early by his dishonesty over an Atari contract payment.

His own: he died at 56. The nine-month delay before surgery is the clearest case
in the notebook of a strength inverting into a fatal weakness.

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"He invented the personal computer / MP3 player / smartphone."** None of these. He integrated and productised existing categories better than anyone.
- **"350 products to 10."** Number unreliable. The four-quadrant grid is real.
- **"He was fired in 1985."** He was stripped of responsibilities and resigned. Functionally a firing, formally not.
- **"Cruelty was necessary."** The most damaging myth attached to him, and the one most imitated. Tim Cook has produced comparable results without it, and Pixar's Braintrust shows Jobs himself knew the other method worked.
- **"He never did market research."** Apple has always done extensive customer research. He rejected *design by committee*, which is a different claim.
- **The Stanford commencement speech (2005)** is superb and is also a carefully constructed piece of self-narrative delivered by someone who had recently been diagnosed with cancer.

## The pattern

**Jobs is the notebook's argument for subtraction.**

Almost every leader adds. Adding is safe, popular, and looks like progress:
another product, another initiative, another feature. It is also how
organisations become incoherent.

He deleted. Aggressively, publicly, and against internal resistance — including
deleting his own most profitable product when the next thing was better.

The transferable version is uncomfortable and specific: **the things blocking
your great outcome are not the bad projects. They are the merely good ones**,
which have credible advocates, real revenue, and no obvious reason to die.

And the warning the notebook must attach: he is the most-imitated figure in
modern business and the imitation almost always copies the wrong thing. People
copy the rudeness and the certainty, which were his failure modes. The
transferable parts are focus, integration, narrative, and — from Pixar — a
structured mechanism for being told you are wrong.

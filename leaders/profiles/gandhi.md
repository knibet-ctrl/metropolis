# Mohandas K. Gandhi (1869–1948)

*Leader of Indian independence. The notebook's central case for changing the battlefield rather than fighting harder on it.*

---

## What he inherited

A comfortable merchant-caste family in Gujarat; his father was a local official.
Not poor, not powerful. He was an unremarkable student, married at thirteen by
arrangement, and sent to London to study law.

He was, by his own account, **a failure as a barrister in India** — he froze in
court during his first case, unable to speak, and had to return the fee. He took
a job in South Africa largely because nothing else was working.

**The formative event (1893).** Aged 23, holding a first-class ticket, he was
thrown off a train at **Pietermaritzburg** for refusing to move to third class.
He spent the night in the cold waiting room deciding whether to return to India
or stay and fight. He stayed — and remained in South Africa for **21 years**.

That interval is easy to skip past. He developed and tested his entire method
over two decades on a smaller stage before ever applying it to India. **He did
not arrive at Indian independence with a theory; he arrived with a tested
operating procedure.**

---

## Stories worth knowing

**Satyagraha as a procedure, not a sentiment.** This is the part that gets lost
in the sainthood. Non-violent resistance in his hands was a **repeatable
protocol** with defined steps:

1. **Investigate and negotiate first.** He always tried ordinary petition first, and documented the refusal.
2. **Pick a symbol everyone understands** — not an abstraction.
3. **Announce the action in advance**, including to the authorities. He wrote to the Viceroy before the Salt March explaining exactly what he intended.
4. **Break the law openly and non-violently.** Invite arrest.
5. **Absorb the violence without retaliating**, in front of witnesses.
6. **Force the opponent to choose** between conceding and being seen — by their own public and the world — as brutal.
7. **Train participants.** Volunteers were drilled in taking blows without responding. This was not spontaneous.

Step 7 is what makes it a *technology*. An untrained crowd retaliates, and the
moment it does the entire mechanism inverts.

**The Salt March (12 March – 6 April 1930).** Chosen with enormous care. The
British salt monopoly taxed a substance that anyone could scoop from the sea —
which meant the tax fell on the poorest, was easy to explain, and was
indefensible in a sentence.

He walked roughly 240 miles from Sabarmati to **Dandi** with a growing column,
journalists in attendance the entire way, stopping to speak in villages. At the
sea he picked up a lump of natural salt and, by the widely reported account,
said: *"With this, I am shaking the foundations of the British Empire."*

Then millions of people made salt illegally. Around 60,000 were arrested,
including Gandhi.

**Dharasana (May 1930).** The follow-up, and the moment the mechanism was most
starkly visible. Volunteers advanced in columns on the Dharasana salt works and
were beaten down by police with steel-tipped lathis, without raising a hand.
Column after column advanced.

The United Press correspondent **Webb Miller** filed a report that ran
worldwide, describing the sound of clubs on unprotected skulls and men going
down without a cry. That single dispatch did more damage to the moral case for
the Raj than any amount of argument.

**He engineered a situation in which the opponent's strength was the thing that
destroyed them.**

**The Champaran indigo campaign (1917).** His first Indian action, and worth
knowing because it is the unglamorous version. He went to Bihar, took detailed
testimony from thousands of exploited indigo farmers, was ordered to leave,
refused, and was arrested. The resulting inquiry produced real legal relief.
This is mechanism #1 — direct contact with reality, at scale, on foot.

**The spinning wheel (*charkha*).** He asked Indians to spin and wear
*khadi* — homespun cloth — and to boycott British textiles. This did several
things simultaneously: it attacked British economic interest at a real pressure
point, gave every person including the poorest and women a **daily physical act
of participation**, and produced a visible uniform for the movement.

It is a masterpiece of movement design: a single action that is cheap,
repeatable, economically meaningful, and identity-forming.

**The fasts.** He used fasting as a coercive instrument — most seriously in 1932
against separate electorates for Dalits, and in 1947–48 to stop communal
massacres. The 1947 Calcutta fast is credited with genuinely halting rioting
where troops had failed.

*This is also his most criticised tactic.* **B. R. Ambedkar**, the Dalit leader,
regarded the 1932 fast as moral blackmail — Gandhi threatening to die in order
to force Ambedkar to abandon a hard-won political protection for the most
oppressed people in India. Ambedkar conceded and never forgave him. The
criticism is serious and should not be smoothed over.

**Personal consistency as the source of authority.** He gave away his property,
travelled third class, cleaned latrines himself — deliberately, because that
work was reserved for untouchables — and lived in a way that made him
impossible to accuse of self-interest. His authority was not institutional. He
held no office. **It was entirely constructed from consistency between stated
values and observable daily behaviour**, which is the cheapest and rarest form
of legitimacy.

## How he differed from his contemporaries

Compare with the **Indian National Congress moderates** — Gokhale, and earlier
figures — who petitioned, wrote memoranda, and argued the constitutional case
in English to English officials. They were educated, reasonable, and entirely
ignorable, because they had **no mechanism for imposing a cost**.

Compare with the **revolutionary nationalists** — Bhagat Singh, the
Anushilan Samiti. Brave, and they gave the Raj exactly the enemy it knew how to
fight. Political violence justified emergency powers and let the state occupy
the moral high ground.

Compare with **Jinnah**, an abler lawyer and constitutional tactician, who
operated through elite negotiation. Gandhi's advantage was **mass
participation**: he converted independence from a debate among lawyers into
something a villager could take part in on a Tuesday.

Compare with **Ambedkar**, who argued that Gandhi's approach left caste
oppression fundamentally intact and that political independence without social
revolution would betray the Dalits. This critique has aged well and is now
central to how Gandhi is assessed in India.

## The price

Roughly six years in prison across his life. Repeated fasts that came close to
killing him. He was assassinated on 30 January 1948 by **Nathuram Godse**, a
Hindu nationalist who believed he had been too conciliatory toward Muslims.

He also lived to see his central goal fail in the way that mattered most to him:
independence came with **Partition**, communal massacres on an enormous scale,
and the displacement of millions. He did not attend the independence
celebrations. He was in Calcutta, fasting, trying to stop the killing.

## Where the popular story is wrong

This section is important, because the sanitised Gandhi is not useful and is not
honest.

- **"Non-violence works against anyone."** He himself acknowledged the limits, and his suggestions about how Jews might resist Nazism through non-violence are widely regarded as his worst moral misjudgement. **Mechanism #11 requires an opponent constrained by public opinion.** Against a regime willing to kill everyone with no witnesses, it fails.
- **His early South African writing about Black Africans was racist**, and he campaigned for Indians to be treated as distinct from and above them. He changed considerably over time, but the record exists and is being actively reassessed.
- **His treatment of his wife Kasturba and his sons** was harsh; his eldest son Harilal became estranged and alcoholic. His experiments in *brahmacharya*, which involved sleeping beside young women including his grand-niece to test his celibacy, were disturbing to his own contemporaries and remain indefensible.
- **"He single-handedly won independence."** The Second World War bankrupted Britain, the 1946 naval mutinies frightened it, the Labour government was disposed to leave, and Bose's INA and the revolutionaries applied pressure of a different kind. Gandhi was a major cause among several.
- **The train incident** is real but became a founding myth, polished by retelling.

## The pattern

**Gandhi is the notebook's proof that you can beat a stronger opponent by
changing what the fight is about.**

The British had a monopoly on organised violence in India. He therefore ensured
the contest was never about violence — it was about **legitimacy**, where he
held the advantage and they could not use their strength without losing.

The general form: identify the dimension where your opponent's principal asset
becomes a liability, and move the contest there. It requires witnesses,
discipline, and a willingness to absorb real harm — which is why almost nobody
executes it, and why the ones who do are remembered for a century.

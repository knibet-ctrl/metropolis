# Grace Hopper (1906–1992)

*Computer scientist and US Navy Rear Admiral. The notebook's case for betting that a technology will become accessible to non-experts.*

---

## What she inherited

An unusually supportive start for a woman in mathematics in that era. Her
father, who lost both legs to circulatory disease, insisted his daughters be
educated to support themselves. Her great-grandfather had been a rear admiral,
which she cited when explaining her attachment to the Navy.

At seven she dismantled **seven alarm clocks** to find out how they worked. Her
mother restricted her to one.

She took a mathematics and physics degree at Vassar and a **PhD in mathematics
from Yale in 1934** — a genuinely rare achievement for a woman at the time — and
taught at Vassar.

**She was 37 when she joined the Navy** in 1943, after Pearl Harbor. She had to
obtain a waiver: she was over the age limit and, at around 105 pounds,
underweight for her height. She was assigned to the **Bureau of Ordnance
Computation Project at Harvard**, working on the Mark I.

---

## Stories worth knowing

**The Mark I.** A 51-foot, five-ton electromechanical calculator. Hopper was
among its first programmers and wrote its 561-page manual — one of the first
substantial pieces of computing documentation ever produced.

Her commanding officer, **Howard Aiken**, greeted her by asking why she hadn't
been there sooner and instructing her to compute the coefficients of the arc
tangent series by the following week.

**The bug (9 September 1947).** The team found a **moth** trapped in a relay of
the Mark II, removed it, and taped it into the logbook with the note that it was
the first actual case of a bug being found.

**The correction that matters:** "bug" for a technical fault was already old
engineering slang — Edison used it in the 1870s. **The joke only works because
the term already existed.** Hopper popularised the story, and the logbook page
with the moth is preserved. She told it with precision and enjoyed it.

**The compiler (A-0, 1952) — and the resistance.** This is the important part.

In the early 1950s, programming meant writing machine code — numeric
instructions specific to one machine. The prevailing assumption was that this
was inherent to computing, and that computers were tools for mathematicians.

Hopper's conviction was that **the computer should do the translation work
itself**: a programmer should write something closer to human notation, and a
program should convert it to machine instructions.

Her account of the reception: she was told for roughly three years that
computers could only do arithmetic, that they could not write their own
programs, and that the idea was not worth pursuing. She built it anyway.

*Technical caveat, from the notebook's claims audit: the **A-0 system** is often
called the first compiler, but by modern definitions it functioned more like a
loader/linker, assembling subroutines from a tape library. The **A-2** and later
**FLOW-MATIC** are closer to what we now mean. The superlative is contested; the
significance is not.*

**FLOW-MATIC (1955–59) — programming in English.** Her next argument was more
radical still: that business users should be able to write instructions in
something resembling **English words**.

The objection was that computers could not understand English. Her response —
that they could not understand mathematical symbols either, they were simply
being taught symbols first — is the whole insight in one line.

FLOW-MATIC used statements resembling ordinary English commands. It was the
first programming language of its kind, and it became the primary basis for
**COBOL**.

**COBOL (1959–60).** Hopper was a technical adviser and her FLOW-MATIC work was
the principal foundation. COBOL was designed so that business people — not
mathematicians — could read and write programs, and so that programs could run
on machines from **different manufacturers**.

That second point is enormous. COBOL was an early and deliberate push for
**machine independence** at a time when manufacturers had every commercial
interest in locking customers to their hardware.

**COBOL is still running.** Very large volumes of banking, insurance and
government transaction processing still run on COBOL systems. It is the least
fashionable and among the most consequential languages ever written.

**The nanosecond.** Her most famous teaching device. Asked to explain why
satellite communication had unavoidable delay, she began handing out lengths of
wire about **11.8 inches** long — the distance light travels in a nanosecond.

She gave out thousands over the years. She contrasted them with a coil of wire
representing a microsecond — nearly a thousand feet — to show generals and
executives exactly how much time they were wasting with inefficient code.

**She turned an abstract physical constant into an object people could hold.**
It is one of the finest pieces of technical communication anyone has devised.

**Her rule about permission.** Her best-known line — that it is easier to
apologise than to get permission — she meant operationally, and she taught it.
It is mechanism #4 stated in six words, from someone who spent a career inside a
military bureaucracy.

She also kept a **clock on her wall that ran backwards**, to make the point that
things being done a certain way was not a reason to continue.

Her stated view on the most dangerous phrase in the language — *"we've always
done it that way"* — was consistent enough across decades to be a genuine
operating principle rather than a slogan.

**The retirements.** She retired from the Navy in 1966, was recalled within
months, and the recall kept being extended. She finally retired in **1986, aged
79**, as the oldest serving officer in the US Navy, in a ceremony aboard the
USS Constitution.

She was promoted to **Rear Admiral** in 1985. She then worked as a senior
consultant for Digital Equipment Corporation into her eighties, giving up to
several hundred lectures a year.

A destroyer, the **USS Hopper**, is named for her — one of very few US warships
named for a woman. She received the **Presidential Medal of Freedom** in 2016,
posthumously.

## How she differed from her contemporaries

Compare with the prevailing view of the 1950s, which held that programming was
an activity for a small number of trained mathematicians, and that machine
efficiency mattered more than programmer productivity.

She bet the opposite: that **the number of programmers would matter more than
the efficiency of any one program**, and therefore that lowering the barrier was
the highest-leverage move.

**That bet has won in every technology cycle since** — assembly to high-level
languages, command line to GUI, on-premises to cloud, and now natural-language
interfaces. It is arguably the most reliable recurring pattern in computing
history, and she called it first.

Compare with the manufacturers, who resisted machine independence because
lock-in was profitable. She pushed standards that reduced their power.

Compare with **John von Neumann** and the era's celebrated theorists. They
worked on what computers could *do*. She worked on **who could use them** —
which was regarded as the lesser question and turned out to be the one that
determined the industry's scale.

## The price

Being disbelieved for years by people with more authority. A career spent inside
two of the most conservative institutions available — the military and large
enterprise computing — pushing changes both resisted.

She was also, for much of her career, the only woman in the room, and her
contributions were routinely folded into institutional credit. Her marriage
ended in divorce; she kept the name.

Her price is not prison or poverty. It is the quieter one: **decades of
sustained argument against people who were certain and wrong.**

---

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"She wrote the first compiler."** Contested by modern definitions. A-0 was closer to a linking loader. Her significance rests on the *idea* and on FLOW-MATIC/COBOL.
- **"She coined the term 'bug'."** No. The term predates her by decades. She popularised the moth story, and never claimed to have invented the word.
- **"She invented COBOL."** She was a principal influence and technical adviser; the language was designed by a committee (CODASYL), with **Jean Sammet** and others central to the specification.
- **"A charming eccentric."** The wire and the clock were deliberate rhetorical instruments deployed by someone who understood that a good idea without transmission is worthless.

## The pattern

**Hopper contributes the notebook's most reliable forward-looking bet: in any
technology, the move that makes it usable by non-experts wins.**

The reasoning is structural rather than sentimental. If a capability is
restricted to specialists, its total value is capped by the number of
specialists. Remove the barrier and the value scales with the whole population.
That is why every abstraction layer in computing history has won, and it is why
the people who build them capture the standard.

The second lesson is mechanism #5, and she is the best exemplar in the builder
set: **she manufactured physical objects to carry an abstract idea.** Nobody
forgets a nanosecond after holding one. Compare Rockefeller, who was the most
capable industrialist of his age and never bothered to explain himself — and let
a journalist define him for a century.

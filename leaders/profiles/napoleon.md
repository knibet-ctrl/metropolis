# Napoleon Bonaparte (1769–1821)

*French general and emperor. The notebook's case study in a leader with no stopping rule.*

---

## What he inherited

Minor Corsican nobility — genuinely minor, and Corsica had been French for only
a year when he was born. He spoke Italian first, was mocked at the French
military academy at Brienne for his accent and poverty, and was a scholarship
boy among aristocrats.

He was commissioned into the **artillery** — the least glamorous, most
mathematical branch, and the one most open to talent rather than birth. This
detail matters: artillery required calculation, and he became a leader who
thought quantitatively about logistics, ranges and march rates while his
opponents thought about honour.

The **Revolution** removed the aristocratic officer corps almost overnight,
creating an unprecedented promotion vacuum. He was 24 when he made his name at
the siege of **Toulon** (1793) and a general shortly after. He is, in the
notebook's terms, the clearest case of someone **positioned to exploit a shock**
rather than someone who created one.

## Stories worth knowing

**The corps system — the real innovation.** Armies of the period marched and
fought as a single body, because a detached fragment could be destroyed
piecemeal.

Napoleon organised his army into **corps**: self-contained formations of roughly
20,000–30,000 men, each with its own infantry, cavalry, artillery and staff —
each capable of **fighting a superior enemy alone for a day or more** until the
others arrived.

The consequence is a genuine phase change. His army could **march dispersed
across a wide front** (faster movement, easier foraging, multiple threatened
axes, enemy uncertain where the blow falls) and then **concentrate for battle**.
The famous phrase is "march divided, fight united."

At **Ulm (1805)** he used this to envelop an entire Austrian army and force its
surrender with barely a battle. The campaign, not the fight, won it.

*Correction to the popular account: he did not invent this. Divisional and corps
organisation had been developed in French military thought by Bourcet, Guibert
and others, and used in the Revolutionary armies. Napoleon **systematised and
exploited** it more ruthlessly than anyone. Credit for execution, not invention.*

**Austerlitz (2 December 1805) — his masterpiece.** He deliberately weakened his
own right flank and abandoned the commanding **Pratzen Heights**, presenting the
Austro-Russian army with an apparently irresistible opportunity to cut him off
from Vienna. He even opened negotiations to appear anxious.

They took the bait, shifted mass off the heights to attack his right — and he
drove **Soult's corps up through the middle**, seizing the heights and splitting
their army in two.

He won by **engineering his opponent's decision**, not by out-fighting them.
That is the highest form of the craft.

**The Code Napoléon (1804).** He reportedly said, near the end of his life, that
his true glory was not his forty battles — Waterloo had erased their memory —
but his civil code, which would live forever.

He was right, and it is the most important thing he did. The Code established
equality before the law, secured property rights, abolished feudal privilege,
and provided a clear, written, rational civil law replacing a patchwork of local
custom. It spread across Europe with his armies and **survived his defeat**,
forming the basis of civil law in France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Italy,
Spain, Latin America, Louisiana, and beyond.

He attended many of the drafting sessions personally and argued the details.

*It also entrenched the legal subordination of women, sharply restricting
married women's property and legal rights — a regression on the Revolution.*

**The meritocracy — "careers open to talents."** He promoted marshals from
extraordinary origins: **Murat**, an innkeeper's son; **Ney**, a barrel-maker's
son; **Lannes**, a stable-hand. The Legion of Honour rewarded service rather
than birth. In an aristocratic Europe this was a genuine structural advantage:
his talent pool was the whole population and his opponents' was a caste.

**The other institutions.** The Bank of France, the *lycée* system, the
*Concordat* with the Church, the *préfet* administrative structure, standardised
national records. **The administrative skeleton of modern France is his.**

**Russia (1812) — the failure.** He invaded with an army of roughly 600,000. The
Russians refused decisive battle and retreated, burning as they went. He reached
Moscow and found it abandoned and then burning. He waited five weeks for a
surrender offer that never came, because **he had no theory of what to do if the
enemy simply declined to lose.**

The retreat destroyed the army — cold, starvation, typhus, Cossacks. A small
fraction returned as an organised force.

The root cause is not weather. It is that **there was no defined victory
condition.** Every campaign was justified by the need to secure the last one.
See `failure-modes.md` — "no stopping rule" is the notebook's most reliable
destroyer, and this is its textbook case.

**The Hundred Days (1815).** Escaping Elba, he landed in France with about a
thousand men. Troops sent to arrest him at Grenoble found him walking toward
them alone, opening his coat: *"If any of you will shoot his Emperor, here I
am."* They joined him. He reached Paris without a shot fired.

Then Waterloo, a hundred days later.

## How he differed from his contemporaries

Compare with the **coalition generals** — Mack, the Austrians, the Prussians of
1806. They fought by eighteenth-century convention: manoeuvre for position,
protect supply depots, accept battle when the odds looked right, negotiate.
Napoleon sought **annihilation of the enemy army** as the object, moved at
roughly twice their operational speed, and lived off the country instead of
depending on magazines.

They were not stupid; they were playing the previous game.

Compare with **Wellington**, who eventually beat him — and who was in most
respects his opposite: defensive, meticulous about supply, careful with his
army's lives, politically obedient, and utterly without grandiosity. Wellington
never over-extended. **The man who beat Napoleon was the man who knew when to
stop.**

Compare with **Carnot**, the "organiser of victory," who built the Revolutionary
armies Napoleon inherited. Napoleon's genius was partly in recognising what he
had been handed.

## The price

Millions of dead across two decades of European war. His own two exiles. He died
at 51 on **St Helena**, a remote South Atlantic island, of stomach cancer
(arsenic theories persist and are not well supported). His son was raised in
Austria under a different name and died at 21.

His empire lasted about a decade. The Code lasts still.

---

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"He was short."** He was around 5'6"–5'7" — average or slightly above for the period. The myth comes from confusion between French and English inches, from the tall grenadiers of his guard who surrounded him, and from British caricature.
- **"He invented the corps system."** He systematised existing French doctrine.
- **"Defeated by the Russian winter."** The 1812 army was already largely destroyed by disease, desertion, heat and supply failure *before* the worst cold. The winter finished a corpse.
- **"A revolutionary."** He crowned himself Emperor, reinstated a nobility, reintroduced slavery in the French colonies in 1802 after the Revolution had abolished it, and rolled back women's legal rights.

## The pattern

Napoleon is the notebook's **two-sided case**, and the most instructive one.

The durable half is entirely mechanism #7: the Code, the bank, the schools, the
administrative structure. Boring, institutional, still operating two centuries
later.

The perishable half is everything he is famous for. Forty battles, an empire,
and nothing left.

**He is the strongest evidence in the notebook for the proposition that the
institution-builder beats the genius — because in his case both were the same
person, and we can see which half survived.**

And the warning: escalation without a defined end state is not ambition. It is a
mechanism for converting every victory into the justification for the next
campaign, until one of them is Russia.

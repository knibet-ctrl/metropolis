# Henry Ford (1863–1947)

*Founder of Ford Motor Company. In the notebook for the $5 day — and as a warning about what unchecked success does to a person.*

---

## What he inherited

A Michigan farm and a strong dislike of farming. His mother died when he was
twelve; his father expected him to take over the farm and he left for Detroit at
sixteen to work as a machinist's apprentice.

He was given a **pocket watch** at thirteen and took it apart. He became known
locally as a boy who could repair watches, and he later described machinery as
the thing that made sense to him.

He worked as an engineer at the **Edison Illuminating Company**, building
gasoline engines in his spare time in a shed. Thomas Edison encouraged him
personally at a company dinner in 1896 — a moment Ford recounted for the rest of
his life.

**His first two car companies failed.** The Detroit Automobile Company folded;
the Henry Ford Company ejected him (it became Cadillac). He founded Ford Motor
Company in 1903, on his third attempt, aged forty.

---

## Stories worth knowing

**The Model T (1908) and the decision to make one car.** The automobile industry
of the era was a luxury craft business: cars were built individually, to order,
for the wealthy.

Ford's conviction was that the market was not the rich but **everyone** — and
that reaching everyone required a car that was cheap, simple, durable, and
repairable by a farmer with basic tools. High ground clearance for dirt roads.
Vanadium steel for strength and light weight. Few parts, easily replaced.

He then refused to change it for **nineteen years**. The line about customers
having any colour so long as it is black relates to the fastest-drying paint
available at the scale he required.

The Model T's price fell from around $850 in 1908 to under $300 by the
mid-1920s — while wages rose. **He was running Rockefeller's game in a
different industry: relentless cost reduction on a single standardised product.**

**The moving assembly line (1913).** Ford did not invent interchangeable parts,
division of labour, or the assembly line as a concept. What Highland Park did
was integrate them at scale and **bring the work to the worker**.

The reported inspiration is the **"disassembly line"** of the Chicago
meatpacking plants, where carcasses moved on overhead trolleys past stationary
workers each making one cut. Ford's engineers — **Charles Sorensen**, **Clarence
Avery**, **William Klann** and others — reversed it.

The chassis assembly time fell dramatically, commonly cited as from around
twelve hours to roughly ninety minutes.

*Credit note: this was a team engineering achievement. Ford's contribution was
the demand that it be done and the willingness to fund the experiments.*

**The $5 day (5 January 1914) — and what it was actually for.**

This is the item the notebook flagged as its most likely factual error, so it
needs stating carefully.

Ford roughly **doubled** the basic wage to $5 for an eight-hour day (from about
$2.34 for nine hours). Thousands of men queued outside the plant in winter. Wall
Street was appalled; the *Wall Street Journal* called it an economic crime.

**The popular explanation — that he did it so his workers could buy his cars —
is essentially a myth**, one Ford himself later encouraged. As a direct business
case it does not work: his own employees could never constitute a meaningful
share of his market.

**The actual reason was turnover.** The assembly line had made the work
monotonous and brutal, and Ford's annual labour turnover had reached
extraordinary levels — figures around 370% are commonly cited, meaning the
company had to hire many times its workforce each year just to stay level. The
cost of continuously recruiting and training was enormous.

The $5 day cut turnover dramatically, let him select the best workers from
queues of applicants, raised productivity, and reduced absenteeism. **It paid
for itself.** Economists now treat it as a foundational example of
**efficiency wages** — paying above market rate to raise productivity and
retention.

**The transferable insight is better than the myth:** he found a move where a
single apparent cost bought him **three assets at once** — lower turnover,
better selection, higher output — plus, as a bonus, enormous favourable
publicity.

**The Sociological Department — the ugly half.** The $5 day was conditional.
Ford established a department of investigators who visited employees' homes to
check whether they drank, gambled, kept clean houses, sent their children to
school, or had lodgers. Workers who failed were paid the lower rate.

Immigrant workers were required to attend the **Ford English School**, whose
graduation ceremony involved men in traditional dress walking into a giant
"melting pot" and emerging in American clothes waving flags.

**Paternalism and surveillance were the price of the wage.** It is a genuine
precursor to modern arguments about employer intrusion into private life.

## The failures — and they are instructive

**Refusing to change the Model T.** The trait that made him — absolute
conviction — destroyed his lead. By the early 1920s **General Motors under
Alfred Sloan** was offering annual model changes, a range of brands at different
price points ("a car for every purse and purpose"), colours, and consumer
credit.

Ford refused all of it. He reportedly had prototypes of improved models
destroyed. His son **Edsel** argued for change and was overruled and publicly
humiliated.

Ford's market share collapsed. When he finally stopped Model T production in
1927 to build the Model A, he had to **shut the entire River Rouge plant for
roughly six months**, laying off tens of thousands, because he had done no
parallel development. GM took the lead and kept it.

**This is `failure-modes.md`'s "conviction becomes stubbornness" in its
textbook form.** The same refusal to listen that produced a car for the masses
in 1908 produced obsolescence by 1927.

**The Service Department and the Battle of the Overpass (1937).** Ford resisted
unionisation longer and more violently than his competitors, employing
**Harry Bennett**, a former boxer with underworld connections, to run an
internal security force that intimidated and beat workers.

In 1937 Bennett's men beat United Auto Workers organisers — including
**Walter Reuther** — on an overpass at River Rouge, in front of photographers.
The pictures ran nationally. Ford Motor was the last of the major manufacturers
to recognise the union, in 1941.

**His antisemitism.** This cannot be a footnote. Ford owned *The Dearborn
Independent*, which from 1920 published years of antisemitic articles later
collected as *The International Jew*. The material circulated widely in Europe.
He was praised by name in *Mein Kampf* and accepted a decoration from Nazi
Germany in 1938. He issued a retraction in 1927 under legal and commercial
pressure whose sincerity is doubted.

He is in this notebook as a case study in industrial method. **He was also a
propagandist for one of the twentieth century's great evils, and the two facts
sit in the same person.**

## How he differed from his contemporaries

Compare with the **luxury carmakers** of 1908, who were serving the market that
existed. Ford built for a market that did not yet exist and created it. That is
the genuinely rare move.

Compare with **Alfred Sloan at GM**, who beat him. Sloan's insight was that once
everyone can afford a car, the competition shifts from **price to
differentiation, finance and marketing**. Ford could not see that the game had
changed because he had won the previous one so completely.

**Ford is the notebook's cleanest example of a leader defeated by his own prior
success.**

Compare with **Edsel Ford**, his son, who was right about nearly everything —
styling, model changes, the need to modernise — and was systematically
undermined by his father. He died at 49, and his son Henry Ford II had to
dismantle Bennett's apparatus to save the company.

## The price

Paid by workers on the line, whose jobs were deskilled and monotonous and whose
private lives were inspected; by those beaten by the Service Department; and by
his son.

His own: he outlived his usefulness, watched his company decline under his own
hand, suffered strokes, and died in 1947 while the company was being rescued by
his grandson. Under Bennett's influence in his final years, Ford Motor was so
badly run that it was reportedly losing large sums monthly and estimating
liabilities by weighing invoices.

---

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"He paid $5 a day so workers could buy the cars."** The notebook's flagged error. It was about turnover and efficiency wages. Ford later endorsed the customer story because it sounded better.
- **"He invented the assembly line."** He integrated and scaled existing ideas, and his engineers did the design work.
- **"Any colour so long as it's black."** The earliest Model Ts came in several colours; black dominated later because it dried fastest at volume.
- **"$5 a day for everyone."** Conditional on passing the Sociological Department's inspection, and initially excluding most women and many recent hires.

## The pattern

Ford gives the notebook two lessons, pulling in opposite directions.

**The positive one — mechanism #10:** the highest-return move often looks like
pure cost. He found the intervention where one payment bought three assets
simultaneously. The generalisable question is: *where in my system is the
apparent expense actually a purchase?*

**The negative one, and it is the more useful:** he is the notebook's best case
that **the trait which creates the success is frequently the trait that ends
it.** Total conviction built the Model T and then refused to replace it for a
decade past its time.

The distinguishing test from `failure-modes.md` applies exactly: conviction
updates on evidence; stubbornness reinterprets it. Ford had the evidence — a
collapsing market share and a son telling him — and destroyed the prototypes
instead.

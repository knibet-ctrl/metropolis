# Franklin D. Roosevelt (1882–1945)

*US President 1933–45. The notebook's case study in structured experimentation under genuine uncertainty.*

---

## What he inherited

Personally: wealth and security. Hudson Valley aristocracy, Groton, Harvard,
Columbia Law. A cousin who had been president. He was, by most accounts, a
pleasant lightweight for the first forty years of his life — charming,
ambitious, not deep.

**Polio (1921), aged 39.** He was paralysed from the waist down and never walked
unaided again. He spent the next seven years out of frontline politics, largely
at **Warm Springs, Georgia**, working on rehabilitation and buying the resort
to turn it into a treatment centre for other polio patients.

Nearly every close observer says this changed him. **It converted a
patrician into someone who understood helplessness**, and it forced on him a
seven-year interval of enforced patience.

Politically: he took office in **March 1933** at the pit of the Great
Depression. Roughly a quarter of the workforce was unemployed. The banking
system was in the middle of a total collapse — most states had closed their
banks by inauguration day. Industrial output had fallen by around half. There
were serious voices suggesting the country needed a dictator.

## Stories worth knowing

**"The only thing we have to fear is fear itself" (4 March 1933).** The line is
famous; the *situation* explains why it mattered. The immediate crisis was a
bank run — a self-fulfilling panic. Fear was not a metaphor; it was the
mechanism destroying the banks.

**The Hundred Days.** He declared a national bank holiday, closing every bank in
the country, and pushed the Emergency Banking Act through Congress in a single
day. Then, on 12 March, he gave the **first fireside chat**, explaining in plain
language how banking worked, why the banks had been closed, and why the ones
reopening were safe.

When they reopened, **deposits exceeded withdrawals.** He had talked a country
out of a panic in about a week.

Then, in roughly three months: the CCC, the TVA, the AAA, the NIRA, the FDIC,
the Glass–Steagall separation of commercial and investment banking, going off
gold. Fifteen major bills.

**Deliberate experimentation.** At Oglethorpe University in May 1932 he had
said: *"The country needs and, unless I mistake its temper, the country demands
bold, persistent experimentation. It is common sense to take a method and try
it: if it fails, admit it frankly and try another. But above all, try
something."*

*(Note: the popular version truncates this. The full sentence is the honest one.)*

And he meant it operationally. He deliberately created **overlapping, competing
agencies with unclear boundaries**, gave rival advisers the same problem, let
them fight, watched what worked, and backed the winner. **Harold Ickes** and
**Harry Hopkins** ran competing public works programmes and detested each other.
This was not disorganisation; it was a portfolio strategy.

He was also willing to reverse. The NRA was struck down by the Supreme Court in
1935 and he moved on. He tried deficit spending, then **cut spending in 1937**
and triggered a sharp recession, then reversed again. He was wrong repeatedly
and did not defend his errors.

**The fireside chats (1933–1944).** About thirty of them. He used **radio to go
around the press**, roughly 80% of which was hostile to him. He spoke slowly, in
short sentences, using domestic analogies — most famously explaining Lend-Lease
by asking listeners to imagine lending a neighbour your garden hose when his
house is on fire.

He deliberately kept them infrequent, believing that if he spoke too often
people would stop listening. **He treated the audience's attention as a
depletable resource** — a genuinely sophisticated media instinct in 1933.

**Frances Perkins.** He appointed the first woman to a US cabinet as Secretary
of Labor. She accepted on the condition that he support a specific list:
unemployment insurance, a minimum wage, an end to child labour, and old-age
insurance. He agreed. She stayed twelve years and is substantially the architect
of **Social Security**.

**The deception about his disability.** He could not walk. He got around by
locking heavy steel braces on his legs, gripping an aide's arm, and swinging his
hips — a gait he practised for years to make it look like walking. Podiums were
built to be gripped. The press corps largely cooperated in not photographing
him in his wheelchair, and the Secret Service reportedly interfered with
photographers who tried.

Of thousands of photographs, only a handful show the chair. He was elected
**four times** by a country that mostly did not know its president could not
stand unaided.

**The court-packing failure (1937).** After a landslide re-election, he tried to
add justices to a Supreme Court that had been striking down New Deal
legislation. It was widely seen as an attack on constitutional structure, and
his own party revolted. He lost badly and it damaged him for years. **His
information environment had degraded after the 1936 landslide — the classic
post-success failure from `failure-modes.md`.**

**Preparing for a war the country did not want.** Through 1939–41 he moved a
strongly isolationist nation toward involvement incrementally: cash-and-carry,
the destroyers-for-bases deal, the Atlantic Charter, and Lend-Lease. He was
frequently evasive and occasionally dishonest about it, which is a real charge
and not easily dismissed. He was also, in the notebook's terms, **sequencing** —
moving only as fast as public opinion could be brought along.

## How he differed from his contemporaries

Compare with **Herbert Hoover**, who was in many ways more qualified — a
genuinely brilliant engineer and the organiser of European famine relief after
WWI, an administrator of real distinction. Hoover had a **coherent theory**
(voluntarism, balanced budgets, resistance to direct federal relief) and applied
it consistently as conditions worsened.

**Hoover had a better model and stuck with it. Roosevelt had no model and kept
testing.** In genuine uncertainty, the second approach won.

Compare with the **European responses** to the same depression, several of which
resolved into authoritarianism. FDR was accused by both left and right of being
a dictator, and by others of saving capitalism from itself. The second charge is
closer to right.

Compare with **Huey Long**, the Louisiana populist with the "Share Our Wealth"
programme, who was a genuine threat from the left and pushed FDR toward the
more redistributive "Second New Deal." Long was assassinated in 1935.

## The price

His health. He was visibly dying through 1944 and ran for a fourth term anyway;
photographs from Yalta in February 1945 are shocking. He died of a cerebral
haemorrhage on 12 April 1945, weeks before the German surrender, having told
almost no one — including his vice-president — how ill he was. **Truman did not
know the atomic bomb existed until after FDR died.** That is a mechanism #12
failure of real consequence.

His marriage to **Eleanor** was effectively a political partnership after she
discovered his affair with Lucy Mercer in 1918. Eleanor became a formidable
political figure in her own right and, arguably, the more admirable of the two.

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"The New Deal ended the Depression."** Contested. Unemployment remained high through the 1930s and the decisive recovery came with **war mobilisation**. What the New Deal reliably did was prevent social collapse and build durable institutions.
- **Japanese American internment (Executive Order 9066, 1942).** He ordered the forced removal and incarceration of around 120,000 people, most of them US citizens. This is not a footnote; it is one of the gravest civil liberties violations in American history.
- **The New Deal's racial compromises.** To pass anything he needed southern Democrats, so Social Security initially excluded agricultural and domestic workers — categories covering the majority of Black workers — and federal housing policy institutionalised redlining. His sequencing had victims.
- **The refugee record**, including the *St. Louis* in 1939, is indefensible.
- **"A great intellect."** Oliver Wendell Holmes reportedly described him as having a second-class intellect and a first-class temperament. That is probably the fairest summary anyone has produced.

## The pattern

**FDR is the notebook's best case for running a portfolio of bets when nobody
knows the answer.**

The move is precise and copyable: when the problem is genuinely novel, **do not
pick the best-argued solution — run several in parallel, instrument them, kill
the losers fast, and scale the winner.** Deng did the same thing with Special
Economic Zones. Amazon does it with product bets.

The prerequisite most people miss is **psychological, not analytical**: you have
to be able to say publicly that something you launched has failed. Hoover
couldn't. Most people can't. FDR's temperament — not his intellect — is what
made the method available to him.

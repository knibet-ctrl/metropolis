# Lee Kuan Yew (1923–2015)

*Prime Minister of Singapore 1959–1990. The notebook's case for consistency over decades beating brilliance in bursts.*

---

## What he inherited

In 1965, one of the worst starting positions of any state in the notebook.

Singapore was **expelled from Malaysia** — not seceded, expelled, by a
parliamentary vote it did not want. Lee announced it on television and **wept**,
which he later said was the only time he lost composure in public.

The inventory:
- **No natural resources whatsoever.** Not even fresh water — it had to be piped from Malaysia, which had just thrown them out.
- **No hinterland**, no agricultural base.
- Roughly 700 square kilometres and about two million people.
- **GDP per capita comparable to poorer developing countries** of the period.
- A racially divided population — Chinese, Malay, Indian — that had just experienced deadly communal riots in 1964.
- Communist insurgency and militant unions.
- The **British announced withdrawal of their military bases**, which accounted for a substantial share of the economy.
- Hostile or wary neighbours: Malaysia, and Indonesia under *Konfrontasi*.

Serious observers did not expect the state to survive.

## Stories worth knowing

**The five things, held for thirty years.** He picked a small number of
priorities and did not deviate:

1. **Rule of law and contract enforcement**, inherited from the British and deliberately preserved.
2. **Anti-corruption**, pursued without exception.
3. **English as the working language**, with mother tongues retained in schools.
4. **Education and skills**, aggressively funded.
5. **Foreign direct investment**, courted rather than resisted.

None of these are clever. The achievement is that **he was still doing exactly
these things in 1990.** Most leaders in comparable positions changed course
every few years, chasing ideology or crisis. Mechanism #2.

**Anti-corruption as structure, not sermon.** He did two things simultaneously.

First, he made the **Corrupt Practices Investigation Bureau** powerful and
independent, reporting directly to the Prime Minister's office, with authority
to investigate anyone.

Second — the counterintuitive move — he **paid ministers and senior civil
servants salaries pegged to top private-sector earnings**, eventually among the
highest in the world for public officials.

His logic: if you underpay talented people with enormous discretionary power,
you have designed a system that will be corrupt, and no amount of moral
exhortation will fix it. Pay them properly and prosecute without exception, and
the expected value of corruption becomes negative.

**He applied it to his own circle.** His minister and long-time colleague
**Teh Cheang Wan** was investigated for bribery in 1986 and killed himself
before charges were brought. Lee did not intervene.

*(The causal claim — that high pay caused low corruption — is contested by
scholars, since enforcement, small size, and inherited institutions all
contribute. But the design logic is the transferable part.)*

**English as the working language.** A politically explosive choice. The
majority population was Chinese; Chinese-language education was a strong
political constituency, and he closed **Nanyang University** as a
Chinese-medium institution.

His reasoning was cold and correct: English was **neutral between the races** —
choosing Chinese would have permanently marginalised Malays and Indians — and it
was the language of global commerce, science and law. It plugged a tiny island
into the world economy.

He simultaneously required mother-tongue study to prevent cultural
deracination. **The bilingual policy is arguably the single highest-return
decision in Singapore's history.**

**Water.** Because Malaysia controlled the supply, he made water an obsession —
reservoirs, catchment areas, and eventually **NEWater** (large-scale reclaimed
water) and desalination. Cleaning the filthy **Singapore River** took roughly a
decade and was pursued personally.

This is mechanism #10 executed literally: he found the one physical constraint
that could destroy the country and spent thirty years removing it.

**Housing and the ownership stake.** The **Housing Development Board** built
public housing on a scale that now accommodates the large majority of the
population — and crucially, residents **buy** their flats, using their
compulsory **Central Provident Fund** savings.

Two deliberate effects. First, ownership gives citizens a financial stake in
national stability. Second, the HDB enforced **ethnic quotas in every block**,
deliberately preventing racial enclaves from forming. Integration was engineered
into the physical layout of the country.

**The unglamorous obsessions.** He personally drove campaigns on tree planting
(to make the city attractive to investors and liveable in the tropics), public
cleanliness, queuing, and — famously — banning chewing gum. He was mocked
internationally for the last one. He did not care.

**The greenery point is not trivial:** he understood that a first-world physical
environment signalled competence to foreign investors, which was the whole
economic strategy.

**Foreign investment against the consensus.** In the 1960s the fashionable
development doctrine was **import substitution** and suspicion of multinationals
as neo-colonial. Lee went the other way entirely and courted multinationals
aggressively, offering stability, low corruption, English, and industrial land
at Jurong.

**He was contrarian against the entire development economics profession of his
era, and he was right.** Mechanism #1 and #3.

## How he differed from his contemporaries

Compare with the broader **post-colonial cohort** of the 1960s — many of whom
had far better starting positions, real resources, and larger populations. The
common failure pattern was ideological drift, personalised rule, resource
capture, and policy reversal every few years. Lee's edge was substantially
**not changing his mind**.

Compare with **Malaysia**, which expelled Singapore and pursued a race-based
affirmative action model. Both approaches are defensible; the divergence in
outcomes is stark and remains politically sensitive.

Compare with **Hong Kong**, the closest analogue — also small, Chinese-majority,
trade-based, English-using, with British institutions. Hong Kong achieved
comparable prosperity with far less state direction, which is the strongest
argument that Lee's authoritarianism was **not necessary** for the economic
result.

That comparison matters and should not be brushed aside. It is the best
available control case.

## The price

**Paid substantially in political freedom, and mostly by other people.**

- **Operation Coldstore (1963)** detained large numbers of opposition figures and alleged communists without trial under the Internal Security Act. Whether the communist threat justified it remains the central controversy of Singapore's history.
- **Chia Thye Poh** was detained for around 23 years without trial.
- Opposition politicians, most notably **J.B. Jeyaretnam**, were pursued through **defamation suits** that bankrupted them and removed them from Parliament. This became the signature technique: not imprisonment, but litigation.
- The press operated under licensing constraints; foreign publications that criticised the government had circulation restricted.
- Extensive social engineering, including the "Graduate Mothers Scheme" of the 1980s, which encouraged educated women to have more children on explicitly eugenic reasoning. It was deeply unpopular and largely withdrawn.

He was unapologetic. His position was that a fragile multiracial city-state
surrounded by larger neighbours could not afford Western liberal permissiveness,
and that the results justified the method. **Whether the trade-off was necessary
is genuinely unresolved, and Hong Kong is the reason it remains open.**

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"He built Singapore from a fishing village."** It had been a major British entrepôt port for over a century, with existing trade infrastructure, legal institutions, and a skilled commercial class. The starting position was dire in 1965 but not primitive.
- **"He did it alone."** **Goh Keng Swee** (economics, the Jurong industrial estate, the armed forces), **S. Rajaratnam** (foreign policy, the National Pledge) and **Hon Sui Sen** were formidable and are consistently under-credited.
- **"Benevolent dictator."** Singapore held real elections throughout; the PAP won them. The system was engineered to make effective opposition extremely difficult, which is a different and more subtle thing than dictatorship.
- **"Authoritarianism caused the growth."** Not demonstrated. Many authoritarian states with the same tools produced nothing. The plausible causes are the institutions, the openness, the human capital investment, and the consistency.

## The pattern

**Lee Kuan Yew is the notebook's cleanest demonstration of mechanism #2:
consistency across decades beats brilliance in bursts.**

Nothing on his list of five priorities is a clever idea. Any competent economist
in 1965 could have written the list. **The rare thing was still executing it in
1990, without drift, without ideological fashion, and without softening the
unpopular parts.**

The second, more transferable lesson is his approach to corruption: he treated a
moral problem as an **incentive design problem**. Do not exhort people to be
honest in a system that makes dishonesty rational. Change the payoff.

And the honest caveat the notebook must keep attached: the political costs were
real, they were borne by identifiable people who spent decades in detention or
bankruptcy, and the Hong Kong comparison suggests they may not have been
necessary.

# Robert FitzRoy (1805–1865)

*Naval officer, captain of HMS Beagle, founder of weather forecasting. The notebook's most important failure case: he ran Churchill's play and it destroyed him.*

---

## Why he is in the notebook

FitzRoy is the control group.

He was right about a large, important thing, long before consensus. He held the
position at enormous personal cost. He built the institution. **And he was
ridiculed, defunded, and died by his own hand, with vindication arriving shortly
afterwards.**

Churchill spent a decade in the wilderness and reality arrived in time.
FitzRoy spent a decade in the wilderness and it did not. **From the inside, in
year four, these two situations are indistinguishable** — and that is the single
most uncomfortable fact in this entire collection.

---

## What he inherited

Aristocratic lineage and a family history of suicide. He was a descendant of
Charles II through the Dukes of Grafton; his uncle, **Viscount Castlereagh**,
the Foreign Secretary, had cut his own throat in 1822. FitzRoy was aware of this
inheritance and reportedly feared it.

He entered the Royal Naval College at twelve and was an exceptional student —
by some accounts the first to pass the lieutenant's examination with full marks.

**He took command of HMS Beagle at 23** in unusual circumstances: the previous
captain, **Pringle Stokes**, had shot himself during a surveying voyage in the
desolate channels of Tierra del Fuego, worn down by isolation and the weather.

FitzRoy inherited both the ship and a vivid demonstration of what the job could
do to a man.

---

## Stories worth knowing

**Why Darwin was aboard.** This is the detail that reframes everything.

FitzRoy, conscious of Stokes's fate and of the isolation of command — a captain
could not socialise with his officers — sought a **gentleman companion** to dine
with and talk to on a voyage expected to last years.

He was looking for conversation, essentially as a defence against his own mind.

The post went to **Charles Darwin**, a 22-year-old with an unfinished direction
in life. FitzRoy reportedly nearly rejected him on the basis of the shape of his
nose, being an adherent of physiognomy, and doubting he had the determination
for the voyage.

**One of the most consequential appointments in the history of science was made
as a hedge against loneliness, and nearly refused over a nose.**

The two argued frequently, especially about slavery — FitzRoy defended it after
a visit to a Brazilian estate; Darwin, from an abolitionist family, objected
sharply, and they briefly stopped speaking.

FitzRoy was a devout literalist about Genesis, and he later suffered
considerably over having enabled *On the Origin of Species*. At the 1860 Oxford
debate he reportedly stood in the audience holding a Bible above his head,
urging the crowd to believe God rather than man. Accounts of the event are
partly retrospective, but his distress is well attested.

**He was also a superb surveyor.** The *Beagle*'s charts of South America were
of exceptional quality and remained in use for a very long time. He purchased a
second vessel **out of his own money** to speed the survey, and was not
reimbursed — an early instance of a pattern that would recur fatally.

**Governor of New Zealand (1843–45).** He attempted to enforce the Treaty of
Waitangi and protect Māori land rights against settler encroachment. **The
settlers had him recalled within two years.**

He was right, unpopular, and removed. The pattern is now visible three times.

**The Meteorological Office (from 1854).** He was appointed to head a new
government department collecting weather statistics for shipping. The brief was
narrow: gather data, publish wind charts.

FitzRoy did something far more ambitious. He built a network of coastal
observers connected by the new **electric telegraph** — the crucial enabling
technology, because for the first time information about weather could travel
faster than the weather itself.

Using barometer readings telegraphed from around the coast, he issued
**storm warnings** to ports, so that ships could stay in harbour.

**He coined the word "forecast"** — choosing it carefully, arguing that it was
not prophecy but the result of calculation from data, and that the word should
signal that distinction.

From 1861 *The Times* published his daily weather predictions — the first
newspaper weather forecasts.

**He also gave away barometers.** He designed a cheap, robust, standardised
instrument with printed instructions — the "FitzRoy barometer" — and arranged
for them to be distributed to fishing communities, so that ordinary fishermen
could read the pressure themselves. **He was democratising the technology, in
the Grace Hopper sense**, a century before that became a recognised strategy.

The **Royal Charter storm (1859)**, which sank a ship of that name with heavy
loss of life, was the disaster that gave his warning system its political
impetus.

## Why it destroyed him

**The scientific establishment turned on him.**

The objection was philosophical as much as technical. The **Royal Society** and
figures within it regarded prediction from incomplete data as **unscientific** —
closer to astrology than to natural philosophy. Weather was not yet understood
to be a physical system amenable to prediction; attempting it looked like
quackery dressed in official clothes.

His forecasts were also, frequently, **wrong**. He had no theory of atmospheric
dynamics, no upper-air data, and no mathematics of fluid motion — none of which
existed. He was pattern-matching from barometric pressure and wind. Under those
constraints the hit rate was moderate, and every miss was public and ridiculed.

Critics in *The Times* and in Parliament complained. Fishermen and shipowners
objected that storm warnings kept boats in port on days that turned out fine,
costing them money.

**He spent his own money.** He funded parts of the work from his personal
finances — as he had done with the *Beagle*'s second vessel — and his family's
means were substantially depleted.

**He worked himself into collapse.** He was doing the forecasting personally,
daily, for years, while defending it politically.

On **30 April 1865**, at his home in Upper Norwood, he cut his own throat with a
razor. He was 59.

**After his death, the storm warning service was discontinued** on the grounds
that it lacked scientific foundation.

**Fishing communities and shipowners protested. It was reinstated within a few
years, and never stopped again.**

## How he differed — and how it did not save him

Compare with **Churchill**. Both held a specific, unpopular, publicly stated
position against establishment consensus, at severe personal cost, for years.
Both were substantively right. **Churchill's validating event arrived in 1940
while he was alive and available; FitzRoy's arrived after his funeral.**

The difference in outcome is not a difference in strategy, character, or
judgement. **It is timing, and timing was outside the control of both men.**

Compare with **Darwin**, who sailed on his ship. Darwin waited roughly twenty
years before publishing, accumulating evidence and allies, and managed his
release carefully. FitzRoy published daily, immediately, under his own name,
where every error was visible.

**Darwin ran mechanism #8 — strategic patience. FitzRoy ran mechanism #4 —
decision velocity — in a field where he could not yet be right often enough for
it to work.**

Compare with **Semmelweis**, dying in the same year, 1865, also correct and also
destroyed. Semmelweis failed at narrative and alienated everyone; FitzRoy did
not — he was a decent, courteous, hardworking man who communicated well and
still lost. **That is worse, and more instructive.**

## The price

His fortune, his health, his reputation among the scientists whose respect he
wanted, and his life. His widow was left in reduced circumstances; a public
subscription was raised for the family.

---

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"Darwin's reactionary captain."** He was a scientific innovator in his own right, an outstanding surveyor, and the founder of operational meteorology. The *Beagle* connection has overshadowed the achievement.
- **"He killed himself because of Darwin."** Overstated. The evidence points to exhaustion, financial strain, public attacks on his forecasting, and a family history of mental illness. His religious distress was real but is not a sufficient explanation.
- **"His forecasts were pseudoscience."** They were pre-theoretical and often wrong. They also saved lives, and the method was fundamentally sound. His critics were more wrong than he was.

## The pattern

**FitzRoy is the notebook's necessary corrective, and he should be read
immediately after Churchill.**

Three things he establishes:

1. **Being right is not sufficient.** He was right about the most important
   thing in his field and it did not save him.
2. **Executing the mechanisms correctly does not guarantee you personally
   survive to see the outcome.** It improves the probability that the outcome
   happens. Those are different claims, and the leadership literature
   systematically conflates them.
3. **The survivorship bias in this notebook is not abstract.** FitzRoy is
   visible only because he was already famous for the *Beagle*. The people who
   ran the same play without a prior claim to fame left no records at all, and
   there are many of them.

The practical consequence, from `sources-and-caveats.md`: when you take a
long-horizon contrarian position, **structure it so that being right late is
survivable.** FitzRoy funded it from his own pocket, did the daily work
personally, and had no institutional protection when the attacks came. The
position was correct; the exposure was fatal.

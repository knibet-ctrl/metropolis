# Abraham Lincoln (1809–1865)

*16th US President. The notebook's best case study in sequencing — right decision, right moment.*

---

## What he inherited

Personally: genuine poverty. A frontier childhood in Kentucky and Indiana,
roughly one year of formal schooling in total, a mother dead when he was nine.
He read law on his own and qualified by self-study.

Politically: he won the 1860 election with **under 40% of the popular vote**,
and was not even on the ballot in most southern states. Between his election
and his inauguration, seven states seceded. He took office as president of a
country that was already coming apart, with a cabinet full of men who thought
they should have the job, and no executive or military experience beyond a brief
militia stint.

He had also lost repeatedly: defeated for the Senate in 1855 and again in 1858
(the Douglas debates), and had served a single undistinguished term in Congress.
**By age 50 he looked like a failure**, which is worth sitting with.

## Stories worth knowing

**The Team of Rivals.** He appointed to his cabinet the men who had opposed him
for the nomination: **William Seward** (Secretary of State), **Salmon Chase**
(Treasury), **Edward Bates** (Attorney General), and later **Edwin Stanton**
(War) — a man who had previously humiliated him professionally, reportedly
calling him a "long-armed ape" during a legal case years earlier.

Seward initially assumed he would run the administration and submitted a memo
effectively proposing to take charge. Lincoln replied politely, firmly, and
without wounding him — and Seward became his most loyal ally.

Chase spent the war undermining him and manoeuvring for the presidency. Lincoln
kept him because he was excellent at Treasury, accepted his resignation only
when it stopped being useful, and then **appointed him Chief Justice** anyway,
on the grounds that he would rule correctly on emancipation.

The point: **he had almost no ego investment in being the smartest person in
the room, and he treated competence as more important than loyalty.**

*Caveat: "Team of Rivals" is Doris Kearns Goodwin's framing. Some historians
note these were also simply the most prominent Republicans available, i.e.
ordinary coalition politics.*

**The Emancipation Proclamation — the sequencing masterpiece.** He personally
opposed slavery for decades. Yet for the first eighteen months of the war he
refused to make emancipation the war aim, and overruled generals who tried it
locally.

Why: the four **border slave states** — Maryland, Kentucky, Missouri, Delaware —
had not seceded. Declaring an abolition war early would likely have pushed them
out, which would have meant losing Washington DC itself. Northern opinion was
also not yet ready.

So he waited. He wrote the proclamation and, on Seward's advice, **held it in a
drawer** — because issuing it after a string of defeats would read as desperation
rather than strength. He waited for a victory. Antietam (17 September 1862) was
barely one, but it was enough. He issued the preliminary proclamation on
22 September 1862.

**The right decision, taken eighteen months too early, would have lost the war
and freed nobody.** This is mechanism #8, and Lincoln is its finest exponent.

**The Gettysburg Address (19 November 1863).** The main speaker that day was
Edward Everett, who spoke for around two hours. Lincoln spoke for about two
minutes, in roughly 270 words.

What he did in those words was reframe the entire conflict: from a war to
preserve a legal union into a war about whether a nation "conceived in liberty
and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal" could endure.
He dated the country from the **Declaration of Independence** (1776, "four score
and seven years ago") rather than the Constitution (1787) — because the
Declaration contains the equality clause and the Constitution contains the
compromises with slavery.

That is a deliberate, load-bearing choice of founding document. **He rewrote
what the country was, in two minutes, and everyone has argued inside his frame
ever since.**

*(Word count varies by manuscript copy — five exist, ranging roughly 267–272.)*

**The letter to Horace Greeley (1862).** Publicly: *"If I could save the Union
without freeing any slave I would do it; and if I could save it by freeing all
the slaves I would do it."* Written while the Emancipation Proclamation was
already sitting in his drawer. He was **pre-positioning public opinion** for a
decision he had already made — letting the country see the reasoning before the
act.

**The unsent letters.** He habitually wrote furious letters to
underperforming generals — most famously to Meade after Gettysburg, for
letting Lee's army escape — and then filed them, unsent. Several were found in
his papers after his death. A deliberate mechanism for separating the emotion
from the decision.

**Firing generals until one worked.** McDowell, McClellan (twice), Pope,
Burnside, Hooker, Meade — before Grant. When told Grant drank, he reportedly
asked what brand so he could send some to his other generals. The anecdote is
probably apocryphal; the *policy* — tolerate flaws, demand results — is not.

**The Second Inaugural (1865).** With the war nearly won, he did not gloat. He
suggested that both sides were implicated in the sin of slavery and closed with
*"with malice toward none, with charity for all."* Written for the peace, not
the victory.

## How he differed from his contemporaries

Compare with the **abolitionists** — William Lloyd Garrison, Wendell Phillips.
Morally clearer and years ahead of him. Garrison publicly burned the
Constitution as a covenant with hell. They were *right earlier*, and they had
no path to power and could not have executed emancipation. Lincoln was
slower, more compromised, and actually did it.

This is a genuinely uncomfortable finding: **the person who is right earliest
is frequently not the person who delivers the outcome.**

Compare with **George McClellan**, who was better educated, better looking, a
superb organiser and trainer of armies, adored by his troops — and who would
not attack. He consistently overestimated enemy numbers and demanded more
resources. He then ran against Lincoln in 1864 on a peace platform and lost.
McClellan is the notebook's archetype of the person who waits for 95%
certainty.

Compare with **Jefferson Davis**, the Confederate president: more experienced
(West Point, Secretary of War, Senator), more conventionally qualified in every
respect, and a worse leader — rigid, quarrelsome with his generals, unable to
delegate or to hold a coalition together.

## The price

Severe and continuous. He suffered from what he called "the hypo" — recurring
depression serious enough that friends once removed razors from his room. His
son **Willie died in the White House in 1862**, and his wife's grief was
publicly conspicuous and politically costly. He was widely mocked in the press
as a baboon and a tyrant.

He signed orders that sent hundreds of thousands of men to their deaths. He
suspended habeas corpus, which remains legally contested. He was assassinated
five days after Lee's surrender, having survived the entire war and not the
peace.

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"The Great Emancipator" acting from pure principle.** He was a gradualist for most of his career, supported colonisation schemes for freed slaves into the 1860s, and stated during the Douglas debates positions on racial equality that are unacceptable today. He moved substantially over time. **He is a case of someone who changed, not someone who was always right.**
- **The Emancipation Proclamation freed almost nobody on the day it was signed** — it applied to territory in rebellion, where the US government had no control, and exempted the loyal border states. Its power was strategic: it made the war about slavery, blocked British and French recognition of the Confederacy, and authorised the enlistment of Black troops. Legal abolition required the **Thirteenth Amendment**.
- **"Written on the back of an envelope on the train."** Myth. There are multiple drafts.

## The pattern

**Lincoln is the notebook's answer to the question "how do you hold principle
and effectiveness at the same time?" — and the answer is: by sequencing, not by
compromising.**

He did not water down the goal. He waited until the conditions made it
survivable, and used the interval to build the political ground for it. Most
people fail in one of two directions: they act on principle too early and lose,
or they wait so long that waiting becomes the position.

The distinguishing test is whether the delay is *purchasing* something specific.
Lincoln always knew exactly what he was buying — the border states, a battlefield
victory, northern opinion. If you cannot name what your delay is buying, you are
not sequencing. You are stalling.

# Nikola Tesla (1856–1943)

*Inventor and electrical engineer. The notebook's case that concentration without capture is just gambling.*

---

## Why he is in the notebook

He ran **mechanism #3** (concentrated bet), **#2** (long horizon) and **#9**
(costly signals) at full intensity, and had genuine technical superiority.

He had **no mechanism #7** — no institution, no mechanism for capturing value
from what he created — and he died in debt in a hotel room while others
monetised his field.

Edison was the worse scientist and the better systems-builder. **Systems won.**

---

## What he inherited

Born in Smiljan, in what is now Croatia, to a Serbian Orthodox priest and a
mother who was illiterate but built household tools and devices and had a
prodigious memory. Tesla credited her for his own visual and mechanical
imagination.

He had an extraordinary and probably pathological mind: he could reportedly
visualise machines in complete detail and run them mentally, testing for wear,
without building prototypes. He also experienced severe sensory episodes,
compulsions around the number three, and a horror of pearls and of human hair.

He studied at Graz and Prague without completing a degree, worked for a
telephone company in Budapest and for Continental Edison in Paris, and arrived
in New York in 1884 with, by the standard account, very little money and a
letter of introduction.

## Stories worth knowing

**The rotating magnetic field.** The core insight, and it is genuinely first
rank. He conceived of a motor driven by **alternating current** producing a
rotating magnetic field — eliminating the commutators and brushes that made DC
motors complex and unreliable.

By his own account the idea came to him while walking in a Budapest park,
reciting Goethe, and he sketched it in the sand with a stick.

**Alternating current's real advantage** is transmission. AC voltage can be
stepped up and down with transformers, so power can be sent at high voltage over
long distances with low losses and then stepped down for use. **Edison's DC
required a generating station roughly every mile.**

This was not a stylistic preference. It was a decisive physical fact, and Tesla
was on the right side of it.

**Edison.** He worked for Edison briefly. The famous story — that he was
promised $50,000 for redesigning Edison's dynamos, completed the work, and was
told it was an American joke — comes from Tesla's own later account and is not
independently documented. He resigned, and for a period **dug ditches** for a
living.

**The War of the Currents (late 1880s–1890s).** Edison, heavily invested in DC,
ran a campaign against AC that included **publicly electrocuting animals** to
demonstrate its danger, and promoting AC for use in the first electric chair so
that the technology would be associated with execution.

**Westinghouse** licensed Tesla's AC patents. The system's superiority was
settled publicly by the lighting of the **1893 Chicago World's Fair** and the
**Niagara Falls** hydroelectric project, which transmitted power to Buffalo
roughly 20 miles away — something DC simply could not do.

**Tesla won the technical argument completely. The world runs on AC.**

**Tearing up the royalty contract (1890s).** The decision the notebook is most
interested in.

Tesla's agreement with Westinghouse reportedly entitled him to a royalty per
horsepower of AC capacity sold. As the technology spread, this would have made
him one of the richest men in the world.

Westinghouse ran into financial trouble, and its backers demanded the royalty
agreement be renegotiated or the company would fail. Westinghouse appealed to
Tesla personally.

**Tesla tore up the contract**, reportedly saying that Westinghouse had believed
in him when no one else had, and that he would rather the AC system succeed than
collect.

It is a genuinely admirable act. It is also the single decision that determined
the rest of his life. **He gave away the only mechanism he had for capturing
value from his own work.**

**Wardenclyffe (1901–1906) — the all-in bet.** He raised roughly $150,000 from
**J.P. Morgan** to build a transatlantic wireless transmission facility on Long
Island — a large tower and an elaborate grounding system.

But Tesla's actual ambition was different from what he had sold. He intended
**wireless transmission of power itself** — energy broadcast through the earth
and atmosphere, free to anyone with a receiver.

Two problems, both fatal:

1. **Morgan was not funding free energy.** When Tesla revealed the scope, Morgan reportedly asked where the meter went. A businessman does not fund a system that cannot be billed for. **Tesla had no theory of revenue at all, and did not appear to consider it a serious question.**
2. **Marconi transmitted across the Atlantic in 1901**, using — among other things — Tesla patents. The commercial race was lost while Wardenclyffe was still being built.

Morgan declined further funding. Tesla could not raise more. The tower was
eventually demolished in 1917 and the land sold to cover debts.

**No fallback. No revenue. No institution. One bet, and it failed.**

**Colorado Springs (1899).** A remarkable and characteristic episode. He built a
laboratory to experiment with high-voltage, high-frequency transmission, created
artificial lightning discharges reported at over a hundred feet, and knocked out
the local power station's generator.

He also detected repeating radio signals and suggested publicly that they might
be **extraterrestrial in origin** — almost certainly natural phenomena or
Marconi's transmissions. It contributed to his growing reputation as a crank,
which made subsequent fundraising harder.

**The later years.** He lived in New York hotels, moved when bills accumulated,
and was eventually supported partly by a small pension from the Yugoslav
government and by Westinghouse quietly paying his hotel bills.

He fed pigeons in Bryant Park and the New York Public Library, and formed an
intense attachment to one particular white pigeon, which he spoke about in terms
usually reserved for a spouse.

He gave birthday press conferences announcing devices — a "death beam,"
among others — that were never demonstrated. These generated headlines and
further eroded his credibility.

He died alone in **Room 3327 of the Hotel New Yorker** in January 1943, aged 86,
in debt. His papers were seized by the Office of Alien Property.

**Months later, the US Supreme Court ruled in a patent case in a manner
generally read as upholding Tesla's radio patents over Marconi's.** The case
concerned government liability and the practical effect is debated, but the
timing — vindication arriving after death — is the FitzRoy pattern again.

## How he differed from his contemporaries

Compare with **Thomas Edison**, the crucial comparison. Edison was the inferior
theoretician and was wrong about DC. He also built **Edison Electric**, ran an
industrial research laboratory at Menlo Park as a systematic invention factory,
managed patents aggressively, cultivated investors and the press, and created
the institution that became **General Electric**.

**Edison built a machine for turning invention into money. Tesla built
inventions.** GE still exists.

Compare with **George Westinghouse**, who had the commercial apparatus to
deploy AC at scale. Tesla supplied the patents; Westinghouse supplied the
company. The technology needed both, and only one of them was compensated for
it.

Compare with **Guglielmo Marconi**, who was arguably less original and who
**shipped, demonstrated publicly, and commercialised**. He won the Nobel Prize
in 1909 and built a business.

Compare with **Bezos** in the builder set. Amazon spent two decades on a long
horizon — but against a tracked revenue model with measured unit economics.
**Long horizon plus no revenue theory is not patience. It is drift.**

## The price

Poverty, debt, obscurity in his final decades, and the erosion of a
extraordinary reputation into eccentricity. He never married and by his own
account had no romantic relationships, believing them incompatible with his
work.

---

## Where the popular story is wrong

This matters, because Tesla has become an internet myth and the myth is not
useful.

- **"Edison stole his ideas."** They were rivals with genuinely different technical positions. The $50,000 story is Tesla's own account. Edison's conduct in the War of the Currents was ugly; theft is a different charge.
- **"He invented radio / X-rays / the internet / free energy."** He made real contributions to radio and to high-frequency work, and observed X-ray effects independently. The maximalist claims are not supported. **Wireless power transmission at global scale does not work — the physics of energy dissipation is against it**, and this is the central reason Wardenclyffe was never going to succeed regardless of funding.
- **"His secrets were suppressed by the government."** His papers were seized and later examined; the assessment was that they contained no viable weapon. Conspiracy readings persist without evidence.
- **"He was a martyr to capitalism."** He was funded generously by Westinghouse and Morgan, and had one of the great revenue streams of the era in his hands and gave it away. **He was not denied capture; he declined it.**

## The pattern

**Tesla is the notebook's clearest demonstration that mechanism #3
(concentration) without mechanism #7 (a machine that captures and compounds) is
gambling.**

Three specific lessons:

1. **Being technically right does not entitle you to the returns.** Value
   accrues to whoever controls deployment, distribution and billing. AC won
   completely, and the money went to Westinghouse and GE.
2. **"Where does the meter go?" is not a philistine question.** Morgan's
   objection was the correct one. A project with no revenue mechanism is a gift,
   and gifts require a permanent donor. Tesla never secured one.
3. **A concentrated bet needs a survivable failure mode.** Wardenclyffe had
   none. Compare Deng's Special Economic Zones — the same appetite for a big
   idea, contained so that failure was quarantined.

And the honest note the notebook should keep: **his royalty decision was
genuinely generous and it was catastrophic.** Mechanism #9 says costly signals
buy credibility. Tesla's cost him the entire capacity to fund his own work for
the following fifty years. **A costly signal should be survivable if it fails —
and this one wasn't a signal at all, it was simply a gift.**

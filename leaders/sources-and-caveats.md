# Sources, Epistemics, and Caveats

## Provenance of this notebook

These files were compiled from general historical knowledge, not from live
web research or primary sources. Treat them as a **structured starting
hypothesis**, not as verified scholarship.

**Before relying on any specific claim** (dates, quotes, attributions), verify
it. Several widely-repeated leadership anecdotes are apocryphal or heavily
embellished — this is a well-documented problem in the genre.

## Five biases baked into this dataset

### 1. Survivorship bias — the big one
We only study people whose bets worked. For every figure here, an unknown
number of people ran the identical strategy and disappeared. This means
**every "strategy" in this notebook is at best necessary, never sufficient.**
The base rate of failure is invisible and probably enormous.

### 2. Narrative bias
Biographies impose coherent intent on what was often improvisation, luck, and
post-hoc rationalisation. Leaders rewrite their own decision-making in
memoirs. Caesar literally wrote his own PR.

### 3. Great-man bias
Structural forces — demographics, technology, disease, geography, commodity
prices — do enormous causal work that gets attributed to individuals.
Ford didn't invent mass production alone; Gandhi's success depended on a
war-exhausted, financially broken Britain. **The person is often the visible
part of a much larger machine.**

### 4. Selection bias toward the loud
Charismatic, quotable people are over-represented because they generate
records. Merkel is included deliberately as a counterweight, and Borlaug as
the extreme case: arguably the highest-impact figure in the file, and almost
nobody can picture him.

### 5. Western and male over-representation
Acknowledged. Partially mitigated (Ashoka, Genghis, Mansa Musa era, Deng,
Lee Kuan Yew, Toussaint, Curie, Graham, Hopper, Maathai, Elizabeth I,
Merkel) but the historical record itself is skewed, and so is this file.

## The failure sample — the missing control group

The notebook studies winners. Here are five cases where the *same mechanisms*
were run competently and produced disaster. **If a mechanism appears in both
samples, it is not a cause of success — it is a cause of variance.**

| Case | Mechanisms run | What was missing |
|---|---|---|
| **Robert FitzRoy** — founded weather forecasting, ridiculed, funded it himself, died by suicide 1865; vindicated shortly after | #2 long horizon, #10 real constraint | Nothing, except luck and timing. He ran Churchill's exact play and the roll went the other way. |
| **Ignaz Semmelweis** — correct about handwashing, had the mortality data, died in an asylum 1865 | #1 asymmetric information, #10 constraint | #5 narrative. He insulted every colleague who could have adopted it. Correctness without transmission is inert. |
| **Nikola Tesla** — technically visionary, tore up his Westinghouse royalties, died in debt | #3 concentration, #9 costly signal | #7 institution. No mechanism to capture value. Edison was the worse scientist and the better systems-builder. |
| **Elizabeth Holmes / Theranos** — ran the *visible* playbook near-perfectly | #5, #9, #3 — all simulated well | #1. No true underlying claim. Amplification applied to a false premise is fraud, not success. |
| **The Great Leap Forward** | #2, #3, #5, #7 at maximum state scale | Any upward channel for bad news. Officials reported fictitious harvests because truth was fatal. Tens of millions dead. |

**What this changes:**

1. **#1 (asymmetric information) is the only non-negotiable mechanism.** The others are multipliers. Multiply zero and you still get zero, just faster and with more investors attached.
2. **#5 (narrative) is not decoration.** Semmelweis proves it.
3. **#3 (concentration) is a coin flip** — it appears equally in both samples.
4. **An error-correction channel separates the two samples more often than talent does.** Lincoln, Toyota, Amazon and Genghis built one deliberately; Mao, late Napoleon and Holmes destroyed theirs.

**The invisible sample** is the real problem: the thousands who spent 20 years
on a problem that didn't matter, or made the contrarian call that was simply
wrong, and left no records. Any success rate you infer from this notebook is
inflated by an unknown and probably large factor.

## How to actually use this notebook

1. **Treat each mechanism as a hypothesis, not a law.** Test it against cases you know well from your own life and field.
2. **Actively hunt counterexamples.** Find highly effective people who did the *opposite* of each mechanism. They exist. That tells you the mechanism's real scope.
3. **Don't cargo-cult surface behaviour.** Wearing a black turtleneck, being rude in meetings, or dropping out of university are correlates of a few famous people, not causes of anything.
4. **Weight the boring mechanisms highest.** Time-horizon arbitrage (#2), direct contact with reality (#1), and institution-building (#7) are the least glamorous and the best supported.

## Recommended reading if you want to go deeper

**On mechanisms and decision-making**
- *Good Strategy / Bad Strategy* — Richard Rumelt (best single book on what strategy actually is)
- *Thinking in Bets* — Annie Duke (separating decision quality from outcome)
- *The Goal* — Goldratt (theory of constraints, mechanism #10, as a novel)
- *Seeing Like a State* — James C. Scott (why grand top-down plans fail)

**Biography / history worth the time**
- *Team of Rivals* — Doris Kearns Goodwin (Lincoln, mechanism #6)
- *Long Walk to Freedom* — Mandela (autobiography; read critically)
- *Personal History* — Katharine Graham
- *The Making of the Atomic Bomb* — Rhodes (large-scale institution-building)

**Correctives — read these to stay honest**
- *Fooled by Randomness* — Taleb (survivorship bias)
- *The Halo Effect* — Rosenzweig (dismantles most business-leadership literature)
- *Ordinary Men* — Browning (the darker side of what ordinary people do under structure)

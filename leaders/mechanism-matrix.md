# Mechanism Matrix — What the 35 Profiles Actually Show

> Answers two of the open questions logged in `wbook.txt`:
> **(1) which mechanisms actually recur**, and **(2) age at first real leverage.**

---

## ⚠ Methodological warning — read this first

**This matrix was coded from profiles written by the same author who defined the
mechanisms.** That is circular. It measures the consistency of a framing, not a
fact about history. A different framework applied to the same 35 people would
produce different counts.

**Treat the frequencies as a map of the notebook, not as evidence about the
world.** The genuinely informative parts are the *absences* and the
*failure-sample contrasts*, because those were forced by the cases rather than
chosen.

## What the counts actually tell you

**1. #1 and #10 are the base pair.** Asymmetric information and finding the real
constraint appear together in most cases and are essentially the same discipline
viewed from two angles: *go where reality is, and identify what's actually
binding.* If a figure has only two mechanisms, it's usually these.

**2. #3 (concentration) has the worst success-to-failure ratio in the file.**
It appears in 9 of 30 successes and 3 of 5 failures. **This is the strongest
argument in the notebook that concentration is a variance amplifier rather than
a success factor** — exactly as `sources-and-caveats.md` claimed, now with the
coding to support it.

**3. #11 (moral leverage) clusters entirely in one situation type.** Gandhi,
King, Maathai, Zelenskyy, and partially Mandela. All were structurally weaker
than their opponent and all had an opponent constrained by opinion, plus
witnesses. **It is not a general mechanism. It is a specialised tool with hard
preconditions**, and the notebook should stop listing it alongside the others as
though it were universally available.

**4. #12 (successors) is the sharpest discriminator between "impressive" and
"changed history."** Alexander, Qin, Caesar, Napoleon, Ford and FDR all failed
it, and all of their systems fractured or degraded immediately after them.
Ashoka, Genghis, Deng, Mandela and Bezos handled it, and their arrangements
outlasted them.

**5. Mechanism #8 is rare because it's psychologically expensive.** Only 7
cases. Elizabeth I, Churchill, Merkel, Deng, Lincoln, FDR, Carnegie. **Holding
optionality or a contrarian position for years without spending it requires
tolerating sustained anxiety**, which is why it shows up mostly in people with
unusual temperaments.

## The absences are more informative than the presences

| Figure | Missing | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Alexander | #7, #12 | Empire fractured within a decade |
| Qin Shi Huang | #12 | Dynasty gone in 4 years; standards lasted 2,000 |
| Rockefeller | #5 | Most capable industrialist of his age; Tarbell defined him for a century |
| Ford | error correction | Destroyed prototypes, humiliated his son, lost the market |
| Tesla | #7, #10 | Won the technical argument completely, died in debt |
| Semmelweis | #5, #6 | Correct with data, and it changed nothing for 20 years |
| Holmes | #1 | Everything else executed well; result was fraud |
| Mao | #1, #12 | Famine |

**Pattern: a single missing mechanism is usually what the story is about.** The
successes are not distinguished by having more mechanisms — they are
distinguished by not having a fatal hole.

## The 13th mechanism the notebook never listed

The matrix makes it visible. **Error correction** — a structural channel through
which bad news reaches the decision-maker — is not in the list of twelve, and it
does more discriminating work than most of them.

| Present | Absent or destroyed |
|---|---|
| Lincoln (rivals in cabinet) | Ford (destroyed prototypes) |
| Genghis (promoted the man who shot at him) | Alexander (killed Cleitus; executed Callisthenes) |
| Ohno (the andon cord) | Napoleon (late period) |
| Bezos (disagree and commit) | Jobs at Apple (but *present* at Pixar) |
| Toyota, Amazon, Pixar | Holmes (NDAs, litigation, siloing) |
| FDR (competing agencies) | Mao (purged Peng Dehuai) |

**It should be promoted to a numbered mechanism.** It is currently buried in
`failure-modes.md` as "destroyer #2," which understates it — it is the single
most reliable difference between the two samples.

## Age at first real leverage

The wbook hypothesis was: *later than people assume.* **Confirmed, and by more
than expected.**

"First real leverage" = the point at which they first had genuine capacity to
act at scale, not their first job or first success.

| Age band | Figures |
|---|---|
| **Under 25** | Alexander (20), Jobs (21), Napoleon (24), Gandhi (24, South Africa) |
| **25–34** | Churchill (25, MP), Elizabeth I (25), MLK (26), Rockefeller (~31), Curie (~31), Carnegie (~30s), Borlaug (30), Bezos (30) |
| **35–44** | Lee Kuan Yew (35), Ashoka (~36), Yunus (36), Hopper (37, Navy), Maathai (37), Ford (40), Zelenskyy (41), Genghis (~44), Mandela (~44, arrest) |
| **45+** | Merkel (45), Graham (46), Toussaint (~48), FDR (50), Lincoln (52), Churchill again (65, PM), Mandela again (71, release), **Deng (74)** |

**Median: roughly 36.**

**The more interesting finding — the gap.** For many figures, the distance
between first leverage and defining achievement is measured in decades:

| Figure | First leverage | Defining work | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Churchill | 25 | 65 | **40 years**, including a decade in the wilderness |
| Mandela | 44 | 71 | **27 years**, in prison |
| Deng | ~40s | 74 | Purged twice in between |
| Lincoln | 52 | 52–56 | Looked like a failure at 50 |
| Graham | 46 | 54–57 | Had never held an executive job |
| Jobs | 21 | 42 | 12 years in exile between |
| Borlaug | 30 | 51 (India/Pakistan) | 21 years of field work first |

**Conclusion: the notebook's own mechanism #2 is visible in the biography data.**
The median age is late, the gap is long, and the people who peaked early
(Alexander, and arguably Jobs's first run) are also the ones whose work did not
survive them.

**The practical implication is unfashionable and well supported: being 35 and
without leverage is the base case, not a failure.** The distribution says the
work usually starts paying in the fourth or fifth decade, and several of the
most consequential figures here had not begun the thing they're remembered for.

## What this file cannot support

- **Any causal claim.** 35 hand-picked cases, coded by the person who selected them.
- **Any probability.** No denominator; the invisible failure sample is unbounded.
- **Cross-era comparison of "leverage."** A Bronze Age king at 20 and a modern executive at 40 are not on the same scale.
- **The frequencies as evidence.** They describe the framing. The absences and the failure contrasts are the parts forced by the cases.

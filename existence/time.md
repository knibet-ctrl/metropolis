# Time

> **Flagged as a gap in the original README.** Time was scattered across
> what-is-life, death and personal-identity without being addressed directly —
> which is a problem, because **every question in this notebook depends on it.**

---

## Why it matters here

Personal identity is a question about persistence *through time*. Death is
about a boundary *in time*. Meaning is usually about a life considered *over
time*. Free will presupposes an open future.

**If time is not what it seems, several of those questions change shape.** And
the physics says it is not what it seems.

---

## The physics (Tier 1 on the results, Tier 2–3 on interpretation)

**Simultaneity is relative.** Special relativity: whether two spatially
separated events happen "at the same time" has no observer-independent answer.
Different observers in relative motion genuinely disagree, and neither is wrong.

**This is not a measurement artefact.** There is no fact about what is
"happening now" on a distant galaxy.

**Time dilation is measured, not theoretical.** Clocks run at different rates
depending on velocity and gravitational potential. GPS satellites require
relativistic correction to function — **the effect is engineered around daily.**

**There is no "now" in physics.** The equations contain no present moment. They
describe a four-dimensional structure in which "now" has no more privileged
status than "here."

## The block universe versus presentism

**Eternalism / block universe.** Past, present and future all exist. The
universe is a four-dimensional block; "now" is indexical, like "here." Your
birth and death both exist in the structure; you experience one point at a time
the way you occupy one place at a time.

**The argument for it is strong:** relativity's relativity of simultaneity is
very hard to reconcile with the view that only the present exists. **Whose
present?** Putnam and Rietdijk formalised this — the Andromeda paradox — showing
that two observers walking past each other on Earth can have "presents" that
differ by days at galactic distance.

**Presentism.** Only the present exists. The past is gone, the future is not
yet. **This is what everyone believes intuitively** and it takes work to reconcile
with relativity.

**The growing block.** Past and present exist; the future does not. An
intermediate position, less popular.

**Status: eternalism is probably the majority view among philosophers of
physics.** It is not settled, and there are physicists who reject the
inference from relativity to eternalism. **Tier 2, leaning eternalist.**

## If eternalism is true, what follows?

Genuinely interesting, and the notebook should be careful not to overclaim.

**What plausibly follows:**
- **Your death is not an annihilation of your past.** The period in which you existed does not stop having existed. Einstein wrote something like this to the family of his friend Besso — that the distinction between past, present and future is a stubbornly persistent illusion. *(This is a real letter and it is frequently quoted more consolingly than its context supports.)*
- **The passage of time is not a feature of the world.** It is a feature of how a memory-forming, entropy-increasing system embedded in the block experiences it.

**What does not follow:**
- **Fatalism.** "The future already exists" does not mean your choices don't matter. Your choices are *part of* the structure — the block contains your deliberating, and it contains the outcome that depends on it. This is exactly the same error as fatalism about determinism (free-will.md).
- **That death doesn't matter.** The deprivation account (death.md) survives: the block contains a finite amount of you, and more would have been better.

**Honest note:** the consolation people take from eternalism — "the good moments
are permanently real" — may be sound, and it may be a comforting reading of a
technical claim. Treat it as Tier 3 for practical purposes.

## The arrow of time

**The genuine puzzle.** Almost all fundamental physical laws are
time-symmetric — they work equally well run backwards. **Yet time manifestly has
a direction.** Eggs break and don't unbreak.

**The standard answer:** the arrow is **thermodynamic**. Entropy increases,
which gives a direction. But this only pushes the question back: entropy
increases *because the universe started in an extraordinarily low-entropy
state.* The **past hypothesis** — that the early universe was in a very
low-entropy configuration — is assumed rather than explained.

**Why the early universe had such low entropy is an open problem.** It may be
the most important unanswered question in physics that most people have never
heard of.

**Derived arrows, all pointing the same way:**
- **Memory.** You remember the past and not the future — because recording requires an entropy increase.
- **Causation.** Causes precede effects.
- **Psychological.** The sense of moving forward.

**These are all downstream of thermodynamics.** You experience time as passing
because you are an entropy-increasing system that accumulates records.

## Why time *feels* like it passes

**Tier 2, and separable from the metaphysics.**

- **The specious present.** Your "now" is not instantaneous. It's an integration window, commonly estimated at a few hundred milliseconds to a few seconds. **You are experiencing a smear, not a point.**
- **Your consciousness lags reality** by roughly 80–100ms, and the brain compensates by back-dating events so it doesn't feel delayed. **You live slightly in the past and are not permitted to notice.**
- **Duration is constructed, not measured.** Time perception varies with attention, arousal, novelty, drugs, temperature and emotion. Fear can produce apparent slowing — though the evidence suggests this is a memory-density effect (more recorded detail) rather than genuinely faster perception.
- **Why years accelerate with age.** Two leading explanations: proportional (a year is a smaller fraction of your life) and **novelty-density** — new experiences create dense memories, and routine creates sparse ones, so a familiar year leaves less trace and feels shorter in retrospect.

**The practical consequence of the second explanation is unusually concrete:
novelty is the only known lever on subjective lifespan.** A life of routine is
literally shorter in memory. This is the most actionable finding in the file.

## Time and the questions in this notebook

**Personal identity.** If eternalism holds, "you" are a four-dimensional worm
extended through time, not a three-dimensional object that persists. **Perdurance
theory** says you have temporal parts the way you have spatial parts — your
childhood is a part of you the way your hand is. This meshes remarkably well
with Parfit: **there is no thin persisting self travelling through time, there
is an extended structure.**

**Death.** If your existence is a bounded region of the block, death is an edge,
not an annihilation. Whether that's consoling is temperament. **The deprivation
account is unaffected** — a longer worm would have contained more.

**Free will.** Eternalism doesn't add a problem beyond determinism, and the
anti-fatalist reply is identical.

**Meaning.** If moments are permanently real rather than passing away, the
common intuition that impermanence drains value is at least questionable. **Or
the reverse** — some argue value depends on scarcity and an infinitely repeatable
moment would be worthless. Both readings are available and neither is settled.

## The traditions on time

- **Buddhism:** *anicca* — impermanence is a mark of existence. Momentariness in some schools: things exist only for an instant and are continuously replaced. **Practically eternalism's opposite, and it reaches the same no-self conclusion.**
- **Stoicism:** only the present is available to you; the past and future are not yours to act on. *(Practically presentist, whatever the metaphysics.)*
- **Hinduism:** vast cyclical time — *yugas*, *kalpas* — on scales that dwarf Western historical imagination and are unusually compatible with cosmological timescales.
- **Abrahamic:** linear time with a beginning and an end. History goes somewhere. **This is the assumption underlying Western ideas of progress**, secularised.
- **Augustine's puzzle:** he asked what time is, and observed that he knew until someone asked him. His analysis — that past and future exist only in the present as memory and expectation — is still cited.

---

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"Time is an illusion."** Sloppy. What's arguably illusory is *passage* and the objective present. Temporal *structure* and the ordering of causally connected events are entirely real.
- **"Relativity means everything is relative."** No. It means some quantities are observer-dependent and others — the speed of light, spacetime interval, causal order between causally connectable events — are absolute.
- **"Physics proves the block universe."** Relativity makes presentism very difficult. It does not strictly entail eternalism, and this is disputed.
- **"Time slows down in emergencies."** Evidence suggests denser memory encoding, not faster perception. Experiments testing whether people can read fast-flashing displays during frightening free-fall found they could not.
- **"Einstein proved your loved ones still exist."** The Besso letter is real and is routinely quoted stripped of its context and its technical meaning.

---

## The practical residue

1. **Novelty is the only known lever on subjective time.** Routine compresses years in memory. If you want a longer-feeling life, the mechanism is new experiences, not more hours.
2. **You are always slightly behind reality**, and your brain hides this. Another entry for the "introspection is unreliable" ledger.
3. **The present is the only place action is possible** — true regardless of which metaphysics wins, which is why every tradition converges on it.
4. **Anti-fatalism holds under eternalism too.** Your deliberation is part of the structure, not a spectator to it.

# Practices — What To Actually Do

> The non-preachy version. Nothing here follows *necessarily* from the
> philosophy — **no fact entails a way of living**, and any file claiming
> otherwise is smuggling values. What follows is what survives when you take the
> evidence seriously and discard what doesn't hold up.

---

## First: sort your question

Before anything else, from the-questions.md.

**If the question arrives with curiosity** — "what is consciousness," "is there
a self," "why is there anything" — philosophy is the right tool. Read on.

**If it arrives with dread, exhaustion, emptiness or hopelessness** —
philosophy is the *wrong* tool and will often make it worse. Existential
rumination is a well-known feature of depression and exhaustion, and it presents
itself convincingly as insight. The characteristic tell is that it feels like
finally seeing clearly, and it produces no action.

In that case the effective interventions are unglamorous and well evidenced:
sleep, food, daylight, physical movement, and talking to another human — a
friend, a GP, a therapist. **If there are thoughts of self-harm, that is a
medical situation, not a philosophical one, and contacting a crisis line or a
doctor is the correct move.**

These are different problems with different solutions, and mistaking the second
for the first is the most common and most costly error in this whole domain.

## The five that are actually supported

Ranked by strength of evidence, not by how profound they sound.

### 1. Invest in relationships, disproportionately
**Evidence: Tier 1. The strongest finding in the notebook.**

Two independent literatures converge — social connection predicts physical
health outcomes (comparable in magnitude to major lifestyle risk factors) and
it is the single strongest correlate of reported meaning.

It is also the finding people most reliably ignore, because it is boring and
because relationships are slow, effortful and unquantifiable.

**Operationally:** the constraint is almost never desire; it's initiation and
frequency. Adult friendship decays through absence, not conflict. Whoever
schedules the thing is the reason it happens.

### 2. Do something demanding that isn't about you
**Evidence: Tier 2, consistent.**

Meaning correlates with contribution and with absorption in difficult
activity — Csikszentmihalyi's flow, and the "significance" component of the
meaning literature. Both halves matter: it should be **hard** (absorption
requires challenge matched to skill) and it should be **directed outward**
(pure self-focus doesn't produce it).

**Wolf's hybrid criterion applies:** you must actually care about it, and it
must be worth caring about. Either alone fails.

### 3. Treat your time as the scarce resource, because it is
**Evidence: Tier 1 on the premise, observational on the regret data.**

You will treat money as scarce and time as abundant. The reverse is true and it
never feels true.

**The deathbed-regret regularities** — reported consistently by palliative care
workers and in retrospective research — cluster around: things not done,
relationships allowed to lapse, time given to work, and lives lived to other
people's expectations. *Self-reported and subject to selection effects; take it
as suggestive.* The consistency is nonetheless notable, and the regrets are
about **omission, not risk.**

**Operationally:** audit where the hours go, not where the money goes. Most
people have never done this once.

### 4. Examine what you're clinging to
**Evidence: strongest cross-tradition convergence in the notebook.**

Buddhism (*taṇhā*), Stoicism (the dichotomy of control), Christianity
(attachment to worldly goods), and clinical psychology (rumination, cognitive
distortion) all independently identify **unexamined craving and the demand that
things be otherwise** as the main engine of avoidable suffering.

When four traditions with incompatible metaphysics agree on a practical
diagnosis, that's the best signal available in a soft domain.

**Operationally, the Stoic version is the most portable:** for whatever is
distressing you, sort it into what is up to you (your judgement, your response,
your next action) and what isn't (outcomes, other people, the past). Then act on
the first column and stop negotiating with the second.

### 5. Interpretation mediates emotion — and interpretation is editable
**Evidence: Tier 1–2. Clearest ancient-to-modern transmission in the notebook.**

Epictetus said people are disturbed not by things but by their views of things.
CBT operationalised exactly this claim and it has a substantial evidence base.

**Operationally:** the gap between event and response is where the leverage is.
Noticing the interpretation — writing it down is the standard technique, because
it's much easier to evaluate a sentence than a feeling — is the whole method.

## Three that are worth trying, with weaker warrant

**Meditation.** Reasonable evidence for stress reduction and attention. Weaker
and more contested evidence for the larger claims. Adverse effects exist and are
under-reported — intensive practice can destabilise some people. **Separate the
practice from the metaphysics** (epistemics.md, principle 7): you can test
whether it helps you without adopting Buddhist cosmology.

**Memento mori / negative visualisation.** The Stoic practices of contemplating
mortality and rehearsing loss. Long practical track record; limited formal
evidence. The mechanism is **proportion** — most anxieties don't survive contact
with the fact of finitude. Overdone, it becomes morbidity.

**Writing a coherent narrative.** People who can tell a story that makes sense
of their life, including the bad parts, report more meaning — and **redemptive
framings** (difficulty led to growth) correlate with wellbeing. Expressive
writing has a modest evidence base. The caution: narratives can be false, and
"everything happens for a reason" is both unevidenced and cruel when applied to
other people's catastrophes.

## Practical consequences of the specific positions

If the arguments in the files hold, these follow fairly directly.

**From no-self (personal-identity.md):**
- The person who wronged you years ago is connected to today's person by a chain, not an identity. **Not automatic forgiveness — a change in what you think you're holding.**
- Changing substantially isn't betraying an essence. There's no essence to betray.
- Self-criticism is being levelled at a process by the same process. Notice how strange that is.

**From free will (free-will.md):**
- Nobody chose their starting conditions. **This should change how you judge others substantially and how you judge yourself more.**
- Guilt as information about what to change: useful. Guilt as deserved suffering: no foundation.
- **Fatalism does not follow.** Your deliberating is part of the causal chain. Caused choices still have effects.

**From death (death.md):**
- **Do the practical work.** A will, an advance directive, saying the things you'd regret leaving unsaid. This reliably reduces death anxiety more than thinking about death does.
- Fear of *dying* (pain, indignity, loss of control) and fear of *being dead* are separable. The first is substantially addressable through medical and practical planning.

**From meaning (meaning.md):**
- **Meaning ≠ happiness.** Children, caregiving, and difficult work reliably reduce measured moment-to-moment happiness and increase reported meaning. Choosing between them is a real choice.
- **"Find your passion" is bad advice.** Interest generally follows competence and contribution. Waiting to discover a pre-existing passion is a common way to lose a decade.

**From society (society.md):**
- Needing people is what the organism is, not a weakness. **The self-sufficiency ideal has no biological basis.**
- Loneliness functions like hunger — a signal, not a character defect.

## What this notebook will not tell you

**What to do with your life.** That's the-questions.md row (d): not a knowledge
question. No file, expert, tradition or argument can answer it, and every source
that claims to is substituting its values for yours.

What the notebook can do is clear away the malformed versions — so that the
answerable parts become visible and you stop demanding answers from disciplines
that can't supply them.

## The honest summary

If you strip everything to what's actually load-bearing:

1. **You are a process, not a thing** — a pattern that persists while its material flows through. (Tier 1–2.)
2. **There is probably no persisting self** — four independent methods agree. (Tier 2, unusually strong.)
3. **Death is almost certainly annihilation.** (Tier 1 confidence, not certainty.)
4. **Nobody knows why there is anything, and nobody is going to.** (Tier 4.)
5. **Consciousness is genuinely unexplained** and you are permitted to sit with that. (Tier 4 → 2.)
6. **Meaning requires both caring and something worth caring about.** (Tier 2.)
7. **You need other people, biologically, and this is the best-supported and most-ignored finding in the whole file.** (Tier 1.)

**Six of those seven are uncertain. The one that's settled is the one nobody
finds interesting.**

That's not a disappointment. It's the actual result, and it points somewhere
specific: the questions that feel deepest pay the least, and the practical
answer was never going to be found by thinking harder about it alone.

# Meaning

> **Status: Tier 2/3.** Cosmic purpose is unevidenced. Whether anything is
> *objectively* worth doing is genuinely argued. That subjective meaning exists
> and can be studied is not in doubt.

---

## Three things called "meaning" — keep them apart

Nearly every argument in this area is caused by conflating these.

**1. Cosmic meaning.** The universe has a purpose; you have an assigned role in
it. **Status: unevidenced.** No scientific finding supports it; none refutes it.

**2. Objective value.** Some things are genuinely worth doing whether or not
anyone wants to do them — reducing suffering, understanding truth, creating
beauty. **Status: seriously argued both directions.** This does not require a
cosmic plan.

**3. Subjective meaning.** Your life feels significant to you; you have purposes
you care about. **Status: obviously real, empirically studied.**

**Nihilism is usually a claim about (1).** People who assert it are typically
heard as denying (3), which is why the conversation goes badly. You can hold
that the universe has no plan and that your daughter matters enormously without
any contradiction.

## The positions

### Nihilism
**Claim:** nothing matters, in any of the three senses. Value is an illusion.

**Strongest form:** if there is no cosmic purpose and no mind-independent
value, then "mattering" has no referent. Our sense that things matter is an
evolved heuristic, not a perception of anything.

**Weakest point:** it is nearly impossible to live and is arguably
self-undermining — the nihilist who argues for nihilism appears to think getting
it right matters. Most people who describe themselves as nihilists are actually
disappointed objectivists: they wanted (1), found it absent, and concluded (2)
and (3) went with it.

### Absurdism (Camus)
**Claim:** the absurd is not a property of the universe or of us. It is the
**relationship** between a human demand for meaning and a universe that offers
none. Neither term is absurd alone; the confrontation is.

Camus identified three responses and rejected two:
- **Suicide** — rejected. It resolves the tension by eliminating one term.
- **The "leap of faith"** — adopting religion or an ideology to supply the missing meaning. Rejected as "philosophical suicide": it also eliminates a term, by pretending the universe answers.
- **Revolt** — accept the absurd, refuse to resolve it, and live fully anyway.

**Sisyphus** is the image: condemned to roll a boulder uphill forever, watching
it roll back, for eternity. Camus's conclusion is that we must imagine Sisyphus
happy — because the moment of walking back down the hill is his, and his
consciousness of his fate is precisely his victory over it.

**Why it holds up:** it is the only major position that neither invents an
answer nor collapses into despair. It requires no metaphysical commitments.

**Where it's vulnerable:** "revolt" is more of a stance than an argument, and it
is not obvious why lucid defiance is better than lucid resignation.

### Existentialism (Sartre, de Beauvoir)
**Claim:** *existence precedes essence.* A paper-knife is designed with a
purpose in mind — essence first. A human is not. You exist first, and then make
yourself through your choices.

Consequences Sartre drew:
- **Radical freedom.** You are always choosing, including when you choose not to.
- **"Condemned to be free."** The freedom is not a gift; it is a burden, because there's no external standard to appeal to.
- **Bad faith (*mauvaise foi*)** — the most useful concept in existentialism. Pretending you have no choice: "I couldn't, because of my job / my family / my nature." Sartre's example is the waiter performing "being a waiter" a bit too precisely, hiding from the fact that he is choosing it moment by moment.
- **Anguish** — the vertigo of realising nothing outside you validates your choices.

**De Beauvoir's correction, and it's an important one:** Sartre's freedom is too
abstract. Real freedom is constrained by situation — by gender, class, body,
poverty, oppression. **Freedom is real and it is not evenly distributed.** *The
Ethics of Ambiguity* is the better book on this.

**Vulnerability:** if you create your values, why these values? Existentialism
has a persistent difficulty explaining why one choice is better than another,
which is why it can shade into aestheticism.

### Objective value / naturalism
**Claim:** you don't need a cosmic plan for some things to genuinely matter.
Suffering is bad — not "bad relative to a preference," but bad. That's not a
claim about the universe having intentions; it's a claim about the nature of
suffering.

**Strongest point:** it takes seriously that moral intuitions are stubborn and
convergent in ways that pure subjectivism struggles to explain. Almost nobody
actually believes gratuitous cruelty is merely disfavoured.

**Weakest point:** where does objective value live, in a physical universe?
Mackie's "argument from queerness" — such properties would be metaphysically
unlike anything else we know.

### Susan Wolf's hybrid — the strongest position in this file
**Claim:** "Meaning arises when subjective attraction meets objective
attractiveness." Meaning requires **both**:

- **Subjective engagement.** You must actually care. A life spent dutifully doing worthy things you're dead inside about is not meaningful.
- **Objective worth.** The object must be worth caring about. Her example: a life devoted entirely to memorising phone books, or to a corrupt but absorbing pursuit, is not made meaningful by intensity of engagement.

**Why this is the best available answer:** it explains the two failure modes we
actually recognise. The bored high-achiever who does everything right and feels
nothing. And the passionate obsessive whose passion is directed at something
empty. **Both are recognisable, and pure subjectivism can only explain one.**

It also matches the psychological evidence below, which is a point in its
favour.

### Religious answers
**Claim:** meaning is given, not made — by a creator, a *dharma*, a covenant,
a divine purpose.

**Strongest point:** if true, it is the only position that supplies meaning
that is not ultimately contingent on human attitudes. It also has an
enormously better practical track record than the alternatives at sustaining
people through catastrophe — this is an empirical observation, not an argument
for truth.

**Weakest point:** it requires the metaphysics to be true, and it faces the
**Euthyphro problem** — is something good because God commands it, or does God
command it because it's good? If the former, goodness is arbitrary. If the
latter, goodness is independent of God and you haven't grounded it in God after
all.

## What the empirical research says

Separate from the metaphysics, meaning in life is a studied psychological
construct, and the findings are reasonably robust.

Researchers generally decompose it into three components:
- **Coherence** — your life makes sense; events fit a comprehensible pattern.
- **Purpose** — you have goals and direction.
- **Significance** — your existence matters; it has worth.

Consistent correlates of reported meaning:
- **Relationships.** The strongest and most consistent predictor across studies. Social connection outperforms nearly everything else measured.
- **Contribution to something beyond yourself.** Work, care, craft, community.
- **Coherent narrative.** People who can tell a story that makes sense of their life — including its bad parts — report more meaning. **Redemptive narratives** (McAdams) — framing difficulty as leading to growth — correlate with wellbeing.
- **Absorption / flow** (Csikszentmihalyi) — engagement in activities matched to skill.

Also consistently found:
- **Meaning and happiness are distinguishable.** Raising children reliably reduces moment-to-moment happiness measures and reliably increases reported meaning. So does caring for a dying relative. **A meaningful life is not the same as a pleasant one**, and confusing them causes real damage.
- **Meaning appears more strongly linked to giving than receiving.**

*Caveat: this literature is self-report-heavy, cross-sectional in large part,
and subject to the replication problems affecting social psychology. Directional
findings are more trustworthy than effect sizes.*

## Frankl, and why his case is unusual

Viktor Frankl survived Auschwitz and wrote *Man's Search for Meaning*, arguing
that the fundamental human drive is toward meaning, and that meaning can be
found in any circumstance — through work, through love, or **through the
attitude one takes toward unavoidable suffering.**

His central claim: **"Everything can be taken from a man but one thing: the
last of the human freedoms — to choose one's attitude in any given set of
circumstances."**

**Why it carries weight:** he wasn't theorising from a study. He observed that
prisoners who lost a sense of future purpose deteriorated and died quickly, and
he was there.

**Two honest caveats:**
1. **Survivorship bias, in the most literal sense possible.** We have this book because he lived. The observation about purpose and survival cannot be separated from the enormous role of luck in who survived.
2. His logotherapy has a modest formal evidence base, and the book is better read as testimony than as clinical validation.

The core insight — that the last available freedom is the response, and that it
is not nothing — survives both caveats.

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"Find your passion."** Weakly supported and often harmful. The evidence points more toward **passion following mastery and contribution** than preceding them. Waiting to discover a pre-existing passion is a common way to do nothing for a decade.
- **"Follow your bliss / do what makes you happy."** Happiness and meaning diverge. Optimising for moment-to-moment pleasure predicts a life that feels empty in retrospect.
- **"Science has shown life is meaningless."** Science addresses claim (1) not at all — it is silent on purpose, because purpose isn't the kind of thing its methods detect. Absence of evidence in a domain your instrument can't measure isn't evidence of absence.
- **"Just choose your own meaning."** Too easy. If meaning were purely chosen, choosing to find meaning in counting blades of grass would work, and it doesn't. **This is exactly what Wolf's hybrid view is designed to fix.**
- **"Camus said life is meaningless so do what you want."** He said the *absurd* is the relationship between our demand and the silence, and that the honest response is lucid revolt — which is demanding, not permissive.
- **"Frankl proved meaning cures suffering."** He testified to something. It isn't a controlled finding.

## The practical residue

If you strip out everything unresolvable, this is what's left standing:

1. **Sort which of the three meanings you're asking about.** Most distress here is about (1) and most of the answer is available in (2) and (3).
2. **Meaning is more reliably found in engagement than in reflection.** Nobody has ever thought their way to it. The evidence points at doing, contributing, and connecting.
3. **Both halves are required.** Care about something (subjective) that is actually worth caring about (objective). One without the other reliably fails.
4. **Relationships are the strongest single predictor** in the empirical literature. This is boring and it is what the data says.
5. **Meaning ≠ happiness.** Choosing between them is a real choice, and choosing meaning will sometimes make you less comfortable.

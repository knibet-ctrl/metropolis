# Ethics — How To Act

> **The other gap.** `meaning.md` asks whether life matters. It never asks what
> you should *do*. Those are different questions and the second is more urgent.

---

## Why this is separate from meaning

You can have a meaningful life doing harm. Napoleon's life was meaningful by
every measure in `meaning.md` — engagement, significance, coherence,
contribution to something larger. **Meaning is not a moral category.**

The leaders notebook makes this uncomfortably clear: Genghis Khan, Caesar and
Mao all ran the mechanisms of a significant life. Effectiveness and meaning are
both orthogonal to goodness.

## The three main frameworks

Each is strongest exactly where the others are weakest, which is why the debate
has run for 2,500 years without resolution.

### Consequentialism
**Claim:** the rightness of an act depends on its outcomes. Utilitarianism:
maximise wellbeing, minimise suffering, counting everyone equally.

**Strongest points:**
- **It takes suffering seriously as the thing that matters.** Bentham's question about animals — not "can they reason" but "can they suffer" — reframed moral consideration permanently.
- **Impartiality.** Your child's suffering and a stranger's count the same. Uncomfortable, and it's the engine behind most moral progress: abolition, suffrage, animal welfare.
- **It's actionable.** You can estimate consequences. This is why it dominates policy analysis.

**Strongest objections:**
- **It permits atrocities in principle.** If harvesting one unwilling person's organs saves five, the arithmetic says do it. Every attempted patch (rule utilitarianism, indirect utilitarianism) weakens the theory's directness.
- **Demandingness.** Taken seriously, you should give until further giving costs you more than it helps others. Singer accepts this. Almost nobody lives it.
- **You cannot actually compute it.** Consequences ramify indefinitely and unpredictably.

### Deontology
**Claim:** some acts are right or wrong regardless of outcome. Duties and rights
constrain what may be done to a person even for good ends.

**Kant's core:** act only on principles you could will to be universal law, and
treat humanity **never merely as a means but always also as an end.**

**Strongest points:**
- **It explains rights**, which consequentialism struggles with. Rights are precisely things that cannot be overridden by aggregate benefit.
- **It matches strong moral intuitions.** Most people are certain that torturing an innocent is wrong even if it produces net benefit, and consequentialism has to explain that certainty away.
- **Dignity.** The prohibition on using people as instruments does real work.

**Strongest objections:**
- **Rigidity.** Kant's own position that lying is always wrong, even to a murderer asking where your friend is hiding, is widely regarded as a reductio.
- **Conflicting duties** with no principled resolution procedure.
- **Where do the duties come from?** The derivation is contested.

### Virtue ethics
**Claim:** the question isn't "what should I do" but **"what kind of person
should I become."** Right action flows from character. Aristotle: *eudaimonia* —
flourishing — achieved through cultivated excellence, with virtue as a mean
between extremes (courage between cowardice and recklessness).

**Strongest points:**
- **It matches how moral development actually works.** Nobody becomes good by memorising rules; they become good through habituation, practice and imitation of exemplars.
- **It handles the whole of life**, including what to care about, not just discrete dilemmas.
- **It explains moral perception** — the good person *notices* the morally relevant feature, which rule-based accounts take for granted.

**Strongest objections:**
- **Little action guidance.** "Do what the virtuous person would do" doesn't tell you what that is.
- **Cultural relativity of virtue lists.**
- **Situationist critique:** psychology suggests behaviour is driven more by situation than by stable character traits. *(Note: this critique leaned heavily on studies — including Milgram and Stanford — that have since been substantially reassessed. The critique is weaker than it looked in 2000.)*

## Where the frameworks converge

The disagreements are famous; the convergence is more useful and rarely stated.

**All three condemn:** gratuitous cruelty, betrayal of trust, exploitation of
the vulnerable, dishonesty for personal gain, indifference to suffering you
could cheaply prevent.

**All three endorse:** honesty, keeping commitments, reciprocity, care for
dependants, some obligation to strangers.

**The frameworks disagree at the margins and agree in the centre.** Almost every
actual moral decision you will face falls in the centre. **The trolley problems
are pedagogically useful and practically irrelevant** — you will spend your life
in cases where all three theories give the same answer and the difficulty is
doing it, not knowing it.

**This is the most practically important sentence in the file: the hard part of
ethics is almost never knowing what's right. It's cost, courage and
self-deception.**

## Where morality comes from — and why it doesn't settle anything

**The descriptive account (Tier 2, well developed).** Moral intuitions have
evolutionary origins — kin selection, reciprocal altruism, reputation, and
altruistic punishment (society.md). Haidt's moral foundations work suggests
several recurring dimensions across cultures (care, fairness, loyalty,
authority, sanctity, liberty) weighted differently by group and political
disposition. *Contested in its details; the general point that intuitions come
first and reasoning follows has good support.*

**The is-ought gap (Hume).** You cannot derive an *ought* from an *is*. That
humans evolved to feel something is wrong does not establish that it is wrong.

**Why this cuts both ways:**
- It blocks "it's natural, therefore it's right" (the naturalistic fallacy).
- It also blocks "it's just evolved intuition, therefore it's not real" (the genetic fallacy). **Explaining the origin of a belief doesn't refute it** — the evolutionary origin of our arithmetic intuitions doesn't make 2+2=4 false.

**Moral realism vs anti-realism** remains genuinely open (Tier 2–3). But note:
**almost nobody lives as an anti-realist.** People who deny objective moral facts
still experience cruelty to children as wrong rather than as disfavoured. That
isn't an argument, but the gap between stated and lived positions is data of a
kind.

## The practical framework that actually works

Not a fourth theory — a procedure. Use the frameworks as **lenses**, since each
catches what the others miss.

**1. Consequences.** Who is affected, and how? Include people you'll never meet
and effects you won't see. *Catches: harm you didn't intend.*

**2. Constraints.** Am I using someone merely as a means? Am I violating
something they're entitled to regardless of the benefit? *Catches:
rationalisation via arithmetic.*

**3. Character.** What does doing this repeatedly make me? Would I be
comfortable if it were known? *Catches: exceptions you're making for yourself.*

**4. Rawls's veil.** Would I accept this rule if I didn't know which position
I'd occupy? *Catches: self-serving rules.*

**5. The universalisation check.** What if everyone in my situation did this?
*Catches: free-riding.*

**If all five agree, act. If they conflict, you have located a genuine dilemma
— and that is much rarer than people pretend.** Most of the time the lenses
agree and the difficulty is elsewhere.

## The failure modes — where ethics actually goes wrong

This is the part worth internalising, because it's where the real damage is.

**1. Motivated reasoning.** You reach the conclusion you wanted and construct
the argument afterwards. **Haidt's metaphor: intuition is the elephant,
reasoning is the rider, and the rider mostly serves the elephant.** The tell is
speed — if the justification arrived instantly, it's a rationalisation.

**2. Scope insensitivity.** People donate roughly the same to save 2,000 birds
as 200,000. **Our moral emotions do not scale**, which is why distant mass
suffering produces less response than one identifiable victim. This is a known
bug and it can be partially corrected by deliberately checking numbers.

**3. Diffusion of responsibility.** In a system, everyone is following
procedure and nobody is responsible for the outcome. This is how most
large-scale institutional harm happens — not through villains.

**4. Moral licensing.** Having done something good, people permit themselves
something worse. *(The specific studies have replication issues; the pattern is
recognisable enough to watch for.)*

**5. Gradualism.** Each step is only slightly worse than the last, and no single
step is the one you'd refuse. **This is how Theranos happened** — see the
profile. The relevant question is never "is this step acceptable" but "would I
accept where this sequence ends?"

**6. In-group narrowing.** Moral concern contracts under threat. Almost all
atrocity involves first placing a group outside the circle of moral
consideration.

**7. Confusing effectiveness with virtue.** The leaders notebook's central
warning. Genghis Khan was extraordinarily effective. **Method and morality are
separate axes**, and the biography industry blurs them constantly.

## The uncomfortable conclusions

**1. You are almost certainly complicit in something.** Supply chains, animal
agriculture, carbon, the distribution of global wealth. Every framework
implicates ordinary participation in ordinary systems. **The honest response is
not paralysis and not denial, but choosing where to actually accept cost.**

**2. Impartiality is correct in theory and unlivable in practice.** No framework
handles this well. Special obligations to your own children are near-universally
felt and hard to justify impartially. *(Partial resolution: a world where people
care especially for their own dependants may produce better outcomes than one
where they don't. Consequentialism can license partiality — but it does so
instrumentally, which most parents would find insulting.)*

**3. Moral luck (Bernard Williams, Nagel).** Two equally reckless drivers; one
happens to hit a child. We judge them very differently, and it is not clear this
is defensible. **How good a person you get to be depends substantially on
circumstances you didn't choose** — which connects directly to free-will.md.

**4. Knowing is the easy part.** Nearly all moral failure is failure of nerve,
cost-avoidance, or self-deception. The leaders notebook found the same thing:
**every figure paid a specific price their equally capable peers declined to
pay.** That's a moral finding disguised as a strategic one.

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"Ethics is subjective, so anything goes."** Anti-realism doesn't entail that all positions are equally defensible, and virtually no philosopher holds this.
- **"The trolley problem is what ethics is about."** Edge cases used to test theories, not a model of moral life.
- **"Utilitarians would harvest your organs."** No serious utilitarian defends this; the standard reply concerns the consequences of living in a society where it's permitted.
- **"Kant said never lie, so deontology is absurd."** Kant did say it and most deontologists don't follow him there.
- **"Evolution explains morality, so morality is fake."** Genetic fallacy. Origin ≠ validity.
- **"Milgram and Stanford proved anyone can be made evil."** Both have been substantially reassessed — Stanford in particular has severe methodological problems. Still widely taught as settled.
- **"Moral progress proves moral realism."** Suggestive. It could also be expanding self-interest through interdependence.

# Free Will

> **Status: Tier 2, with one Tier 1 component.** The physics is largely settled.
> The dispute is about what deserves the name "free will" — and, much more
> importantly, what follows for responsibility.

---

## The setup

Three positions, and it's worth being precise about them because the debate is
notoriously muddied by people meaning different things.

**Hard determinism.** Every event, including every decision, is fully caused by
prior events plus the laws of nature. You could not have done otherwise. Free
will is an illusion.

**Libertarian free will.** Some choices are genuinely up to you in a way not
fully determined by prior states. You could have done otherwise, holding
everything about the past fixed.

**Compatibilism.** Determinism is true *and* free will is real, because free
will was never the ability to violate causation. It is the capacity to act on
your own reasons and desires without external compulsion. **This is the
majority position among academic philosophers**, which surprises most people.

---

## What the physics actually says

**Tier 1, and the crucial point is usually missed:**

Classical physics is deterministic. Quantum mechanics introduces genuine
indeterminacy at small scales (on standard interpretations).

**Neither helps.**

If your decision is determined by prior causes, you didn't originate it. If your
decision is partly random, **you didn't originate that either** — a quantum coin
flip in your neurons is not you deciding; it's noise. Randomness gives you
unpredictability, not authorship.

**This is the argument that closes off libertarian free will, and it does not
depend on which physics turns out to be right.** Determinism and randomness both
fail to deliver the thing wanted. Libertarians must argue for a third kind of
causation — **agent causation**, where a self originates a causal chain without
being caused to. No one has given a coherent physical account of what that
would be.

**The consequence argument (Peter van Inwagen)**, stated cleanly:
1. If determinism is true, my actions follow from the distant past plus the laws.
2. I have no control over the distant past or the laws.
3. Therefore I have no control over what follows from them.

Compatibilists reject premise 3's notion of "control," which is exactly where
the real argument lives.

## The neuroscience — and why it proves less than advertised

**Libet's experiments (1983).** Subjects flexed a wrist whenever they felt like
it and reported the moment they became aware of the urge, by noting the position
of a fast clock hand. EEG showed a **readiness potential** in motor cortex
beginning several hundred milliseconds **before** the reported conscious
intention.

Widely reported as: **the brain decides before you know it.**

**Soon et al. (2008)** used fMRI and pushed the lead time to several seconds for
a left/right button choice, with modest predictive accuracy.

**The objections are serious and largely successful:**

1. **The tasks are trivial and arbitrary.** "Flex whenever you feel like it" has no reasons on either side. This is precisely the kind of decision we'd least expect to involve deliberation. Generalising to "should I take this job" is unwarranted.
2. **The readiness potential may not be a decision.** Schurger and colleagues (2012) argued it reflects **spontaneous neural fluctuation** crossing a threshold — the accumulation of ongoing noise, not a hidden decision being made. On this reading, the RP is an artefact of averaging trials backwards from the moment of action.
3. **Timing self-reports are unreliable** at this resolution. The clock method has known problems.
4. **Predictive accuracy in Soon et al. was low** — well above chance, nowhere near determination.
5. **Libet himself proposed "free won't"** — that conscious veto remained possible in the window before action.

**Honest summary: the Libet literature is consistent with the absence of
libertarian free will, and it does not demonstrate it.** It is routinely
overstated in popular writing. **The philosophical argument from physics is much
stronger than the neuroscience**, and this is worth knowing — the empirical
result gets the headlines and does the least work.

## The compatibilist case, taken seriously

It's easy to dismiss compatibilism as redefining the term to win. That's unfair,
and the position deserves its best form.

**The core claim:** the thing we actually care about when we care about freedom
is not metaphysical origination. It is **the difference between acting on your
own reasons and being compelled.**

Consider the cases where we genuinely withhold responsibility:
- Someone physically forced your hand.
- You were coerced under threat.
- You have a brain tumour altering your impulse control.
- You are delusional and cannot perceive the situation accurately.
- You are a small child.

**None of these are cases where determinism failed.** Determinism is equally
true in all cases, including the ones where we hold people responsible. So
determinism cannot be the thing that undermines responsibility — **it doesn't
distinguish the cases we care about.**

What distinguishes them is whether the person's own reasoning and values were
the operative cause, and whether they were **responsive to reasons** — capable
of being moved by argument, evidence, and consequences.

**Frankfurt's hierarchical account:** you are free when your first-order desires
(wanting a drink) align with your second-order desires (wanting to want a
drink). An addict who wants to stop but drinks anyway is unfree in exactly the
sense that matters, and the unfreedom is entirely internal.

**Frankfurt cases** also attack the "could have done otherwise" requirement
directly: imagine a neuroscientist who will intervene if you're about to choose
B, but you choose A on your own. You could not have done otherwise — and you're
still responsible, because the actual cause of your action was you. **This is
the strongest single argument in compatibilism's favour** and it decouples
responsibility from alternate possibilities.

## Where the real disagreement is: desert

Both sides largely agree on the physics. **The dispute is about whether anyone
deserves to be punished or praised in a basic sense.**

**The hard incompatibilist position (Derk Pereboom, Galen Strawson, Gregg
Caruso):**

Strawson's **basic argument** is the sharpest thing in this file:
1. What you do follows from how you are.
2. To be responsible for what you do, you'd have to be responsible for how you are.
3. To be responsible for how you are, you'd have to have chosen to be that way.
4. But any such choice would follow from how you already were.
5. **Infinite regress. Therefore ultimate responsibility is impossible.**

You did not choose your genes, your parents, your culture, your childhood, your
neurochemistry, or the thousand contingencies that made you the sort of person
who chooses as you do.

**This argument is very hard to escape**, and it does not depend on
determinism — it goes through regardless.

**What follows practically, on this view:**
- **Retributive punishment is unjustified.** Nobody basically deserves suffering.
- **Forward-looking justification survives intact:** deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation, protection. Caruso's public-health-quarantine model is the developed version.
- **Resentment and self-loathing lose their footing.** This is where the view has genuine practical bite.

**The compatibilist reply:** responsibility practices are not premised on
metaphysical desert; they're a social technology for shaping reasons-responsive
agents. Holding someone responsible *is part of how they become responsible*.
Praise and blame are inputs to the causal system, not verdicts on a soul.

## What actually follows for you

This is where people go wrong, so it's worth being blunt.

**1. Fatalism does not follow, and the inference is a straightforward error.**
"It's all determined so nothing I do matters" is confused. **Your deliberation
is part of the causal chain.** A person who thinks carefully produces different
outcomes than one who doesn't. That the thinking was itself caused doesn't
remove it from the chain. Determinism says your choices have causes, not that
they have no effects.

**2. It should change how you judge others — substantially.** Nobody chose their
starting conditions. The person who is lazy, cruel, or addicted got there
through a causal history they did not author. This is not the same as saying
anything is acceptable, or that consequences should be removed. It is a reason
to prefer **understanding and prevention over contempt**, and to be sceptical of
punishment that serves no forward-looking purpose.

**3. It should change how you judge yourself — probably more.** Most people find
it easy to apply the argument outward and impossible to apply it inward. The
self-directed version is where the relief is: **your failures also had causes,
and most of them predate you.** Guilt as information about what to change is
useful. Guilt as deserved suffering has no foundation.

**4. Some caution.** There is a body of research suggesting that inducing
disbelief in free will can increase cheating and reduce helping in laboratory
settings. **The replication record on these specific studies is mixed and the
effects appear small.** Don't overweight it — but don't assume a person's
practical moral psychology is unaffected by what they believe here.

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"Quantum mechanics leaves room for free will."** It doesn't. Randomness isn't authorship. This is the most common confusion in the topic.
- **"Libet proved free will is an illusion."** He didn't. The tasks were trivial, the RP interpretation is disputed, and the philosophical argument does far more work.
- **"Neuroscientists have shown we're just machines."** The finding — that mental events depend on brain events — was never seriously in doubt and is not a discovery about freedom.
- **"Most philosophers are hard determinists."** The opposite. Surveys of professional philosophers consistently show **compatibilism as the plurality position**, by a wide margin.
- **"No free will means no morality."** Consequentialist and contractualist ethics don't require libertarian free will at all. Even most retributivists have had to reckon with this.

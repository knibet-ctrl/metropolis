# Traditions — What They Actually Claim

> **Status: descriptive, not evaluative on truth.** Presented as accurately as
> possible, including where they contradict each other. **They do disagree, and
> pretending otherwise is the central dishonesty of popular spirituality.**

---

## The rule for this file

**"All religions say the same thing" is false.**

Buddhism denies a permanent self. Vedanta affirms an eternal one. These are
direct contradictions on the most important question either addresses. Christian
theology asserts a single unrepeatable life followed by judgement; Hindu and
Buddhist frameworks assert rebirth. **These cannot all be true.**

The perennialist claim — that all traditions point at one underlying reality —
is itself a specific metaphysical position, mostly a nineteenth and
twentieth-century Western one, and it is not neutral. It tends to smooth over
precisely what practitioners consider essential.

**What *is* true:** they converge substantially on **practical ethics** —
reciprocity, restraint of greed and anger, care for the vulnerable, honesty.
Convergence on how to live, divergence on what is real.

## Buddhism

**Core diagnosis — the Four Noble Truths:**
1. *Dukkha* — existence is characterised by unsatisfactoriness. (Usually translated "suffering," which is too strong. Closer: the persistent friction of things not staying as we want.)
2. Its cause is *taṇhā* — craving, clinging, thirst.
3. It can cease.
4. There is a path — the Eightfold Path.

**The three marks of existence:**
- ***Anicca* — impermanence.** Everything changes, continuously, without exception.
- ***Dukkha* — unsatisfactoriness.** Because we cling to what changes.
- ***Anattā* — non-self.** There is no permanent, independent, unchanging self.

***Anattā* is the crux, and it is what distinguishes Buddhism from nearly
everything else.** Examine experience closely and you find sensations,
perceptions, mental formations and consciousness — the five *skandhas* — and no
owner behind them. **Not "the self is bad" but "look, and you won't find one."**

**Dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*):** everything arises in dependence
on conditions. Nothing has independent existence. This is the metaphysical
engine underneath the psychology.

**On rebirth:** the traditional teaching affirms it — with the twist that since
there is no self, **what is reborn is not a soul but a causal continuity**, like
one flame lighting another. Western secular Buddhism often quietly drops rebirth
entirely, which traditional practitioners regard as a significant amputation.
**Be aware which version you're reading.**

**The convergence worth noting:** *anattā* arrives at essentially the same
conclusion as Hume and Parfit (personal-identity.md) by an entirely different
method — introspective investigation rather than argument, roughly 2,300 years
earlier. **That is the single most striking cross-tradition agreement in the
notebook.**

**What it demands:** practice, not belief. The claims are presented as testable
by direct investigation, which is unusual among religious traditions and is part
of why it travels well.

## Stoicism

**Core claim: the dichotomy of control.** Some things are up to us — our
judgements, intentions, and responses. Most things are not — the body,
reputation, other people, outcomes, the past. **Suffering comes from treating
the second category as though it were the first.**

Epictetus, who was born enslaved: *"Men are disturbed not by things, but by the
views which they take of things."*

**Key practices:**
- ***Premeditatio malorum*** — deliberately rehearsing loss and misfortune, to reduce its shock and increase present gratitude.
- ***Memento mori*** — regular contemplation of mortality, for proportion.
- **The view from above** — imagining your situation from a great distance, in space and in time.
- ***Amor fati*** — not merely accepting what happens but willing it.

**The metaphysics people skip:** the Stoics were not modern self-help
practitioners. They believed the cosmos was rationally ordered by *logos*, that
virtue was the only genuine good, and that human reason was a fragment of
cosmic reason. **The equanimity was grounded in a claim that the universe is
rational and providential.** Modern Stoicism generally drops this — which is
legitimate, but it removes the foundation the ancients rested on.

**The honest criticism:** it can slide into passivity or into a refusal to feel
what should be felt. Stoic sources actually address this — the *preferred
indifferents* allow that health and wealth are genuinely worth pursuing — but
the popular version often lands as emotional suppression, which is not the same
as equanimity.

**Where it overlaps modern evidence:** cognitive behavioural therapy's core
premise — that interpretation mediates between event and emotional response —
is explicitly derived from Stoic sources. Albert Ellis said so directly. **This
is the tradition with the clearest line into contemporary clinical practice.**

## Advaita Vedanta

**Core claim: *Ātman* is *Brahman*.** The innermost self is identical with
ultimate reality. Not similar — identical. The apparent separateness of
individual selves and of self from world is *māyā*, a kind of superimposition or
mistaken cognition.

**The method:** *neti neti* — "not this, not this." Systematically negate
everything you can observe as an object. You are not the body — you observe it.
Not the emotions — you observe them. Not the thoughts. **What remains, which
cannot be made an object because it is the observing itself, is *Ātman*.**

**Liberation (*mokṣa*)** is not the acquisition of anything. It is the removal
of ignorance — recognising what was always the case.

**The direct conflict with Buddhism, stated plainly:**

| | Buddhism | Advaita Vedanta |
|---|---|---|
| Is there an unchanging self? | **No** (*anattā*) | **Yes** (*Ātman*) |
| What is the deepest reality? | Emptiness, dependent origination | *Brahman*, pure being-consciousness |
| What is liberation? | Cessation of craving | Recognition of identity with *Brahman* |

They used similar methods and reached opposite conclusions. **Both traditions
have argued about this with each other for over a thousand years. Anyone who
tells you they're saying the same thing has read neither carefully.**

## Daoism

**Core claim:** there is a natural way things move — the *Dao* — and human
suffering largely comes from forcing against it.

***Wu wei*** — usually translated "non-action," better understood as
**effortless or non-forcing action.** Not passivity. Acting with the grain
rather than against it. The image is water: soft, yielding, and it carves
canyons.

**The opening of the *Dao De Jing*** — the Dao that can be spoken is not the
eternal Dao — is a claim about the limits of language that runs through the
whole tradition. **Conceptualising is itself part of the problem.**

**Zhuangzi's butterfly dream:** he dreamt he was a butterfly, and on waking did
not know whether he was a man who had dreamt he was a butterfly or a butterfly
now dreaming he was a man. Not scepticism for its own sake — a challenge to the
firmness of the categories we impose.

**Contrast with Confucianism**, its domestic rival: Confucius sought to cultivate
virtue through ritual, role, hierarchy and social obligation. Daoism sought to
release the artificial constraints that social convention imposes. **China
carried both for two thousand years, and the tension is productive rather than
resolved.**

## The Abrahamic traditions

Grouped only for structure; the differences among them are substantial.

**Shared framework:** a single God who is personal and purposive; creation is
intentional rather than accidental; human life has assigned significance; there
is a moral order grounded in God; history is going somewhere.

**Judaism.** Covenant rather than primarily creed. Emphasis on right action
(*halakha*) and on argument as a religious practice — the Talmud preserves
minority opinions deliberately. **Notably reticent about the afterlife**
compared with its successors; the focus is this world and this people's
obligations. *Tikkun olam* — repairing the world.

**Christianity.** Grace over merit; the claim of incarnation; and — the
distinctive philosophical contribution — that the divine enters and suffers
finitude. The Augustinian tradition holds that human restlessness is itself
evidence of what it seeks: *"our heart is restless until it rests in Thee."*

**Islam.** *Tawhid* — the absolute oneness and non-composite nature of God.
Submission (*islam*) to God's will as the correct human posture. A highly
developed legal and philosophical tradition; Islamic philosophers (Ibn Sina, Ibn
Rushd) preserved and extended Greek thought and shaped medieval European
philosophy substantially. **Sufism** developed a sophisticated mystical strand
concerned with direct experience of the divine and annihilation of the ego
(*fana*).

**The strongest philosophical arguments in this family:**
- **Cosmological/contingency:** contingent things require a necessary being.
- **Fine-tuning:** physical constants appear improbably suited to permit complexity.
- **Moral realism:** objective moral facts are easier to ground with a God than without.

**The strongest objections:**
- **The problem of evil.** Gratuitous suffering — especially of children and animals — is very hard to reconcile with omnipotence and omnibenevolence. This remains the most serious objection and the theodicies are, to many, unsatisfying.
- **Divine hiddenness (Schellenberg):** if a relationship with God is the point, why is God's existence not evident to sincere seekers?
- **The regress problem:** invoking God to explain existence relocates the question rather than answering it.

**The notebook's own bias, stated:** the naturalist positions in these files are
treated in more depth than the theistic ones. That's an asymmetry in the
author's knowledge, not a verdict, and it's flagged in the README as something
to correct.

## Existentialism as a tradition

Covered in meaning.md, listed here for completeness. Its distinctive claim
relative to the above: **there is no given essence, so meaning is constructed
rather than discovered or received.** It sits in direct opposition to both the
Abrahamic (meaning is given) and the Vedantic (meaning is uncovered) positions,
and closer to Buddhism in denying a fixed self while rejecting Buddhism's
metaphysics.

## What they converge on, and what they don't

**Genuine convergence — practical ethics:**
- Some version of reciprocity appears in nearly all of them.
- Restraint of craving, anger and ego.
- Obligation to the poor and vulnerable.
- Honesty, and the corrosiveness of self-deception.
- **The view that unexamined desire is the primary source of avoidable suffering.** This is the strongest cross-tradition agreement in the file, and it is worth taking seriously precisely because it comes from independent sources.

**Genuine, irreducible disagreement:**
- Is there a permanent self? (Buddhism no, Vedanta yes, Abrahamic yes-as-soul)
- One life or many? (Abrahamic one, Indic many)
- Is the universe personal? (Abrahamic yes, Buddhism/Daoism no)
- Is liberation attained, given, or recognised?
- Is the material world real, illusory, fallen, or good?

**Methodological difference that matters:** Buddhism and Daoism present claims
as verifiable through practice. The Abrahamic traditions rest substantially on
revelation and authority. Stoicism rests on reason. **These are different
epistemologies, and evaluating one by another's standard is a category error.**

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"All religions teach the same thing."** They converge on ethics and diverge sharply on metaphysics. The divergence is what practitioners consider essential.
- **"Buddhism isn't a religion, it's a philosophy."** A Western repackaging. Traditional Buddhism has cosmology, rebirth, merit, devotional practice, and supernatural elements throughout.
- **"Stoicism means suppressing emotion."** It means examining the judgements that generate emotion. Stoic sources describe *eupatheiai* — good emotional states — as achievable.
- **"Mindfulness is a neutral secular technique."** It is a component extracted from a soteriological system and stripped of its ethical framework and its goal. It works for stress reduction; whether it does what the tradition claimed is a different question.
- **"Wu wei means do nothing."** Non-forcing, not non-doing.
- **"Eastern philosophy is intuitive, Western is rational."** Nonsense in both directions. Indian and Buddhist logic and epistemology are formidably technical; Western mysticism is extensive.
- **"Science has disproved religion."** Science addresses empirical claims. Where traditions make empirical claims (a young earth, a global flood), they can be and have been tested. Claims about purpose and value are not the kind of thing the method reaches.

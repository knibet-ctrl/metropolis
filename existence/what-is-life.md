# What Is Life?

> **Status: Tier 2 — contested but tractable.** There is no agreed scientific
> definition of life. This is not a minor gap. It is a live embarrassment in
> biology and it may be telling us something important.

---

## The standard definition, and why it fails

Textbooks usually list criteria: metabolism, growth, reproduction, response to
stimuli, homeostasis, evolution by natural selection.

Every one of them fails on some case.

| Criterion | Counterexample |
|---|---|
| Reproduction | Mules. Worker bees. Any sterile organism. Are they not alive? |
| Metabolism | Bacterial spores and tardigrades in cryptobiosis have no measurable metabolism for years, then resume |
| Growth | Crystals grow. Fires grow and consume fuel and spread |
| Response to stimuli | Thermostats respond. So do many chemical systems |
| Homeostasis | A candle flame maintains its own shape and temperature |
| Evolution | Computer viruses evolve. Some chemical systems undergo selection |

**NASA's working definition** — a self-sustaining chemical system capable of
Darwinian evolution — is the most widely used. It is a working tool, explicitly
provisional, and it also excludes mules.

## The case that breaks everything: viruses

A virus has genes, evolves rapidly, is subject to natural selection, and is
manifestly doing something. Outside a host it is an inert crystal-like particle
with no metabolism and no activity whatsoever.

Is it alive? **The honest answer is that biology has no consensus**, and the
question may not have a fact of the matter.

Then it gets worse:
- **Viroids** — bare loops of RNA, no protein coat, and they cause disease in plants.
- **Prions** — misfolded proteins with no genetic material at all, which propagate by inducing the same misfolding in other proteins. They replicate and spread. Nothing else about them resembles life.
- **Giant viruses** (Mimivirus, Pandoravirus) — larger than some bacteria, with genomes bigger than some cellular organisms, including genes for protein synthesis machinery previously thought to be exclusively cellular.

**The boundary is not fuzzy at the edges. There is no boundary.**

## The deep possibility: "life" may not be a natural kind

Here is the position worth sitting with.

We assume "alive" carves nature at a joint, the way "electron" or "gold" does.
It may not. It may be more like **"weed"** or **"vehicle"** — a real and useful
human category that does not correspond to a single underlying feature of the
world.

Evidence for this reading:
- Every attempted definition fails on cases we intuitively want to include or exclude.
- The failures are not random; they cluster at exactly the places you'd expect if there is a **continuum** from simple chemistry to complex chemistry with no threshold anywhere.
- We only have **one example**. All known life shares DNA, RNA, ribosomes, ATP, roughly the same genetic code, and the same chirality. **We are trying to define a category from a sample size of one.**

If we ever find a second, independent origin — on Mars, in Europa's ocean, or
as a "shadow biosphere" here on Earth — the definitional question changes
overnight. Until then, we are describing our relatives and calling it a
category.

## The best physical framing: dissipative structures

The most illuminating scientific angle is thermodynamic rather than
definitional.

**The apparent paradox:** the second law says entropy increases. Life is
strikingly ordered. Does life violate physics?

**No — and the resolution is the interesting part.** Living things are **open
systems**. They maintain internal order by increasing entropy in their
surroundings faster than they reduce it internally. Schrödinger's phrase in
*What Is Life?* (1944) was that organisms feed on "negative entropy."

**Ilya Prigogine's** work on dissipative structures generalises this: certain
systems far from thermodynamic equilibrium spontaneously self-organise, and
they persist precisely *because* they are efficient at dissipating energy
gradients.

A hurricane is one. A convection cell is one. A flame is one. **A cell is one.**

**Jeremy England's** "dissipation-driven adaptation" proposal (2013) extends
this: matter driven by an energy source may be statistically pushed toward
configurations that absorb and dissipate that energy well — which would make
something life-like *thermodynamically favoured* rather than a fluke.

*Status: interesting, influential, and not established. Tier 3.*

**The implication if this framing is right:** life is not a substance or a
threshold. It is **a process — a particular way that matter behaves when energy
flows through it.** You are not a thing. You are a pattern that persists by
routing energy, more like a whirlpool than a rock.

The whirlpool analogy is the single most useful image in this file. A whirlpool
has a stable, recognisable form. It has boundaries. It persists for a long time.
And there is no water in it that stays — the water is entirely replaced,
continuously. **The form persists; the substance flows through.** That is you.

*(Note the chemistry: essentially every atom in your body is replaced over
years. The commonly cited "you are entirely new every seven years" figure is a
myth — turnover rates vary enormously, from days for gut lining to a lifetime
for some neurons and eye lens proteins. The general point stands; the tidy
number does not.)*

## Autopoiesis — the best conceptual definition

**Maturana and Varela** (1970s) proposed that the defining feature of life is
**autopoiesis**: a system that continuously produces the components that
constitute it, including the boundary that separates it from its environment.

A cell makes the membrane that makes the cell possible. It is self-producing
and self-bounding.

**Why this is better than the checklist:** it identifies a *relational
structure* rather than a list of properties, and it explains why the boundary
matters. A virus is not autopoietic — it does not produce its own components;
it hijacks a system that does. On this account viruses are not alive, and the
reason given is principled rather than arbitrary.

**Why it's still not settled:** it may be too restrictive, and it struggles
with questions about whether an ecosystem, or the biosphere, qualifies.

## How did it start? — the origin of life

**Status: open, actively researched, genuine progress, no answer.**

What is reasonably established:
- Earth is roughly 4.5 billion years old; the earliest reasonably convincing evidence of life is around 3.5–3.8 billion years ago. **Life appeared relatively soon after the planet cooled enough to permit it** — which is suggestive, though from one data point.
- **Miller–Urey (1952)** and successors show amino acids form readily from simple chemistry under plausible early conditions. Amino acids and nucleobases have also been found in meteorites. **The building blocks are easy.**
- The building blocks are not the problem. **The problem is assembly, replication and enclosure.**

The main competing scenarios:

**RNA World.** RNA can both carry information and catalyse reactions
(ribozymes), which solves the chicken-and-egg problem of DNA needing proteins
and proteins needing DNA. Strong supporting evidence: the ribosome's catalytic
core is RNA. **Main difficulty:** getting RNA to form and self-replicate without
enzymes is chemically very hard.

**Metabolism-first.** Self-sustaining chemical reaction networks came first;
genetics came later. Associated with **alkaline hydrothermal vents** — Michael
Russell, Nick Lane — where natural proton gradients across mineral membranes
resemble the chemiosmotic gradients all living cells use for energy. **This is a
genuinely striking correspondence.**

**Lipid-first.** Compartments came first, because fatty acids spontaneously form
vesicles.

**Panspermia.** Life arrived from elsewhere. Note that this **does not answer
the question** — it relocates it.

**Honest summary: we know the ingredients are easy and common. We do not know
the recipe.** Nobody has produced life from non-life in a laboratory. It is not
clear whether the transition was near-inevitable or extraordinarily improbable,
and that single unknown is what makes estimates of life elsewhere in the
universe almost worthless.

## What this actually implies for you

Three things, and they are not consolations — they are just what follows.

**1. You are a process, not an object.** The whirlpool. This is not a metaphor
imported from mysticism; it is what the thermodynamics says. What persists is
an organised pattern, not a substance.

**2. The boundary between you and the world is functional, not fundamental.**
You are an open system in continuous material exchange with your environment —
air, water, food, heat, microbes. Roughly half the cells associated with your
body are not human. The skin is a permeable interface, not a wall.

**3. "Alive" is a spectrum, not a switch — and this matters for death.** If
there is no sharp line at the start, there is unlikely to be one at the end.
Medicine has already had to redefine death repeatedly (cardiac death, then
brain death, and now ongoing disputes about brainstem criteria) precisely
because the line keeps moving. See death.md.

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"Life is defined by reproduction, metabolism, growth..."** presented in schools as settled. It isn't, and every criterion has counterexamples.
- **"Life violates the second law."** No. Open systems export entropy. This is not controversial and is frequently misrepresented in both creationist and popular-science material.
- **"Scientists have created life in the lab."** Not yet. Craig Venter's team synthesised a genome and inserted it into an existing emptied cell — an enormous achievement, and it started with a living cell.
- **"Every atom in your body is replaced every seven years."** Myth. Turnover rates vary from days to decades to never.
- **"The odds of life arising by chance are astronomically small."** Nobody knows the odds. Any specific number you have seen quoted — for or against — is fabricated, because we cannot compute the probability of an event whose mechanism we do not know.

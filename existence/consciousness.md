# Consciousness

> **Status: Tier 4 approaching Tier 2.** The single most genuinely open problem
> in this notebook. We can map the correlates of consciousness in enormous
> detail and cannot explain why there is experience at all — or say clearly what
> an explanation would look like.

---

## The distinction that makes the problem visible

**David Chalmers (1995)** separated two things that had been run together.

**The "easy" problems** — easy only by comparison; they are the work of decades:
- How does the brain discriminate stimuli and react appropriately?
- How does it integrate information from different senses?
- How does it report on its own internal states?
- What distinguishes waking from dreamless sleep?
- How is attention focused and behaviour controlled?

These are **functional** questions. They ask what a system does. In principle,
enough neuroscience answers them, and enormous progress has been made.

**The hard problem:**

> Why is any of that accompanied by *experience*?

Why is there something it is like to see red, rather than the wavelength being
processed, categorised, reported and acted upon **in the dark**?

A system could in principle do everything a person does — discriminate, report,
avoid damage, say "that hurts" — with nothing it is like to be that system.
Such a being is a **philosophical zombie**. The fact that we can *coherently
describe* one, whether or not one could exist, is what shows the hard problem
is not just another easy problem in disguise.

**Thomas Nagel's** formulation (1974) is the cleanest: we can learn everything
about bat echolocation — the physics, the neurons, the behaviour — and still not
know **what it is like to be a bat**. There is a fact we cannot reach from the
outside.

**Frank Jackson's Mary's Room (1982):** Mary knows every physical fact about
colour vision but has lived her life in a black-and-white room. She leaves and
sees red for the first time. **Does she learn something new?** If yes, then not
all facts are physical facts. (The counterargument — that she gains an ability
or a new mode of access rather than a new fact — is the standard physicalist
reply and is not obviously wrong.)

## The major theories, steelmanned

### 1. Physicalism / functionalism
**Claim:** consciousness *is* a certain kind of information processing. Get the
functional organisation right and experience comes along necessarily.

**Strongest point:** everything else in biology yielded to physical
explanation. "Life force" seemed irreducible until biochemistry dissolved it.
Consciousness may be the last vitalism.

**Weakest point:** it does not address the hard problem so much as bet that the
problem will dissolve. No account has yet been given of *why* any particular
processing should feel like anything.

### 2. Illusionism (Dennett, Frankish)
**Claim:** the hard problem is generated by a faulty introspective model. There
are no qualia in the sense assumed. We have representations *of* having
ineffable inner experience, and those representations are what need explaining
— not the experience.

**Strongest point:** it takes seriously that introspection is a constructed
model and demonstrably unreliable, and it is the only view that explains why we
*think* there's a hard problem.

**Weakest point:** most people find it changes the subject. **The seeming is
itself the thing to be explained.** An illusion still has to be experienced by
someone.

### 3. Integrated Information Theory — IIT (Tononi)
**Claim:** consciousness *is* integrated information, quantified as **Φ (phi)**.
A system is conscious to the degree that it has cause-effect power on itself
that cannot be decomposed into independent parts.

**Strongest point:** it makes precise, quantitative, in-principle testable
predictions, and it correctly predicts why the **cerebellum** — with four times
more neurons than the cortex — contributes almost nothing to consciousness. Its
architecture is modular and feed-forward, so its Φ is low. That is a genuine
explanatory success.

**Weakest points:** Φ is computationally intractable for real brains. And the
theory implies that certain simple grid-like systems would be conscious — which
critics regard as a reductio. In 2023 a large group of researchers published a
letter calling IIT "pseudoscience," which was itself controversial; the dispute
is ongoing and heated.

### 4. Global Workspace Theory (Baars, Dehaene)
**Claim:** the brain has many parallel unconscious processors. Consciousness is
what happens when information is **broadcast globally** to the whole system,
making it available for report, memory, and flexible action.

**Strongest point:** excellent empirical support. It predicts and explains the
"ignition" — a sudden, widespread, late (~300ms) burst of frontoparietal
activity that reliably distinguishes stimuli people report seeing from
identical stimuli they don't.

**Weakest point:** it is a superb theory of *access* consciousness — what
information is available. It arguably says nothing about *phenomenal*
consciousness — why the broadcast is experienced.

### 5. Higher-Order theories
**Claim:** a mental state is conscious when there is another mental state
*about* it. Experience requires representation of one's own representing.

**Strongest point:** explains the connection between consciousness and
self-awareness, and links to prefrontal cortex findings.

**Weakest point:** does it merely relocate the problem? Why should a
second-order representation be any more experiential than a first-order one?

### 6. Panpsychism
**Claim:** experience is a fundamental feature of reality, like mass or charge.
Complex consciousness is built from simpler proto-experiential constituents.

**Strongest point:** it dissolves the emergence problem rather than solving it.
Getting experience from *no* experience looks impossible; getting complex
experience from simple experience is at least the right shape of problem.
Serious contemporary philosophers — Goff, Strawson, and Chalmers as a live
option — defend it. **It is not a fringe view any more, though it remains a
minority one.**

**Weakest point:** the **combination problem**. How do a trillion tiny
experiences combine into one unified experience? Nobody has an answer, and it
may be as hard as the problem it was invented to solve.

### 7. Mysterianism (Colin McGinn)
**Claim:** consciousness has a natural explanation and human cognition is
constitutionally incapable of grasping it — the way a dog cannot grasp
arithmetic.

**Strongest point:** we are evolved primates with cognitive limits, and there is
no reason to expect the universe to be comprehensible to us in every respect.

**Weakest point:** unfalsifiable, and functions as a stop sign on inquiry.

## What neuroscience has actually established

This is Tier 1 material and it is substantial. The correlates are not a small
achievement.

- **Specific damage abolishes specific experience.** Lesions to V4 abolish colour experience — including the ability to imagine or dream in colour. Damage to the fusiform face area produces prosopagnosia.
- **Anaesthesia** reliably abolishes experience while leaving many brain functions running. The best current account is that it disrupts long-range cortical *integration* — which supports both IIT and GWT.
- **The neural signature of report.** Conscious perception correlates with late, widespread, recurrent frontoparietal activity, not with early sensory processing. Identical stimuli that are and aren't consciously seen differ in exactly this.
- **Split-brain patients** (severed corpus callosum) can exhibit two apparently independent streams of processing, with the verbal left hemisphere confabulating explanations for actions initiated by the right. *(Note: recent work suggests split-brain unity is more preserved than the classic textbook account claims. The confabulation findings are robust; the "two selves" reading is contested.)*
- **Blindsight.** Patients with V1 damage report seeing nothing and yet perform above chance on visual tasks. **Processing without experience is real and demonstrable.**
- **Confabulation is pervasive.** In Michael Gazzaniga's framing, the left hemisphere runs an "interpreter" that generates plausible explanations for behaviour it did not cause, with complete confidence and no sense of making it up.

**The last point deserves emphasis, because it is the most personally relevant
finding in this file: your sense of knowing why you did something is
substantially manufactured after the fact.**

## The hardest fact: other minds

**You have direct evidence of exactly one consciousness.**

You infer everyone else's from behavioural and physical similarity. That
inference is overwhelmingly reasonable and it is not proof. There is no
conceivable test that detects consciousness from outside — every test measures
behaviour or physiology, and the entire hard problem is that those might occur
without experience.

**This is Tier 4 and it is not going to move.** It becomes practically urgent
for: patients in vegetative states, animals of uncertain neural complexity, and
AI systems that produce fluent reports of inner life.

The honest position: **we have no consciousness detector, we are unlikely to
get one, and we will nonetheless have to make consequential decisions as if we
did.**

---

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"Consciousness is in the prefrontal cortex / the claustrum / the pineal gland."** No single location. It appears to depend on large-scale integrated activity, most likely thalamocortical.
- **"We use 10% of our brains."** Complete myth.
- **"Quantum mechanics explains consciousness."** The Penrose–Hameroff orchestrated objective reduction proposal is a genuine hypothesis by serious people and is regarded by most physicists and neuroscientists as very unlikely — the brain is warm, wet and noisy, and quantum coherence should decohere far too fast. Most invocations of "quantum consciousness" outside this specific proposal are meaningless.
- **"Neuroscience has explained consciousness."** It has mapped correlates in extraordinary detail. Correlation is not explanation, and every serious researcher in the field says so.
- **"AI is/isn't conscious — obviously."** Both confident positions are unwarranted. We cannot detect consciousness from outside, and fluent self-report is exactly what we would expect from a system trained on human self-reports whether or not anything is happening. **The honest answer is that we don't know and currently have no method that could tell us.**

## What follows for you

**1. Your introspective reports are unreliable.** Confabulation, blindsight, and
change blindness all show the same thing: you have far less access to your own
processing than it feels like. **"I know my own mind" is the least
well-supported common belief in this notebook.**

**2. The certainty of an experience is not evidence about its cause.** This
applies to mystical experience, psychedelic insight, near-death experience and
religious conviction. The experience is real. Its *self-interpretation* is a
brain event, not a report from outside the brain.

**3. It is entirely reasonable to hold this open.** Consciousness is the one
place in this notebook where "nobody knows, including the experts" is the
literal state of the field. You are permitted to sit with that.

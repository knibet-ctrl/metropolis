# Adolescence — What Actually Happens

> **Status: TIER 1** on the physical and neural timeline. **TIER 1** that the

## The physical timeline

| Event | Timing | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Adrenarche | ~6-8 years | Adrenal androgens rise; the earliest hormonal marker, largely invisible |
| Gonadarche / puberty onset | Girls ~8-13, boys ~9-14 | Wide normal range |
| Menarche | Median ~12.5 in industrialised countries | Down from ~16-17 in mid-19th century Europe. Nutrition and body composition are the main drivers; endocrine disruptors are a live but unsettled hypothesis |
| Peak height velocity | Girls ~11.5-12, boys ~13.5-14 | Girls ahead by roughly two years |
| Grey matter volume peak | Girls ~11-12, boys ~12-14 | Giedd et al. (1999, *Nature Neuroscience*), longitudinal MRI |
| Synaptic pruning and myelination | Continue through the twenties | Regionally staggered; sensory areas mature first, association cortex last |
| Sleep phase delay | Onset in early puberty | Biologically driven melatonin shift of roughly 1-2 hours. School start times conflict with it. **TIER 1** |

**Secular trend note.** Puberty is arriving earlier while social adulthood
arrives later — the gap between physical maturity and adult roles is far wider
than in any previous human society. This is not a psychological finding but it
frames everything else in the file.

## The prefrontal cortex story, corrected

**The claim in circulation:** "The prefrontal cortex isn't fully developed until
25, which is why teenagers make bad decisions."

**What is true:** structural and functional maturation of the prefrontal cortex,
and of the white-matter tracts connecting it to subcortical regions, continues
well past the teenage years. This is well documented.

**What is wrong with the claim as stated:**

| Problem | Detail |
|---|---|
| **25 is not a threshold** | There is no discontinuity at 25 in any brain measure. The number appears to derive from the upper age bound of early longitudinal MRI samples — a sampling artefact converted into a fact |
| **"Fully developed" is not a defined state** | Cortical thinning, myelination and connectivity change continuously across the lifespan. There is no measured endpoint to reach |
| **Group averages, individual conclusions** | Somerville (2016) makes this point sharply: the distributions of brain measures for 17-year-olds and 25-year-olds overlap heavily. No scan classifies an individual as mature |
| **Structure does not license behavioural inference** | "Immature PFC → bad decisions" is a story laid over correlational data, not a demonstrated causal pathway |
| **It is used in both directions in law** | Cited to argue adolescents are less culpable (*Roper v. Simmons*, 2005, abolishing the juvenile death penalty in the US) and to argue for extending paternalistic control over young adults. The neuroscience does not adjudicate this; values do |

**The better-supported model** is Steinberg's and Casey's **dual systems /
maturational imbalance** account: the socioemotional and reward-processing
system rises sharply at puberty and peaks in mid-adolescence, while the
cognitive-control system matures gradually and later. The *gap* between the two
curves, not the absolute immaturity of either, characterises adolescence.
**TIER 2** — the behavioural pattern replicates cross-nationally (Steinberg's
11-country study), the neural interpretation is more contested.

## Risk-taking, and its logic

**The counterintuitive finding.** Adolescents are not ignorant of risk and are
not worse at estimating it. When assessed calmly and alone, their risk
judgements resemble adults'. Reyna & Farley's review found that adolescents
actually **deliberate more** than adults about risky choices, and over-weight
possible benefits — where adults use faster, gist-based avoidance.

**What changes the picture is context, specifically peers.** Gardner & Steinberg
(2005): in a simulated driving task, the presence of peers roughly **doubled**
risky behaviour in adolescents, produced a smaller increase in college-age
participants, and had **no effect on adults**. Replicated with fMRI showing
heightened reward-region activation under peer observation.

**Evolutionary framing — TIER 3, and flagged as such.** The standard account:
adolescence is the life stage for dispersal, mate-seeking and status
acquisition, in which risk-taking has fitness payoffs; sensation-seeking peaks
where the reproductive returns to boldness are highest; peer orientation is
adaptive because peers, not parents, are the future social environment. This is
plausible, coherent, cross-species-suggestive (adolescent risk-taking is
observed in other mammals), and largely unfalsifiable as usually stated. It is a
frame, not a finding.

## Moffitt's taxonomy — the best-supported thing in this file

Terrie Moffitt (1993), developed on the **Dunedin Multidisciplinary Health and
Development Study** (1,037 people born in 1972-73, followed for over 50 years
with retention above 90%).

| Type | Prevalence | Profile |
|---|---|---|
| **Adolescence-limited** | The majority of offenders | Antisocial behaviour begins in adolescence, is statistically normative, and desists in the early twenties. No prior neurodevelopmental or family risk. Often social mimicry of the other group |
| **Life-course-persistent** | Roughly 5-10% | Difficulties visible from early childhood: neurocognitive deficits, difficult temperament, adverse family environment. Accounts for a disproportionate share of serious and violent offending across the lifespan |

**Why this matters practically:** the two groups look identical at 16 and have
entirely different trajectories. Treating adolescence-limited offenders as
though they were life-course-persistent — through incarceration, exclusion, or
grouping them with high-risk peers — is one of the clearest ways to make
outcomes worse. See the deviancy-training results below and in
the-control-group.md.

**TIER 1-2.** The two-group structure replicates widely; the sharp dichotomy has
been softened by later work finding intermediate trajectories.

## Peer influence: selection versus socialisation

Adolescent friends resemble each other on delinquency, substance use, academic
engagement and much else. Two mechanisms, and disentangling them is the whole
game:

| Mechanism | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **Selection** | People choose similar friends. Longitudinal network studies consistently find selection accounts for a **large share** — in many analyses the majority — of observed friend similarity |
| **Socialisation** | Friends change each other. Real, and smaller than the raw correlations imply |

**Deviancy training — the strongest causal evidence, and it is a harm finding.**
Dishion, McCord & Poulin (1999) showed that aggregating high-risk adolescents
into groups produces **iatrogenic effects**: mutual reinforcement of rule-breaking
talk predicts later escalation. This is confirmed by the **Cambridge-Somerville
Youth Study** — Joan McCord's 30-year follow-up (1978) of a randomised
prevention programme begun in 1939 found that treated boys had **worse** adult
outcomes on alcoholism, serious mental illness, occupational status and
mortality. Summer-camp exposure with other treated boys was the strongest
predictor of harm.

**This is the single most important applied result about adolescence: putting
troubled teenagers together as an intervention makes things worse.** It is also
the design of most youth interventions.

## Identity formation

Erikson (1950): adolescence is the stage of **identity versus role confusion**.
Marcia (1966) operationalised it into four statuses on the axes of exploration
and commitment:

| | **No commitment** | **Commitment** |
|---|---|---|
| **No exploration** | Diffusion | Foreclosure |
| **Exploration** | Moratorium | Achievement |

**Assessment: TIER 3.** Useful vocabulary; weak measurement; little predictive
validity; the developmental sequence is largely stipulated rather than
demonstrated. It has survived because it is teachable and describes something
people recognise, which is not the same as being supported. Arnett's "emerging
adulthood" (2000) extends the same framework to 18-29 and inherits the same
evidentiary weakness — it is a good description of educated Westerners in the
late twentieth century and is often presented as a developmental stage.

## Is adolescent turmoil universal?

**G. Stanley Hall (1904)** coined *Sturm und Drang* — storm and stress — as a
biologically inevitable feature of adolescence.

**Margaret Mead's *Coming of Age in Samoa* (1928)** claimed the opposite: an
easy, conflict-free Samoan adolescence, therefore turmoil is cultural. It became
one of the most influential books in social science.

**Derek Freeman's *Margaret Mead and Samoa* (1983)** argued that Mead had been
deliberately misled by young informants, and that Samoan society was in fact
strongly concerned with virginity and prone to adolescent conflict.

**The honest position is that both were overstated.** Mead's fieldwork was short,
her Samoan was limited, and her conclusions outran her data. Freeman's rebuttal
was itself selective, polemical, and has been substantially criticised in turn
(Shankman and others). Neither book settles the question, and citing either as
established is a mistake. **TIER 2 for the underlying question.**

**What the evidence supports** (Arnett, 1999): storm and stress is **real in
tendency and not universal or inevitable**. Three components — conflict with
parents, mood disruption, and risk behaviour — do rise in adolescence on average,
with **large individual and cultural variation**. Offer's studies found roughly
80% of adolescents were not in significant turmoil. Cultures with clearer role
transitions, earlier economic participation and less peer segregation appear to
show less of it. **The intense, prolonged, peer-segregated adolescence of
industrialised societies is not the human default.**

## Social media and adolescent mental health — genuinely contested

**Do not read this section for a verdict. There isn't one.**

### The trend data (largely agreed)

| Indicator | Change |
|---|---|
| US high-school girls reporting persistent sadness/hopelessness (CDC YRBS) | ~36% (2011) → ~57% (2021)† |
| US adolescent major depressive episode (NSDUH) | Roughly doubled, ~2010-2019 |
| US suicide rate, ages 10-14 | Rose sharply from around 2007, from a low base |
| UK/Nordic self-harm hospital presentations, girls | Substantial rises over the same period |

The rises are real, concentrated in girls and younger adolescents, and roughly
synchronous across several Anglophone and Nordic countries from ~2010-2012.
**Contested:** how much is genuine incidence versus changed reporting,
help-seeking, diagnostic practice and awareness. Objective indicators like
suicide and hospitalisation are harder to explain away and are part of why the
alarm is taken seriously.

### The case that smartphones and social media caused it — Haidt, Twenge

- Timing: the inflection matches smartphone saturation and front-facing cameras
  (~2010-2012) across multiple countries.
- Sex asymmetry: worst for girls, consistent with appearance-based and
  relationally aggressive platforms.
- Displacement: less sleep, less in-person time, less unsupervised play.
- Quasi-experiments: **Braghieri, Levy & Makarin (2022)** exploited the staggered
  rollout of Facebook across US colleges (2004-2006) and found worsened mental
  health, around **0.085 SD**†. **Allcott et al. (2020)**: four-week paid Facebook
  deactivation RCT improved subjective wellbeing by roughly **0.09 SD**†.
- Collective-action framing: even a small individual effect can be large in
  aggregate if the harm is the changed social environment, not the personal dose.

### The case against — Odgers, Przybylski, Orben, Ferguson

- **Orben & Przybylski (2019, *Nature Human Behaviour*)**: specification-curve
  analysis across three large datasets (N ≈ 355,000) found digital technology use
  explained about **0.4% of variance** in adolescent wellbeing†, comparable to
  wearing glasses or eating potatoes. The choice of specification moved the
  result more than the data did.
- Cross-national trends are inconsistent; several countries with high smartphone
  penetration show no comparable rise.
- Direction of causation: distressed adolescents use more social media.
  Longitudinal within-person designs find weak and often bidirectional links.
- The named quasi-experiments have small effect sizes and short horizons, and the
  Facebook-rollout period bears little resemblance to current platforms.
- The moral-panic base rate is bad: comics, television, video games, Dungeons
  and Dragons, rock music. Every previous case was overstated.

### Where this actually stands

| Question | Status |
|---|---|
| Did adolescent distress rise sharply after ~2010 in several countries? | **Largely agreed** |
| Is social media use correlated with lower wellbeing? | **Yes, and the correlation is very small in large datasets** |
| Is the correlation causal, and in which direction? | **Open** |
| Would reducing use improve mental health at population scale? | **Open. Deactivation RCTs are short, small, and confounded by expectancy** |
| Do phone bans in schools help? | **Weak and mixed evidence.** Being tested at scale now |
| Is heavy use harmful for a subset — pre-existing vulnerability, appearance-focused content, night-time use? | **Most plausible live hypothesis, and the least studied** |

**My reading, marked as such: the aggregate correlational effect is genuinely
tiny, and the trend data are genuinely alarming, and both of those can be true if
the harm is concentrated, non-linear, or operates through the changed social
environment rather than individual dose. That is a hypothesis, not a finding.**
Anyone presenting this question as settled — in either direction — is ahead of
the evidence.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular story | Reality |
|---|---|
| "The teenage brain isn't done until 25" | No threshold at 25; the figure is a sampling artefact. Maturation is continuous and regionally staggered, and individual distributions overlap heavily |
| "Teenagers don't understand risk" | They estimate risk about as well as adults and deliberate more. The difference is contextual and peer-driven |
| "Peer pressure makes teenagers do things" | Selection accounts for much of friend similarity. But *peer presence* has a demonstrated causal effect on risk behaviour, which is a different mechanism from persuasion |
| "Adolescent rebellion is universal and biological" | Real in tendency, highly variable across individuals and cultures. Roughly 80% of adolescents are not in turmoil. Mead was overstated; so was Freeman |
| "Group programmes help troubled teens" | Aggregating high-risk adolescents reliably makes outcomes worse — Cambridge-Somerville, deviancy training, Scared Straight |
| "Social media is destroying a generation" / "there's no evidence at all" | Both overstate. The trends are real, the aggregate individual-level effect is very small, and causation is unresolved |
| "Adolescent offending predicts an adult criminal" | For the large majority it does not. Moffitt's adolescence-limited group desists |
| "Later school start times are a soft option" | The pubertal sleep phase delay is biological and well documented, and start-time trials show measurable benefits |

## Open questions

- [ ] What caused the post-2010 rise in adolescent distress? The strongest
      candidate list includes phones, changed childhood autonomy, academic
      pressure, and measurement change — and nobody has separated them.
- [ ] Is the dual-systems model a real neural account or a redescription of the
      behavioural data?
- [ ] Why is the rise so concentrated in girls, and why did it start in the same
      few years across several countries?
- [ ] Can peer influence be harnessed rather than only avoided? Paluck's
      network-based school interventions suggest yes; replication is thin.
- [ ] Does "emerging adulthood" describe a developmental stage or a cohort and
      class-specific social arrangement?
- [ ] How much of adolescent risk behaviour would disappear if adolescents had
      real economic and social roles, as they did in most societies and eras?

## The ACE study

Vincent Felitti and Robert Anda, Kaiser Permanente San Diego, 1995-97,
published 1998 (*American Journal of Preventive Medicine*). **N = 17,337**
adult HMO members completing a retrospective questionnaire on ten categories of
childhood adversity.

**The ten categories:** physical abuse, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, physical
neglect, emotional neglect, parental separation/divorce, witnessing domestic
violence against the mother, household substance abuse, household mental
illness, household member incarcerated.

**The headline finding — a dose-response gradient between ACE count and adult
health and behavioural outcomes:**

| Outcome | Approximate risk, ACE ≥ 4 vs ACE = 0 |
|---|---|
| Attempted suicide | ~12x† |
| Injected drug use | ~10x† |
| Alcoholism | ~7x† |
| Depression | ~4.6x† |
| Smoking | ~2.2x |
| Ischaemic heart disease, COPD, liver disease | Elevated, roughly 1.5-2.5x |
| Severe obesity, physical inactivity | ~1.4-1.6x |

Roughly **two-thirds** of the sample reported at least one ACE; about **12.5%**
reported four or more. Categories co-occurred strongly — adversities cluster.

**What this establishes: TIER 1.** Childhood adversity is common, clusters, and
is associated with substantially elevated population risk across a wide range of
adult outcomes, with a monotonic gradient. That is a real and important finding
and it changed medicine's attention to childhood.

## The limitations, which are serious

| Limitation | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Retrospective self-report** | The whole edifice rests on adults recalling childhood. **Baldwin et al. (2019, *JAMA Psychiatry*)** meta-analysed prospective versus retrospective measures of childhood maltreatment: agreement was **poor, κ ≈ 0.19**†. Prospective and retrospective measures identify **largely non-overlapping groups of people**. Danese and Widom (2020) found that psychopathology was associated with **retrospective** reports rather than prospective records — which is compatible with current distress shaping recall |
| **Sample** | Kaiser HMO members, mean age ~57, ~75-80% white, insured, mostly college-educated, mostly employed. Frequently described as if it were a general-population study |
| **Genetic and family confounding** | Parents transmit both adversity and genes. Sibling and twin designs consistently find maltreatment-outcome associations shrink, sometimes substantially, when family background is controlled |
| **The score is crude** | All ten items weighted equally. Chronic sexual abuse counts the same as parental divorce. Severity, chronicity, timing and subsequent support are all ignored |
| **Missing adversities** | Poverty, racism, bullying, community violence, bereavement, serious illness, war and migration are not in the original ten |
| **Reverse and common causation** | Household mental illness and substance abuse are both adversities and heritable risk factors |
| **Multiplicity and publication incentives** | The ACE literature is enormous, largely cross-sectional, largely positive, and has never been systematically assessed for publication bias in the way the psychology replication projects assessed theirs |

### The screening problem

**Anda, Porter & Brown (2020) — two of the original authors — explicitly warned
against using ACE scores to screen individuals**, on the grounds that
sensitivity and specificity are inadequate for individual-level decisions.

The warning came after the practice had spread. California's *ACEs Aware*
initiative funded ACE screening in primary care from 2019 at scale. This is a
live example of an evidence-to-policy failure of exactly the kind catalogued in
knowledge/institutions-of-knowledge.md: a real population finding converted into
an individual diagnostic instrument it cannot support, with implementation
running ahead of validation.

**The arithmetic makes the point.** Even for the most strongly associated
outcome, the great majority of people with ACE ≥ 4 do not have it. A 12-fold
relative risk on a low base rate is still a low absolute rate. **A high ACE score
is a marker of elevated population risk. It is not a prediction about a person,
and it should never be delivered to a person as one.**

## Resilience is the modal outcome

This is the finding that the popular literature systematically inverts.

**George Bonanno's trajectory work** on bereavement, disaster, injury and
terrorism exposure consistently identifies four trajectories:

| Trajectory | Typical share | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Resilience** | **~35-65%, usually the largest group** | Stable low distress throughout; brief disruption at most |
| **Recovery** | ~15-25% | Elevated distress that returns to baseline over months to a couple of years |
| **Chronic dysfunction** | ~5-30% depending on event severity | Sustained high distress |
| **Delayed onset** | ~0-15% | Distress rising over time; the least common and most disputed |

**PTSD after trauma exposure is a minority outcome.** Population estimates:
roughly 5-10% after most potentially traumatic events, substantially higher
after rape (~45%) and combat. Most people exposed to most traumatic events do
not develop a disorder.

**Werner & Smith's Kauai study** followed all 698 children born on Kauai in 1955
for over forty years. About a third were classified high-risk on perinatal
stress, poverty, family instability and parental psychopathology. **Roughly
one-third of that high-risk group developed into competent, confident, caring
adults with no significant problems** — and by the age-40 follow-up, most of the
remainder had also turned things around, often through later turning points.

**Ann Masten's "ordinary magic" (2001)** is the summarising insight: resilience
does not come from rare traits in exceptional individuals. It arises from
ordinary, common human adaptive systems — a functioning attachment relationship,
adequate cognitive and self-regulatory capacity, a community, a school, a
purpose. **Resilience is what happens when the basic protective systems are
intact.** Correspondingly, the greatest damage occurs when those systems
themselves are destroyed — which is precisely the Romanian institution case.

## What actually predicts resilience

| Factor | Evidence | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| **At least one stable, caring adult relationship** | The most consistently identified factor across the whole literature. Werner & Smith; Masten; the Harvard Center's summaries | 1-2 |
| **Self-regulation / executive function** | Predicts across many outcomes; partly heritable, partly trainable | 2 |
| **Cognitive ability** | Robust predictor, uncomfortable to say | 2 |
| **Effective schools and community institutions** | Consistent | 2 |
| **Material resources** | Poverty removal is the single most reliable adversity reduction. Cash-transfer trials show mental-health benefits | 1-2 |
| **Turning points in adulthood** | Sampson & Laub's reanalysis of the Glueck data: marriage, stable employment and military service predicted desistance from crime independently of childhood risk. **Adulthood is not the passive read-out of childhood** | 2 |
| **Sense of agency / self-efficacy** | Consistent in correlational work; hard to manipulate | 3 |
| **Meaning-making, religious or otherwise** | Correlational, plausible, confounded | 3 |

**Cessation matters more than composition.** Across the literature, the best
predictor of recovery is that the adversity **stopped** — that the environment
changed. This is a hopeful finding and an actionable one, and it is
under-emphasised relative to trait explanations of resilience.

## The biology — real mechanism, overstated evidence

**"Toxic stress"** (Shonkoff and colleagues) proposes that chronic activation of
the stress response in the absence of buffering relationships alters HPA-axis
regulation, immune function and brain development.

| Claim | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Chronic severe stress affects the developing HPA axis in animal models | **TIER 1** in rodents and non-human primates |
| Human cortisol profiles differ by adversity exposure | **TIER 3 as usually stated.** The human cortisol literature is heterogeneous, with hyper- and hypo-cortisolism both reported, small effects, and heavy analytic flexibility. It is one of the least reliable literatures in the notebook |
| Adversity shortens telomeres | **TIER 3.** Meta-analytic effects are small and show clear publication-bias signatures |
| Adversity reduces hippocampal volume | **TIER 2.** Real in severe maltreatment samples; small; direction of causation not fully established |
| Amygdala hyper-reactivity to threat cues after maltreatment | **TIER 2.** Reasonably replicated in maltreated samples; individually uninformative |
| "Trauma is stored in the body" / *The Body Keeps the Score* | **TIER 3-4 as a mechanistic claim.** The book is a clinically valuable and humane synthesis, and many of its specific neurobiological assertions run well ahead of the evidence. Treat it as a clinical framework, not a physiology text |

**Why the overstatement is not harmless.** "Your brain has been changed by what
happened to you" is delivered to patients as an explanation and heard as a
sentence. Given that the underlying biomarker literature is weak and the
resilience data are strong, the messaging is both epistemically unwarranted and
clinically counterproductive.

## Treatment: what works and what doesn't

| Intervention | Status |
|---|---|
| **Trauma-focused CBT** | Best-supported treatment for PTSD in children and adults. **TIER 1** |
| **EMDR** | Works — comparably to trauma-focused CBT. But dismantling studies (Davidson & Parker, 2001, and others) find the **eye movements contribute nothing**; the active ingredients are exposure and cognitive processing. The theory is wrong and the treatment works. **TIER 1-2** |
| **Prolonged exposure** | Well supported. **TIER 1** |
| **Psychological debriefing / CISD** | **Ineffective and possibly harmful.** Cochrane reviews (Rose et al., 2002) found single-session debriefing did not prevent PTSD and some trials showed worse outcomes. Still deployed after disasters. **TIER 1 that it does not work** |
| **"Trauma-informed care"** | A broad organisational orientation, not a specific treatment. Sensible in principle; the specific-outcome evidence is thin. **TIER 3** |
| **Cash transfers and poverty reduction** | Repeatedly show mental-health benefits in RCTs. The most direct route to reducing several ACE categories. **TIER 2** |
| **Parenting programmes for maltreatment risk** (e.g. Nurse-Family Partnership, Triple P, Incredible Years) | Mixed. NFP showed strong early US results; UK replication (the Building Blocks trial, 2015) found **no effect on the primary outcomes**. A textbook scaling/replication failure. **TIER 2** |

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular story | Reality |
|---|---|
| "A high ACE score means you will have these outcomes" | It means elevated population risk. The majority of people with ACE ≥ 4 do not have the outcomes. The original authors warned against individual screening |
| "ACEs are measured objectively" | Almost all ACE research is retrospective self-report, and prospective and retrospective measures identify **largely different people** (κ ≈ 0.19) |
| "Childhood trauma rewires the brain permanently" | The severe-deprivation evidence supports lasting effects. The general "rewiring" claim rests on a small, heterogeneous, publication-biased biomarker literature |
| "Most people who experience trauma develop PTSD" | Most do not. Resilience is the modal trajectory |
| "Resilience is a rare trait some people have" | Masten's "ordinary magic": it emerges from common adaptive systems. It is normal, not exceptional |
| "Debriefing survivors right after a disaster helps" | It doesn't, and may harm. One of the clearest negative findings in clinical psychology, still widely practised |
| "EMDR works because of the eye movements" | Dismantling studies say no. It works; the stated mechanism doesn't |
| "Childhood determines adulthood" | Sampson & Laub: adult turning points — marriage, work, service — change trajectories independently of childhood risk. Determinism is not supported and is actively harmful to communicate |

## What to say to someone with a difficult childhood

This is not a research finding; it is the responsible summary of the research
above, and the file is incomplete without it.

- Adversity raises risk in populations. **It does not determine your life.**
- Most people who have been through what you have been through do not have the
  outcomes in the risk tables.
- The strongest protective factor identified across sixty years of research is
  **one stable relationship** — which can be formed at any age, and does not have
  to be with a parent.
- The strongest predictor of recovery is that the adversity **stopped**.
- Adulthood contains real turning points, and they are documented, not
  motivational.
- Nothing about your brain has been read off a scan, because no scan can classify
  an individual.

## Open questions

- [ ] Given the κ ≈ 0.19 finding, what fraction of the ACE literature is
      measuring adversity and what fraction is measuring current distress
      reconstructing the past?
- [ ] How much of the ACE-outcome association survives genetically informative
      designs? Estimates vary widely and this is the field's biggest unresolved
      confound.
- [ ] What is the actual dose-response function? The step-linear score has no
      theoretical justification and severity is unweighted.
- [ ] Why did the Nurse-Family Partnership fail to replicate in the UK? Programme
      fidelity, counterfactual quality (a functioning health service), or the
      original estimate?
- [ ] Is there any human biomarker of childhood adversity robust enough to be
      individually informative? Currently: no.
- [ ] Does telling people about their ACE score help or harm? Astonishingly, this
      has barely been tested, despite mass screening being implemented.

# Attachment and the Early Years

> **Status: TIER 1** for the institutional deprivation findings — the strongest
> causal evidence in the notebook. **TIER 2** for attachment classification and
> its predictive power. **TIER 3** for most "critical period" claims outside
> vision and first-language acquisition.

> **Written from general knowledge with no live source verification.**

> **Care note.** Attachment theory has been used to tell mothers that returning
> to work damages their children, and to explain adopted and looked-after
> children's difficulties as relational failures on the part of carers. Neither
> use is supported. The evidence supports a narrow, strong claim about severe
> deprivation and a much weaker claim about ordinary variation in caregiving.

## Bowlby: the claim, and the part that was wrong

John Bowlby, *Maternal Care and Mental Health* (WHO, 1951), then the *Attachment
and Loss* trilogy (1969, 1973, 1980).

**The durable core.** Infants have an evolved behavioural system that maintains
proximity to a caregiver, activated by threat and deactivated by contact.
Caregiver availability builds "internal working models" of relationships. Human
infants require a sustained relationship with a small number of specific
caregivers, not merely food and hygiene. **TIER 1** for the behavioural system;
**TIER 2** for internal working models, which are hard to measure.

**The part that was wrong, and it did harm.** The 1951 "maternal deprivation"
thesis implied that any substantial separation from the *biological mother
specifically* caused lasting damage. It was mobilised, at exactly the moment
women were being moved out of postwar employment, to argue against maternal
work and nursery care.

**Michael Rutter, *Maternal Deprivation Reassessed* (1972)** did the correction
and it holds up:
- **Privation** (never having formed an attachment) is not the same as
  **deprivation** (losing one). Their consequences differ substantially.
- The attachment figure need not be the mother, need not be female, need not be
  biologically related, and need not be singular.
- Much of what Bowlby attributed to separation was attributable to the
  discord, disorganisation and poverty that accompanied it.
- Day care of adequate quality does not produce insecure attachment. The
  large NICHD Study of Early Child Care (from 1991, N~1,300) found child-care
  quality and quantity had modest effects; maternal sensitivity and family
  factors predicted more, and centre-based care in early years was associated
  with a small increase in later externalising behaviour — small, contested, and
  not an attachment effect.

## Ainsworth and the Strange Situation

Mary Ainsworth, Baltimore study and Uganda fieldwork; the Strange Situation
procedure (1969; codified in Ainsworth et al., *Patterns of Attachment*, 1978).
Twenty minutes, eight episodes, two separations and two reunions, infants aged
roughly 12-18 months. **The classification is based on reunion behaviour**, not
on distress at separation — a detail almost universally misreported.

| Classification | Typical low-risk Western prevalence | Reunion behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **B — Secure** | ~60-65% | Seeks contact, is comforted, returns to play |
| **A — Insecure-avoidant** | ~20% | Ignores or avoids the caregiver; physiological arousal is nevertheless elevated |
| **C — Insecure-resistant/ambivalent** | ~10-15% | Seeks contact but resists it; hard to settle |
| **D — Disorganised** (Main & Solomon, 1986/1990) | ~15% low-risk; up to ~80% in maltreatment samples | Contradictory, freezing, apprehensive behaviour. Added later; the strongest risk marker |

**How well does it predict?** Modestly. This is the key quantitative fact and it
is almost never stated in popular treatments.

| Meta-analysis | Finding | Effect size |
|---|---|---|
| Fearon et al. (2010) | Disorganisation → externalising behaviour | d ≈ 0.34 |
| Groh et al. (2012) | Attachment insecurity → internalising | d ≈ 0.15 |
| Groh et al. (2014) | Insecurity → social competence | small-to-moderate |
| Verhage et al. (2016) | Caregiver attachment state of mind → infant attachment ("transmission gap") | The parent's own attachment representation predicts the infant's, but **the observed caregiving behaviour explains only part of the link** — the mechanism is unresolved |

**d ≈ 0.15-0.35 is small to moderate.** It is a real signal in a population; it
is nowhere near sufficient to classify an individual child or to diagnose a
relationship. Long-term stability of classification is moderate at best and
drops sharply when major life events intervene.

**And a genuine surprise:** twin studies of infant attachment (Bokhorst et al.
2003; Roisman & Fraley 2008) find **essentially zero heritability** and
substantial **shared environment** — the reverse of nearly every other trait in
this notebook. Attachment security appears genuinely to be about the caregiving
context. **TIER 2**, on modest samples, but it is the best counterexample to
"everything is heritable" available.

**Cross-cultural distribution differences.** Van IJzendoorn & Kroonenberg's
(1988) meta-analysis of 32 samples in 8 countries found more avoidant
classification in northern Germany, more resistant in Japan and Israeli kibbutzim.
Two readings: real differences in caregiving, or the procedure means different
things in different rearing contexts (a Japanese infant rarely left alone finds
the separation genuinely novel). Unresolved. **TIER 2** — and it is a warning
about exporting the instrument.

## Institutional deprivation: the clearest causal evidence in the notebook

This is where the field's strongest claim lives, and it comes from two studies.

### The English and Romanian Adoptees (ERA) study — Michael Rutter, from 1992

**Design.** 165 children adopted into UK families from Romanian institutions
after 1989, compared with 52 non-deprived UK adoptees. Grouped by age at
adoption: under 6 months, 6-24 months, over 24 months. Followed to ages 6, 11,
15 and — in the *Lancet* report by Sonuga-Barke et al. (2017) — **ages 22-25**.

**Findings:**

| Group | Outcome |
|---|---|
| Adopted **before ~6 months** | Largely indistinguishable from UK adoptees on cognitive and emotional measures. **Substantial recovery** |
| Adopted **after ~6 months** | Persistent "deprivation-specific patterns" into young adulthood: **disinhibited social engagement, quasi-autistic features, inattention/overactivity, and cognitive impairment**. Reduced head circumference at entry, partly persisting |
| Young adulthood (22-25) | The late-adopted group had markedly elevated rates of emotional problems, low educational attainment and unemployment. Some difficulties that had appeared to remit in middle childhood **re-emerged in adolescence and adulthood** |

**The two headline conclusions.** First, **severe global deprivation causes
lasting harm.** Second, **duration and timing matter, with a threshold in the
region of six months of exposure.** These are the strongest causal statements in
the notebook.

### The Bucharest Early Intervention Project (BEIP) — Nelson, Zeanah, Fox, from 2000

**Design.** A **randomised controlled trial**: 136 institutionalised Romanian
children randomised to high-quality foster care or care-as-usual, with a
never-institutionalised comparison group. Randomisation makes this exceptionally
strong evidence — and exceptionally ethically fraught, which the investigators
addressed at length and which remains debated.

**Findings:**

| Domain | Result |
|---|---|
| IQ | Foster-care group substantially higher (roughly 8-10 points by 54 months); **earlier placement, better outcome** |
| Language | Sensitive period effects; children placed before roughly 15-24 months did substantially better |
| Attachment security | Sharply improved with foster placement; institutionalised children showed very high rates of disorganised and unclassifiable attachment |
| Internalising problems | Reduced, particularly in girls |
| ADHD / externalising | **Largely unimproved by foster care** — an important negative finding |
| EEG / brain activity | Reduced cortical activity in institutionalised children; normalisation greater with earlier placement |
| Growth | Catch-up growth following placement |

**What both studies establish jointly: TIER 1.** Severe deprivation in infancy
produces lasting cognitive, social and neural effects; removal produces
substantial but incomplete recovery; earlier removal is better; and the recovery
is domain-specific, with attachment and IQ responding better than attention and
disinhibited social behaviour.

**What they do NOT establish.** Nothing about ordinary variation in family life.
Romanian institutions of that era involved ratios of one caregiver to 10-20
infants, no individual attention, minimal stimulation, malnutrition and
infection. **Generalising from this to whether a parent works, uses nursery, or
sleep-trains is a category error of enormous magnitude, and it is made
constantly.**

## Critical periods and sensitive periods

A **critical period** is a window outside which a capacity cannot be acquired.
A **sensitive period** is a window in which acquisition is easier. Almost
everything in development is at most the latter.

| Domain | Status | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Binocular vision / ocular dominance** | **Genuine critical period. TIER 1** | Hubel & Wiesel's monocular deprivation work in cats and monkeys (1960s; Nobel 1981). Human congenital cataract: later surgery, worse acuity |
| **Visual recovery more broadly** | **Weaker than assumed. TIER 2** | Project Prakash (Pawan Sinha) treated congenitally blind children and adolescents in India; substantial functional visual learning occurred well beyond the supposed window. The window is softer than the textbook version |
| **First-language acquisition** | **Sensitive period, strongly supported. TIER 1** | Deaf individuals exposed to sign language late show permanent deficits in morphosyntax proportional to age of first exposure (Newport; Mayberry). The Genie case (1970) is consistent but is a single, confounded, ethically appalling case and should not be load-bearing |
| **Second-language phonology** | Sensitive period. **TIER 1-2** | Native-like accent requires early start, roughly before 10-12 |
| **Second-language grammar** | **Later than believed. TIER 2** | Hartshorne, Tenenbaum & Pinker (2018), N≈670,000: grammar-learning ability stays high until roughly 17-18 then declines. Widely misread as confirming an early critical period; it does the opposite |
| **Attachment** | Sensitive period, roughly the first two years | ERA and BEIP. **TIER 1-2** |
| **Absolute pitch** | Sensitive period, contested | Requires early training plus probable genetic predisposition |
| **Everything else — maths, music, sport, social skills, "learning"** | **No demonstrated critical period. TIER 1 for the negative claim** | The burden of proof has never been discharged |

### The "first three years" overreach

The 1997 White House Conference on Early Childhood Development and the *I Am
Your Child* campaign promoted the claim that experience in the first three years
sets the brain's capacities more or less permanently, on the basis of
synaptogenesis and pruning curves, enriched-environment rodent studies, and the
critical period for vision.

**John Bruer's *The Myth of the First Three Years* (1999)** dismantled this, and
the dismantling holds:
- Synaptic density peaks and prunes on very different schedules in different
  cortical regions, and continues into adolescence. It does not map onto learning
  capacity.
- "Enriched environment" in rodent studies means *a normal-ish cage with objects
  and other rats*, compared against solitary confinement in a bare box. It is a
  deprivation study relabelled as an enrichment study. **This substitution is the
  hinge of the whole popular argument and it is a straightforward error.**
- Adult neuroplasticity is substantial. The brain does not close for business.

**What survives.** The first years matter for the reasons anyone would expect —
nutrition, safety, language exposure, a stable caregiver, absence of severe
deprivation — not because a window slams shut. **A four-year-old with a
difficult start is not a finished product, and the evidence base contains no
support for saying otherwise.**

## Attachment theory is not "attachment parenting"

| Attachment theory (Bowlby, Ainsworth, Rutter) | Attachment parenting (Sears, *The Baby Book*, 1992) |
|---|---|
| A research programme about the infant's relational system | A commercial parenting brand with a prescribed practice list |
| Predicts that responsive, available caregiving supports security | Prescribes babywearing, bed-sharing, extended breastfeeding, constant proximity |
| Says the caregiver need not be the mother, need not be constant, need not be biological | In practice places nearly all demands on the mother |
| Modest effect sizes, honestly reported | Strong claims of long-term consequence |
| Does not claim that separation, nursery or formula produce insecurity | Frequently implies exactly that |

**No component of the attachment-parenting practice list has been shown to
produce secure attachment.** Security tracks *responsiveness to signals* — the
caregiver reads the infant accurately and responds — which is compatible with
many arrangements and many household structures. And the specific practice of
bed-sharing carries real, quantified SIDS risk in the presence of soft bedding,
sofas, parental smoking, alcohol or drugs. See culture-and-context.md, where the
anthropological fact and the safety evidence are kept separate.

**The distinction matters because attachment parenting generates guilt with the
authority of a scientific theory it is not.**

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular story | Reality |
|---|---|
| "The first three years determine everything" | Bruer (1999). The synaptic pruning argument does not support it and the recovery data contradict it |
| "Nursery causes insecure attachment" | NICHD study: no. Family factors predict more than childcare factors |
| "The Strange Situation measures how much the baby loves the mother" | It classifies reunion behaviour into a four-way scheme with moderate reliability and small-to-moderate predictive validity |
| "Insecure attachment predicts a damaged adult" | d ≈ 0.15-0.35. Population signal, not individual prognosis |
| "Romanian orphanage findings show early experience is destiny" | They show *severe global deprivation* has lasting effects and that *earlier rescue works*. Children adopted before six months largely recovered — which is a finding about plasticity, not fixity |
| "Attachment is heritable temperament" | Twin studies find near-zero heritability and substantial shared environment for infant attachment |
| "Attachment parenting is what attachment theory recommends" | It is a commercial movement with no demonstrated effect on attachment security |
| "Bowlby proved mothers shouldn't work" | Bowlby's 1951 framing was over-extended and Rutter corrected it in 1972. The correction is 50 years old and still hasn't reached the popular literature |

## Open questions

- [ ] What explains the "transmission gap" — why the parent's attachment
      representation predicts the infant's classification better than observed
      caregiving behaviour does?
- [ ] Is disorganised attachment a coherent category or a residual bin for
      unclassifiable behaviour? Serious researchers hold both views.
- [ ] Do cross-cultural distribution differences reflect real caregiving
      differences or instrument non-equivalence? Unresolved since 1988.
- [ ] Why do attention and disinhibited social engagement recover so much less
      than IQ and attachment after institutional deprivation? A domain-specific
      mechanism is implied and not identified.
- [ ] Where exactly is the timing threshold for deprivation effects, and does it
      differ by domain? "About six months" is doing a lot of work on modest N.
- [ ] Can attachment-based interventions (e.g. Video-feedback Intervention,
      Attachment and Biobehavioral Catch-up) show durable outcome effects rather
      than short-term classification shifts? Promising, not established.

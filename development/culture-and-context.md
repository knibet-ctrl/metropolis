## The sampling problem

**Henrich, Heine & Norenzayan (2010), *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, "The
weirdest people in the world?"** WEIRD = Western, Educated, Industrialised,
Rich, Democratic.

| Finding | Figure |
|---|---|
| Share of samples in top psychology journals from WEIRD countries | ~96%† |
| Share of world population those countries represent | ~12% |
| Share of samples from the United States alone | ~68%† |
| Share of American samples that were undergraduates | ~67-80%† |

Arnett (2008) found the same pattern independently. **Nielsen et al. (2017)
found developmental psychology to be, if anything, worse** — and developmental
psychology is the field whose findings are most routinely described as
"universal stages of human development."

**Why this is not merely a generalisability caveat.** Henrich et al.'s point is
stronger: on several measured dimensions — visual illusions, fairness in
economic games, spatial reasoning, analytic versus holistic cognition, moral
reasoning, self-concept — **WEIRD populations are not merely unrepresentative
but outliers**, often at the extreme of the observed human range. A universal
inferred from an outlier is worse than a universal inferred from a random
sample.

## Childrearing arrangements vary enormously

| Dimension | Western industrial norm | Documented range |
|---|---|---|
| **Who cares for the infant** | Mother, near-exclusively, in the early months | **Alloparenting is the human norm.** Efe (Ituri forest) infants are handled by many caregivers per hour and nursed by multiple women. Aka fathers hold infants a higher proportion of the time than in any other recorded society. Hrdy's *Mothers and Others* (2009) argues cooperative breeding is a species characteristic |
| **Sleep** | Infant alone, in a separate room, through the night | Solitary infant sleep is **historically and cross-culturally rare**. Co-sleeping is the majority human arrangement (see the safety note below) |
| **Carrying** | Pram, cot, floor | Continuous body contact for the first year or more in many foraging and horticultural societies |
| **Talking to babies** | High-volume child-directed speech from adults | Kaluli (Papua New Guinea) and Samoan caregivers described by Ochs & Schieffelin as **not addressing prelinguistic infants as conversational partners**. Children acquire language on normal timetables |
| **Work and chores** | Children economically useless, protected from labour | Lancy's "chore curriculum": in most documented societies children contribute real labour from ~4-6. **Zelizer (*Pricing the Priceless Child*, 1985) dated the "economically useless, emotionally priceless" child to roughly 1870-1930 in the US** |
| **Autonomy** | Adult-supervised, structured, scheduled | Autonomous mixed-age play groups with minimal supervision are the historical norm |
| **Praise** | Frequent, explicit, individual | Rare or actively avoided in several societies where it is considered dangerous or shaming |
| **Obedience vs self-reliance** | Contested Western emphasis on negotiation and explanation | Keller's German/Nso comparison: Nso (Cameroon) mothers use proximal, body-oriented parenting emphasising hierarchy and interdependence; German middle-class mothers use distal, face-to-face, object-focused parenting emphasising autonomy. **Both produce competent adults in their own contexts** |

**Safety note, kept deliberately separate from the anthropology.** The
observation that co-sleeping is the human norm is not a recommendation. Bed-sharing
carries substantially elevated SIDS risk in the presence of soft bedding, sofas
or armchairs, parental smoking, alcohol or drugs, or an infant under three months
or preterm. The evidence-based advice — room-sharing without bed-sharing, supine
position, firm flat surface — is well supported. **Descriptive anthropology and
risk guidance answer different questions and should never be blended.**

## The word gap, and why it is a good case study

**The claim.** Hart & Risley (1995): children of professional families hear
roughly **30 million more words** by age 3 than children of families on welfare,
and this gap predicts later vocabulary and school outcomes. It became one of the
most influential findings in education policy — the basis for national
initiatives, city programmes and a great deal of parental instruction.

**The problems:**

| Problem | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Sample size** | **N = 42 families**, 13 professional, 23 working-class, 6 on welfare. Six families are carrying the low end of the most-cited gap in developmental science |
| **Extrapolation** | Hourly recordings extrapolated to 14 waking hours a day, 365 days a year, for four years |
| **Who counts as a speaker** | Only speech from the primary caregiver to the target child was counted |
| **Replication** | **Sperry, Sperry & Miller (2019, *Child Development*)** recorded five communities and found that when **all** speakers in the environment were counted, some low-income children heard as many or more words than Hart & Risley's professional families. Golinkoff, Hoff, Rowe, Tamis-LeMonda & Hirsh-Pasek replied, defending a smaller version of the gap. **Neither side denies that a gap exists; the size, and whether it is a class deficit or a difference in interactional style, are disputed** |
| **Better predictor available** | Romeo et al. (2018) found **conversational turns** — back-and-forth exchanges — predicted language outcomes and left-hemisphere language-region activation better than raw word counts, independently of SES. Interaction beats volume |
| **Deficit framing** | The finding was operationalised as "poor parents talk too little", which is both a moral claim about parents and a poor summary of the data |

**What survives: TIER 2.** Language input matters for language development.
Contingent, responsive back-and-forth matters more than word count. The specific
30-million figure should not be cited.

**And the cross-cultural check:** Kaluli and Samoan children acquire language on
normal timetables without Western-style child-directed speech, so CDS cannot be
*necessary*. But Shneidman & Goldin-Meadow (2012) found that among Yucatec Maya
children, *directed* speech specifically predicted vocabulary. The reconciliation
is probably that children need sufficient accessible input and that societies
supply it in different ways, with a floor rather than a linear dose-response.
**TIER 2.**

## Intensive parenting is recent, class-specific and rising

| Evidence | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Hays (1996)** | Named "intensive mothering": child-centred, expert-guided, emotionally absorbing, labour-intensive, financially expensive. Identified it as an ideology, not a description of what children need |
| **Lareau (2003)** | US class contrast: middle-class "concerted cultivation" (scheduled activities, reasoning, institutional advocacy) versus working-class "accomplishment of natural growth" (autonomy, kin networks, less structured time). Both produce competent children; the former transmits institutional fluency, which schools reward |
| **Time-use data** (Sayer, Bianchi & Robinson 2004; Dotti Sani & Treas 2016, 11 countries) | **Parental time with children has risen substantially since the 1960s**, in most countries, including for mothers in paid employment. The intuition that parents used to spend more time with children is backwards |
| **Ramey & Ramey (2010), "The Rug Rat Race"** | College-educated US parents' childcare time rose sharply from around 1995, concentrated on older children, timed to intensifying college admissions competition. **The mechanism is positional competition, not new knowledge about child development** |

**The implication.** A great deal of modern parenting effort is a response to
scarcity of positional goods, not to evidence about children's needs. It is
rational at the individual level and largely zero-sum at the population level.
This deserves stating plainly because it is otherwise experienced as personal
inadequacy.

## What appears genuinely universal

| Phenomenon | Confidence | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Language acquisition sequence** | **TIER 1** | Cooing → canonical babbling ~6-10 months → first words ~10-14 months → two-word combinations ~18-24 → morphological overregularisation. Sequence is robust; timing varies |
| **Attachment behaviours** | **TIER 1** for the behaviours; **TIER 2** for classification distributions | Proximity-seeking, separation protest, secure-base use appear everywhere. The Strange Situation category proportions differ, and may reflect the instrument as much as the child |
| **Motor milestone sequence** | **TIER 1** for order, **TIER 2** for timing | The WHO Multicentre Growth Reference Study (2006, six countries) found broadly overlapping windows. But Kipsigis and Jamaican infants sit and walk earlier where caregivers deliberately practise those skills (Super, 1976). **Timing is trainable; sequence largely isn't** |
| **Stranger wariness onset ~6-9 months** | **TIER 2** | Universal in appearance; intensity varies with how many caregivers the infant routinely encounters |
| **Basic emotion expression in infancy** | **TIER 2** | The strong universality claim for adult facial expressions is contested (Barrett and others). Infant distress and comfort-seeking are less controversial |
| **Play, including pretend play** | **TIER 1** for occurrence, **TIER 2** for form | Universal; adult participation in children's play is highly culture-specific and rare outside WEIRD societies |
| **Sensitivity to fairness and reciprocity in mid-childhood** | **TIER 2** | Present everywhere; thresholds and content vary markedly (Blake et al., seven societies) |
| **Puberty and its endocrine sequence** | **TIER 1** | Timing shifts with nutrition |

## What is presented as universal and is not

| Presented as universal | Actually |
|---|---|
| Solitary infant sleep, sleeping through the night | Recent, Western, and not a developmental milestone |
| The "terrible twos" | A Western folk category. Toddler assertiveness is general; framing it as a discrete stage is not |
| Adolescent rebellion against parents | Real in tendency, much weaker where adolescents have economic roles. See adolescence.md |
| Adults playing with children as a caregiving duty | Rare outside WEIRD societies. Children play with other children |
| Praise as a motivational tool | Culture-specific and sometimes actively avoided |
| Extended childhood free of work | ~150 years old in the West |
| Exclusive maternal care in the first year | Contradicted by the alloparenting record and by the cooperative-breeding account of human evolution |
| Developmental "stages" as discrete steps | Piaget's stages are a useful description with weak evidence for discreteness; the ages are variable and several tasks were later shown to underestimate infants badly |

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular story | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Developmental psychology describes human development" | It largely describes development in an outlier population — ~96% WEIRD samples for ~12% of humanity, and developmental psychology is worse than the field average |
| "Children need constant one-to-one adult attention" | Alloparenting and mixed-age peer groups are the human norm. Anxiety about this is a WEIRD artefact |
| "Poor parents don't talk to their children" | The word-gap literature is 42 families, extrapolated, counting only the primary caregiver, and contested by direct replication. Conversational turns matter more than word counts |
| "Parents today spend less time with their children" | Time-use data show the opposite. Parental childcare time has risen substantially since the 1960s |
| "Intensive parenting is what the science recommends" | It is a class-specific ideology whose modern intensification tracks admissions competition, not developmental research |
| "Co-sleeping is natural, therefore safe" | It is the human norm; it also carries measurable risk under specific identifiable conditions. Both are true and neither settles the other |
| "Piaget mapped the universal stages" | The sequence is broadly robust; the discreteness of stages is not, and several classic tasks understated infant competence badly |
| "Attachment classifications are universal" | The behaviours may be; the classification distributions differ across cultures, unresolved since van IJzendoorn & Kroonenberg (1988) |

## Open questions

- [ ] How much of the "universal" developmental canon would survive replication
      in non-WEIRD samples? Almost nobody has tried at scale.
- [ ] Is the Strange Situation measuring the same construct in Japan, Germany and
      the US? Thirty-five years unresolved.
- [ ] What is the actual shape of the language-input function — a floor, a linear
      dose-response, or a threshold? Policy assumes linear; the cross-cultural
      evidence suggests a floor.
- [ ] Does concerted cultivation cause institutional advantage or mark
      pre-existing advantage? Lareau's design is ethnographic and cannot separate
      them.
- [ ] What are the costs of the intensive-parenting norm — to parental mental
      health, to fertility, to children's autonomy — and has anyone measured them
      against its benefits?
- [ ] Are alloparented children different in any measurable respect from
      exclusively-mother-reared children, holding resources constant? Remarkably
      under-studied given the theoretical stakes.

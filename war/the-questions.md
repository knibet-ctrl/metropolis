# The Questions

> **Status: structural file.** No single tier — the whole point is that these questions sit at *different* tiers and are routinely fused into one bad question.
> **Provenance: written from general knowledge without live source verification.**

Almost every unproductive argument about war is a category error. Someone cites chimpanzee raiding to explain the invasion of Iraq. Someone cites the 1914 July Crisis to explain why humans have an aggression instinct. Someone cites falling homicide rates to argue that great-power war is obsolete. These are five different questions with five different evidence bases, five different failure modes, and five different confidence levels.

---

## The five questions

| # | Question | Unit of analysis | Evidence base | Tier | Where it goes wrong |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Q1** | Why are humans *capable* of lethal violence? | Individual / species | Evolutionary biology, primatology, developmental psych, neuroscience | Tier 2 | Capacity is confused with propensity, and propensity with inevitability |
| **Q2** | Why does *organised* war exist as an institution? | Society | Archaeology, anthropology, historical sociology | Tier 2 | Tiny non-random samples; state-contact contamination |
| **Q3** | Why did *this particular* war start? | Dyad / crisis | Diplomatic history, archives, formal bargaining theory | Tier 2 | Hindsight; overdetermination; the winners write the documents |
| **Q4** | What decides who *wins*? | Campaign / war | Military history, operations research, quantitative IR | Tier 1–2 | After-action narratives systematically launder luck into skill |
| **Q5** | Is violence *declining*? | World-system over time | Long-run datasets, historical demography, statistics | Tier 2 | Fat tails; denominator choice; pre-1800 data are largely guesses |

**The single most common error:** using evidence from a lower-numbered question to settle a higher-numbered one. Chimpanzee coalitional killing (Q1) is silent on the July Crisis (Q3). Fearon's bargaining model (Q3) is silent on whether war is declining (Q5).

## Q1 — Why are humans capable of violence?

**What is being asked:** what in human psychology and biology makes lethal aggression possible at all?

| Sub-question | Status |
|---|---|
| Do humans have a general "aggression instinct" that builds up and must be discharged? | **Rejected.** The hydraulic/catharsis model (Lorenz, Freud's Thanatos) has essentially no empirical support. Catharsis experiments generally find venting *increases* subsequent aggression. |
| Is reactive aggression (hot, defensive) neurally distinct from proactive/instrumental aggression (cold, planned)? | Reasonably well supported. Wrangham's distinction between reactive and proactive aggression; humans appear unusually *low* in reactive and unusually *high* in proactive aggression relative to other primates. |
| Is there a heritable component to individual violent behaviour? | Yes, modest and heavily gene-environment interactive. The MAOA literature (Caspi et al. 2002) is the famous case and has had a rocky replication history — treat with caution. |
| Does capacity imply that war is natural or inevitable? | **No.** This is the central non-sequitur. Humans are also capable of extraordinary restraint, and most humans in most societies never kill anyone. |

**Caveat:** Q1 is the question most vulnerable to motivated reasoning in both directions — "human nature" as an alibi for war, or the denial of biology as a guarantee against it.

## Q2 — Why does organised war exist?

**What is being asked:** how did coordinated, planned, group-versus-group killing become a durable social institution?

The candidate answers are not mutually exclusive:

| Account | Core claim | Best evidence | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deep-ancestral | Coalitional violence predates agriculture; war is an elaboration of it | Jebel Sahaba, Nataruk, chimpanzee intergroup killing | Sites are excavated *because* they contain violence; sample is not random |
| Agricultural/storage | War requires storable surplus, fixed territory, and something worth taking | Sharp rise in fortification and mass graves in the Neolithic | Absence of evidence in mobile populations may be taphonomic |
| State-formation | Organised war and the state co-produce each other; Tilly's "war made the state and the state made war" | Early-modern European fiscal-military development | Explains state war well, tribal war poorly |
| Tribal-zone | Much recorded "primitive war" is a product of contact with expanding states | Ferguson's work on ethnographic contact effects | Cannot explain pre-contact archaeological cases |

Detailed in [origins-of-war.md](origins-of-war.md). Cross-reference [../existence/society.md](../existence/society.md) on conditional cooperation — the same in-group machinery that makes large-scale cooperation possible is what makes large-scale organised violence possible.

## Q3 — Why did *this* war start?

**What is being asked:** the causal account of a specific conflict onset.

This is where the bargaining literature lives ([why-wars-start.md](why-wars-start.md)). The essential move is Fearon's: war is *ex post inefficient*, so the interesting question is not "what did they want" but "why couldn't they get it without fighting?"

| Layer | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Structural | What made war possible? | Shifting power, alliance systems, offense-defense balance |
| Dispositional | What made these actors war-prone? | Regime type, ideology, domestic coalitions |
| Proximate | What made this crisis fail? | Information failure, commitment problem, misperception |
| Trigger | What was the immediate spark? | Assassination, incident, ultimatum expiry |

**The failure mode is level-slippage:** treating a trigger as a cause, or a structural condition as an explanation for timing. Erik Gartzke's point ("War Is in the Error Term", 1999) is sharp here: if wars result from *private information*, then their timing is in principle unpredictable from public variables — which is exactly what the forecasting record shows.

## Q4 — What decides who wins?

**What is being asked:** conditional on a war happening, what predicts the outcome?

This is the most tractable question of the five, and the one where popular understanding is furthest from the scholarly picture. The scholarly picture is boring: **supply, industry, manpower depth, disease control, and force employment doctrine.** The popular picture is about generals, morale and decisive battles.

| Popular emphasis | Scholarly weight |
|---|---|
| Genius commanders | Real but heavily overstated; survivorship bias in the biography canon |
| Decisive battles | Most wars are decided by attrition and exhaustion, not a single day |
| Morale and will | Matters, but "will" is usually downstream of supply and casualty rates |
| Technology | Matters much less than *how forces are employed* (Biddle's "modern system") |
| Logistics | Dominant, and boring, and therefore under-narrated |

Detailed in [what-decides-wars.md](what-decides-wars.md) and, for the exceptions, [asymmetric-conflict.md](asymmetric-conflict.md).

## Q5 — Is violence declining?

**What is being asked:** is there a real downward trend in the rate of violent death, or are we in a lull inside a fat-tailed process?

| Component of the claim | Evidence quality |
|---|---|
| European homicide decline since ~1300 | **Strong.** Eisner's series; ~30–100 per 100k down to ~1 per 100k |
| Abolition of judicial torture, slavery, duelling, most capital punishment | **Strong.** Institutional record is unambiguous |
| Decline in interstate war *frequency* since 1945 | **Moderate.** Depends on threshold and on how internationalised civil wars are coded |
| Decline in war *severity* / the "Long Peace" as a real regime change | **Weak.** Statistically underpowered; see Clauset (2018) |
| Pre-state violence rates as a baseline | **Very weak.** Sample not random |

Detailed in [is-violence-declining.md](is-violence-declining.md). Marked Tier 2 and deliberately not resolved.

## Questions that are malformed (Tier 4)

| Malformed question | Why | Better version |
|---|---|---|
| "Is war part of human nature?" | "Human nature" is doing no work; conflates capacity, propensity and inevitability | "What is the distribution of intergroup lethal violence across societies, and what predicts position in that distribution?" |
| "Was the war inevitable?" | Inevitability is not observable; every counterfactual is unfalsifiable | "What was the probability of war given the state of the system in month *t*, and which interventions would have moved it most?" |
| "Who was really to blame?" | Conflates causal, legal and moral responsibility, which come apart | Ask each separately, with each one's own evidentiary standard |
| "Do nuclear weapons keep the peace?" | Sample size of one, no control | "What is the counterfactual model, and what observable would distinguish deterrence from luck?" |
| "Is war ever justified?" | Not empirical; belongs to [../existence/ethics.md](../existence/ethics.md) | Keep, but do not pretend data settles it |

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"Studies show war is in our genes."** No study shows this. The strongest genuine claim is that humans possess the *capacity* for coordinated lethal aggression, which is a very weak premise. Bonobos are as closely related to us as chimpanzees and show no observed lethal intergroup raiding — which by itself defeats the naive inference.
- **"Wars are caused by scarcity."** Resource scarcity is a weak and inconsistent predictor. Wars are correlated more strongly with *shifting relative power* and *weak state capacity* than with absolute deprivation.
- **"Understanding why a war started tells you who will win."** Independent questions with independent evidence bases. Q3 and Q4 barely talk to each other.
- **"The decline of violence has been proven."** One component (interpersonal homicide in Europe) is very well established. The headline war component is not, and treating them as a single finding is the core rhetorical move that makes the thesis look stronger than it is.
- **"Historians disagree about everything, so nobody knows."** False symmetry. Q4 has robust, replicated findings. The uncertainty is unevenly distributed and pretending otherwise is its own error.

## Open questions

- [ ] Is there a defensible way to estimate a pre-state lethal-violence baseline that corrects for excavation selection effects?
- [ ] Can Q3-type explanations (bargaining failure) and Q5-type claims (secular decline) be unified in a single model, or are they genuinely different levels?
- [ ] What would a well-specified change-point model for war severity look like, and has anyone actually fitted one with a pre-registered alternative?
- [ ] Do civil war and interstate war share enough causal structure to belong in one dataset, or does merging them destroy the signal?
- [ ] How much of the "decline of war" is measurement — better reporting of small conflicts making the denominator look worse?
- [ ] Is "conflict onset" even the right dependent variable, given that most crises never reach a coding threshold?

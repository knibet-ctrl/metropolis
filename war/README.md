# War and Violence

> **Status: index file.** Individual files carry their own confidence tiers.
> **Provenance: written from general knowledge, without live source verification.** Names, dates, figures and study findings are reproduced from memory and should be checked before being quoted or relied upon. Where a number is contested I have tried to say so rather than pick a favourite — but the *ranges themselves* may be misremembered.

This notebook studies organised violence as a catastrophe with structure. It is not military history for its own sake, and it contains nothing that functions as operational guidance. The question throughout is epistemic: **what do we actually know about why humans fight, what decides conflicts, and whether violence is declining — and how much of what we think we know is an artefact of bad data?**

## The casualty-figure warning (read this first)

War death tolls are among the most politically contested numbers in any field. Almost every figure in this notebook is an estimate with a wide interval, produced by a method with known biases, in a context where somebody benefits from the number being larger or smaller.

| Failure mode | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Registration collapse read as death | An Lushan Rebellion (755–763): a ~36 million drop in Tang census rolls is widely cited as deaths; most historians read it as tax-register collapse | Inflates by an order of magnitude |
| Propaganda figures fossilised | Dresden (Feb 1945): 100,000–500,000 claimed at various times; the 2010 Dresden Historical Commission concluded ~22,700–25,000 | Inflates ~10–20× |
| Survey extrapolation vs body count | Iraq post-2003: documented civilian deaths ~185,000–210,000 (Iraq Body Count) vs ~654,000 excess deaths (Lancet 2006, CI 393k–943k) vs ~405,000 (PLOS Med 2013) vs ~1.03m (ORB, widely criticised) | 5× spread on the same war |
| Denial as state policy | Armenian genocide (1915–16): estimates ~600,000–1.5 million, with active Turkish state denial | Suppresses |
| Definitional threshold | UCDP counts conflicts at 25 battle deaths/year; Correlates of War at 1,000 | Different datasets show different *trends*, not just different levels |
| Excess deaths vs violent deaths | Most war mortality is often indirect (disease, displacement, famine) | Changes the answer by 2–10× depending on definition |

**Rule for this notebook:** give a range, name the method, name the dispute. Never a single confident number for a pre-modern or contested event.

## Ten compressed headline findings

1. **The pre-state baseline rate of lethal violence is genuinely unknown.** Both the Hobbesian picture (constant raiding) and the Rousseauian picture (peaceful foragers) are projections onto a sample too small, too non-random and too contaminated by state contact to settle the question. See [origins-of-war.md](origins-of-war.md).

2. **War is theoretically puzzling and that puzzle is productive.** Because fighting destroys value, a settlement both sides prefer to war almost always exists on paper. Fearon's 1995 answer — private information with incentives to lie, commitment problems, indivisibility — is the most useful single framework in the field, and it is a *filter*, not a complete theory.

3. **Logistics beat tactics, and disease beat both.** Until roughly the twentieth century, more soldiers died of disease than of enemy action in most large campaigns. The decisive constraints on armies have historically been calories, fodder, transport and sanitation.

4. **Materiel usually wins, and the exceptions are political.** The richer, larger, better-supplied side wins most interstate wars. When it doesn't, the usual mechanism is asymmetry of *cost tolerance and time horizon*, not asymmetry of firepower (Mack 1975).

5. **Whether nuclear weapons have kept the peace is unresolved, and the sample size is one.** There has been no great-power war since 1945 and no combat use of a nuclear weapon since Nagasaki. There is no way to distinguish deterrence working from luck, and the documented close calls are numerous enough to make the luck hypothesis serious.

6. **Interpersonal violence in Europe fell by roughly 30–50× since the Middle Ages, and that finding is solid.** Manuel Eisner's historical homicide series is the strongest quantitative pillar of any "decline of violence" claim. The *war* component of that claim is far weaker.

7. **The "long peace" is not yet statistically distinguishable from a lull.** Because war severity is fat-tailed, Aaron Clauset (2018) estimated roughly another century of quiet would be needed before the post-1945 pattern is significant against the 1823–2003 record. This is a statement about statistical power, not a prediction.

8. **Atrocity is committed overwhelmingly by ordinary people under specific institutional conditions.** Browning's Reserve Police Battalion 101 is the load-bearing case. But the 1970s social-psychology scaffolding around it (Milgram's headline framing, the Stanford Prison Experiment) has been substantially reassessed and should not be leaned on.

9. **Territorial conquest has nearly disappeared as a routine outcome of war.** State death by conquest was common before 1945 and became rare after. This is one of the largest changes in the international system and it is badly under-discussed. It is also, by construction, reversible.

10. **Confident military prediction has a poor record and the failures rhyme.** 1914's short-war illusion, interwar strategic-bombing theory, Vietnam's attrition metrics, and the 2003 Iraq stabilisation assumptions all failed in the same direction: underestimating adversary adaptation and the political layer.

## Honest caveats

- **No live verification.** Written from general knowledge on 2026-09-01. Treat every citation as a pointer to check, not a warrant.
- **Anglophone tilt.** The literature I can reproduce reliably is overwhelmingly English-language and heavily weighted to European and North American cases. Chinese, Indian, Persian, African and Latin American military historiography is under-represented here, and that is a real distortion of the base rates.
- **Survivorship in the archives.** We know most about wars fought by literate bureaucratic states that kept records and lost them slowly.
- **No verdicts on live conflicts.** Where an ongoing conflict is referenced, the point is analytical — what it illustrates about a mechanism or a data problem. This notebook takes no position on the rights and wrongs of any current war.
- **Atrocity is not case data.** [atrocity-and-genocide.md](atrocity-and-genocide.md) discusses mass killing because prevention requires understanding it. It should be read with that constraint held.

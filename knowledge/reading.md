# Reading — Primary Sources Worth The Time

> **Status: recommendations, not claims.** Prefer primaries where readable. Most
> of what is wrong in popular discussion of science comes from people arguing
> about books they have read summaries of — and, increasingly, from people
> arguing about *critiques* of books nobody has read. **The correctives section
> at the end is the most important part of this file.**

---

## Start here — highest return per hour

| Work | Why |
|---|---|
| **Ioannidis — "Why Most Published Research Findings Are False"** (2005, PLoS Medicine) | ~8 pages, free, and the argument is simple arithmetic. **If you read one thing on this list, read this.** Note it is a model, not a measurement |
| **Simmons, Nelson & Simonsohn — "False-Positive Psychology"** (2011, Psychological Science) | The paper that finally landed. Short, funny, devastating. They prove that listening to *When I'm Sixty-Four* makes you younger |
| **Meehl — "Theoretical Risks and Tabular Asterisks"** (1978, J. Consulting and Clinical Psychology) | Predicted the entire replication crisis, in detail, thirty-three years early, and was ignored. **Read it to understand that the problem was never a lack of information** |
| **Smaldino & McElreath — "The Natural Selection of Bad Science"** (2016, Royal Society Open Science) | Shows the crisis emerging from incentives with no bad actors anywhere. The theoretical core of this notebook |
| **Feynman — "Cargo Cult Science"** (1974 Caltech commencement address) | A few pages. "The first principle is that you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool." Also contains the Millikan oil-drop story about how measurements drifted slowly toward truth because nobody wanted to differ too much from the last person |

## Philosophy of science

- **Popper — *The Logic of Scientific Discovery*** (1934 German, 1959 English). Falsificationism from the source. *Conjectures and Refutations* (1963) is more readable and contains the Einstein/Adler contrast that started it.
- **Kuhn — *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*** (1962). The most influential book on this list and the most misused. **Read the 1969 postscript**, where he responds to the relativist reading and narrows "paradigm."
- **Lakatos — "Falsification and the Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes"** (1970). The best single reconciliation of Popper and Kuhn. Progressive versus degenerating programmes is the most useful concept in the field.
- **Feyerabend — *Against Method*** (1975). Genuinely subversive and more careful than its reputation. His Galileo chapters are the substance; the political conclusion does not follow from them.
- **Quine — "Two Dogmas of Empiricism"** (1951). ~25 pages, and it dismantles the analytic/synthetic distinction and confirmation holism in one go.
- **Duhem — *The Aim and Structure of Physical Theory*** (1906). The underdetermination argument, stated by a working physicist.
- **Hacking — *Representing and Intervening*** (1983). The best corrective to a purely theory-focused philosophy of science: **experiments have a life of their own.** Underrated.
- **Mayo — *Statistical Inference as Severe Testing*** (2018). The strongest modern rehabilitation of the Popperian intuition, worked out statistically. Hard going. *Error and the Growth of Experimental Knowledge* (1996) is the earlier statement.
- **Laudan — "The Demise of the Demarcation Problem"** (1983). The essay that closed the question.
- **Godfrey-Smith — *Theory and Reality*** (2003; 2nd ed. 2021). **The best single-volume introduction.** Fair, current, and honest about what is unresolved. If you want one book instead of the eight above, this is it.

## History of science and how theories die

- **Fleck — *Genesis and Development of a Scientific Fact*** (1935). Written by a microbiologist, decades before Kuhn, and Kuhn credited it. "Thought collectives" and "thought styles." **The most underread book on this list.**
- **Oreskes — *The Rejection of Continental Drift*** (1999). Careful, unromantic account of why American geologists were not being stupid. Corrects the heroic version told in most textbooks.
- **Nuland — *The Doctors' Plague*** (2003) on Semmelweis; see also leaders/profiles/ignaz-semmelweis.md.
- **Marshall (ed.) — *Helicobacter Pioneers*** (2002). First-hand accounts, including of Lykoudis, whose case is the one that should be taught.
- **Weiner — *The Piltdown Forgery*** (1955). By one of the men who exposed it.
- **Kevles — *In the Name of Eugenics*** (1985). How respectable, well-funded, peer-reviewed mainstream science produced forced sterilisation. **The most important control-group text on the list.**
- **Soyfer — *Lysenko and the Tragedy of Soviet Science*** (1994). What happens when the incentive structure is set by a state.
- **El-Hai — *The Lobotomist*** (2005). Walter Freeman. Read alongside the fact that Moniz's 1949 Nobel has never been rescinded.
- **Collins — *Gravity's Shadow*** (2004). The experimenter's regress, documented over decades in the gravitational-wave community — before LIGO resolved it. **A sociologist of science who was right and then watched the field prove it.**
- **Latour & Woolgar — *Laboratory Life*** (1979). The founding ethnography. Read for the observation, not the conclusions.

## The replication crisis

- **Ritchie — *Science Fictions*** (2020). The best single popular account: fraud, bias, negligence and hype, by a psychologist who co-authored a failed replication. **Start here if you want one book.**
- **Chambers — *The Seven Deadly Sins of Psychology*** (2017). By the person who created Registered Reports. Insider, systematic, constructive.
- **Open Science Collaboration — "Estimating the Reproducibility of Psychological Science"** (2015, Science). The primary source for the 36% figure. **Read Gilbert et al.'s 2016 critique and the reply alongside it** — the dispute is more instructive than either paper.
- **Turner et al. — "Selective Publication of Antidepressant Trials"** (2008, NEJM). The cleanest measurement of publication bias ever made, because the FDA registry gave a complete denominator.
- **Silberzahn et al. — "Many Analysts, One Data Set"** (2018). 29 teams, one dataset, wildly different answers.
- **Border et al. — "No Support for Historical Candidate Gene..."** (2019, Am J Psychiatry). The demolition of two decades of psychiatric genetics.
- **Gelman & Loken — "The Garden of Forking Paths"** (2013). Why p-hacking requires no intent.
- **Goldacre — *Bad Pharma*** (2012). Trial registration, missing data, and the industry-specific version of everything in this notebook. *Bad Science* (2008) is the general-audience version and is excellent.
- **Le Texier — "Debunking the Stanford Prison Experiment"** (2019, American Psychologist). The archival takedown.
- **Perry — *Behind the Shock Machine*** (2012). Same for Milgram, and more sympathetic to Milgram than you expect.

## Bias, reasoning and judgement

- **Kahneman — *Thinking, Fast and Slow*** (2011). Essential and **partly unreliable**: Kahneman conceded the priming chapter rested on underpowered studies. Read it with the crisis literature to hand.
- **Tetlock — *Expert Political Judgment*** (2005) and **Tetlock & Gardner — *Superforecasting*** (2015). Read both. The first shows the problem, the second shows what works.
- **Meehl — *Clinical versus Statistical Prediction*** (1954). Seventy years old, never refuted, still ignored by practitioners.
- **Kahneman, Sibony & Sunstein — *Noise*** (2021). Inconsistency, not bias, may be the larger error source. The most useful *new* idea in the judgement literature.
- **Mercier & Sperber — *The Enigma of Reason*** (2017). Reasoning evolved for argument. Explains the shape of the bias catalogue better than the bias catalogue does.
- **Haidt — *The Righteous Mind*** (2012). The elephant and the rider. **Note the moral foundations research has had replication difficulties**; the intuitionist core is on firmer ground than the six-foundations taxonomy.
- **Stanovich — *The Bias That Divides Us*** (2021). Myside bias and its near-independence from intelligence. The most important single finding in bias-and-reasoning.md.
- **Gigerenzer — *Calculated Risks*** / ***Reckoning with Risk***. Natural frequencies. **The most immediately actionable book on this whole list.**
- **Kahan** — the cultural cognition papers, particularly the 2012 *Nature Climate Change* piece and "Motivated Numeracy" (2017). Read the replication disputes too.

## Institutions, incentives and metrics

- **Merton — "The Normative Structure of Science"** (1942) and "The Matthew Effect in Science" (1968). Both short.
- **Mitroff — *The Subjective Side of Science*** (1974). Studied Apollo lunar scientists and found them operating on the *opposite* of Merton's norms. Read the pair together; the contrast is the lesson.
- **Muller — *The Tyranny of Metrics*** (2018). Campbell's law across medicine, policing, education and academia.
- **Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen & Webb — "Are Ideas Getting Harder to Find?"** (2020, AER). The productivity-decline evidence.
- **Park, Leahey & Funk — "Papers and Patents Are Becoming Less Disruptive"** (2023, Nature). **Read the critiques alongside it**; the measure is contested.
- **Boldrin & Levine — *Against Intellectual Monopoly*** (2008). The strong sceptical case on patents. A minority position argued seriously.
- **Polanyi — *Personal Knowledge*** (1958) and "The Republic of Science" (1962). Tacit knowledge, and the argument that science is a self-coordinating order rather than a planned one. **The best defence of the existing system on this list, and it is not naive.**
- **Hayek — "The Use of Knowledge in Society"** (1945). About markets, and it is the same problem: knowledge is distributed, local and largely tacit, and no central evaluator has it. Applies directly to grant panels.

## Correctives

**The section that matters.** Everything above leans one way — toward "the
system is more broken than you think." A diet of correctives produces a person
who is confidently wrong in a more sophisticated register. **Read these against
the rest.**

| Work | What it corrects |
|---|---|
| **Gilbert, King, Pettigrew & Wilson (2016), Science** | The strongest technical case that the OSC replication rate is a substantial underestimate. Usually dismissed rather than answered. **Read it.** |
| **Polanyi — "The Republic of Science"** (1962) | The case that decentralised, reputation-driven, unplanned science is not a broken bureaucracy but a functioning spontaneous order |
| **Oreskes — *Why Trust Science?*** (2019) | Argues the warrant for trusting science is precisely its *social* character — diverse, organised, critical scrutiny — not any method. **The best direct answer to this notebook's sceptical framing** |
| **Cobb & Comfort (2023), Nature, on Rosalind Franklin** | Corrects a myth that the corrective literature itself created and repeats |
| **Sokal & Bricmont — *Fashionable Nonsense*** (1998) | The case against the strong programme. **And note both the Sokal hoax and the 2018 grievance-studies affair lack control groups**, which is the exact failing this notebook criticises elsewhere |
| **Pigliucci & Boudry (eds.) — *Philosophy of Pseudoscience*** (2013) | Argues Laudan was too quick and demarcation is worth reviving as a cluster concept |
| **Nichols — *The Death of Expertise*** (2017) | The other side of expertise-and-authority.md. **Overstated in places and correct about the core problem**: the collapse of any distinction between informed and uninformed opinion |
| **Teicholz — *The Big Fat Surprise*** (2014) | The revisionist case on dietary fat. **Include it and then read the criticism of it**, because the revisionist story is itself overstated — the widely repeated claim that Ancel Keys cherry-picked 7 of 22 countries misrepresents the Seven Countries Study's design |
| **Ramsden et al. (2016), BMJ** — recovered Minnesota Coronary Experiment data | The primary evidence in that dispute, rather than the polemics on either side |
| **Wootton — *The Invention of Science*** (2015) | A robust historian's case that something real and discontinuous happened between 1572 and 1704, against the constructivist drift |

## Deliberately not on this list

- **Anything titled "The Scientific Method."** There isn't one.
- **Popular books built primarily on 2000-2015 social psychology.** Large sections of that literature did not survive. This includes several bestsellers whose central findings are now unsupported; check any specific effect against the replication record before repeating it.
- **Single-study science journalism.** See practices.md.
- **Sokal-style hoaxes as evidence about fields.** Entertaining, uncontrolled, and they prove less than they are used to prove — in both directions.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Belief about the literature | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Kuhn showed science is socially constructed" | He denied this repeatedly. The postscript exists because the misreading started immediately |
| "Popper is the standard account of science" | He is the standard account in public discourse and a minority position among philosophers of science |
| "*Thinking, Fast and Slow* is settled science" | Its author publicly retracted a chapter. Read it with the errata in mind |
| "Feyerabend was a relativist crank" | He was a rigorous historian of the Galileo case whose conclusions about method are hard to dismiss and whose politics are separable |
| "*Science Fictions* proves science is broken" | Ritchie's own conclusion is reformist, not nihilist, and he says so in the last chapter that reviewers skip |
| "Reading the critics is enough" | Read Polanyi and Oreskes or you will end up with a confident, one-sided, unfalsifiable view of a field whose central lesson is not to have one |

## Open questions

- [ ] Is there a good history of the *physical* sciences' quality-control practices comparable to the psychology literature? I do not know of one, and its absence is conspicuous.
- [ ] What is the best single treatment of tacit and craft knowledge since Polanyi? Collins's *Tacit and Explicit Knowledge* (2010) is the obvious candidate; I have not read it closely enough to recommend it.
- [ ] Nothing here covers non-Western knowledge traditions, indigenous ecological knowledge, or the history of science outside Europe and North America. **That is a real gap in this notebook, not an editorial judgement.**
- [ ] No adequate primary source exists on the epistemics of machine-learning research. The field is generating an enormous literature with almost none of the safeguards described here, and nobody has written the book.

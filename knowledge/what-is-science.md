# What Is Science? — The Demarcation Problem

> **Status: Tier 1 (negative result).** That no single criterion separates
> science from non-science is about as settled as anything in philosophy gets.
> Laudan declared the problem dead in 1983 and the field largely agreed.
> **What replaces it is Tier 2 and genuinely live.**

---

## Why anyone cared

The problem was not academic. Three practical pressures created it:

1. **Vienna, 1920s.** The logical positivists wanted to separate meaningful
   statements from metaphysics. Their verification criterion — a statement is
   meaningful if it can be conclusively verified — collapsed almost
   immediately: it makes universal laws ("all electrons have charge e")
   meaningless, and it fails its own test.
2. **Popper's specific irritation.** Karl Popper watched Marxism and
   Freudian/Adlerian psychoanalysis explain every possible observation, and
   watched Einstein's general relativity stake everything on a light-bending
   prediction that Eddington's 1919 eclipse expedition could have destroyed.
   *(The 1919 data were noisier and the analysis more debatable than the legend
   suggests — Earman and Glymour, 1980.)* The contrast was the seed of
   falsificationism.
3. **Courts and schools.** *McLean v. Arkansas* (1982) required a judge to
   define science to exclude creation science; Michael Ruse testified with a
   list of criteria, and Laudan attacked the reasoning publicly even though he
   agreed with the verdict. **The demarcation problem has a body count of bad
   legal reasoning.**

## Popper: falsificationism

**The claim:** a theory is scientific if it forbids something. Not "can it be
proved?" but "what observation would refute it?" Science advances by bold
conjectures and severe attempts at refutation. Confirmation is cheap;
corroboration only counts when the theory took a real risk.

**What is right about it, and it is a lot:**
- It correctly identifies the asymmetry between confirmation and refutation.
- It correctly identifies the vice of unfalsifiable theories that explain everything.
- "What would change your mind?" remains the single best question in the notebook.
- It is *normatively* useful even where it is descriptively false.

**Why it fails as a criterion:**

| Problem | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Duhem-Quine** | No hypothesis is tested alone. A failed prediction refutes the conjunction of hypothesis + auxiliary assumptions + instrument theory + background. You can always relocate the blame |
| **Probabilistic hypotheses** | "This drug reduces mortality by 8%" is not strictly falsifiable by any finite dataset. Most of modern science is statistical |
| **Historical falsity** | Scientists do not, and should not, abandon good theories on one anomaly. Newtonian mechanics failed on Uranus; Le Verrier blamed an unseen planet and found Neptune (1846). The *same* move on Mercury's perihelion produced "Vulcan," which does not exist. **Identical reasoning, opposite outcomes — and no rule tells you which case you are in** |
| **Popper on Darwinism** | He called natural selection a "metaphysical research programme" rather than a testable theory, then partially retracted in 1978. The criterion embarrassed its own author on the central theory of biology |
| **Pseudoscience is often falsifiable** | Astrology makes checkable predictions; they fail; the practice continues. Falsifiability doesn't demarcate — **willingness to accept falsification** might, and that is a sociological property, not a logical one |

## Kuhn: paradigms and normal science

**The claim** (*The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*, 1962): mature science
is not perpetual revolution. It is **normal science** — puzzle-solving inside an
accepted framework (a *paradigm*) that supplies problems, standards, exemplars
and instruments. Anomalies accumulate, are tolerated, then become intolerable;
a crisis opens; a revolution installs a new paradigm; and the new paradigm is
partly **incommensurable** with the old — terms shift meaning, standards shift,
and there is no neutral algorithm for the choice.

**The strongest part** is descriptive and, in my view, correct: **most working
scientists spend their careers on puzzles, not on tests of fundamentals, and
this is productive rather than a failure.** Popper's picture of permanent
revolution would prevent the accumulation that makes revolutions possible.

**The weaknesses:**
- **"Paradigm" is hopelessly overloaded.** Margaret Masterman (1970) counted 21 distinct senses in the book. Kuhn later retreated to "disciplinary matrix" and "exemplar."
- **Incommensurability is overstated.** Later Kuhn weakened it substantially to local untranslatability. Scientists in fact compare theories across revolutions constantly; Newtonian mechanics is derivable as a limiting case of relativity.
- **Domain-limited.** The model fits physics and chemistry. Biology, geology and the social sciences do not obviously have paradigms in the strong sense — or have many at once.
- **The relativist reading is not Kuhn's.** He spent decades objecting to it. *(Contested: critics argue his text supports it whatever he intended.)*

## Lakatos: research programmes

Lakatos tried to keep Popper's rationality and Kuhn's history. A **research
programme** has:
- a **hard core** held immune by decision (Newton's laws; natural selection),
- a **protective belt** of auxiliary hypotheses that absorb anomalies,
- a **negative heuristic** (don't touch the core) and a **positive heuristic**
  (how to develop the belt).

The unit of appraisal is the programme over time, not a single theory. A
programme is **progressive** if its modifications predict novel facts that then
check out (Neptune), and **degenerating** if its modifications are purely
ad hoc face-saving (Vulcan, and — Lakatos's own target — much of Marxist
historiography).

**This is the best thing in the file.** It explains why an anomaly does not kill
a theory, why holding the core fixed is rational, and why some theory-rescuing
is legitimate and some is not.

**Where it breaks:** you can usually only classify a programme retrospectively.
Lakatos never specified how long a degenerating programme may degenerate before
abandoning it becomes rational — and history has cases of programmes that
degenerated for decades and then revived. **It gives you an excellent postmortem
and almost no prospective guidance.**

## Feyerabend: against method

*Against Method* (1975). **The strongest steelman in the notebook, and the most
misread.**

**What he actually argued:** every proposed methodological rule has been
violated by successful science, often necessarily. His central case is Galileo,
and he presses it hard: the telescope's reliability was genuinely unestablished
(it produced known artefacts, and there was no optical theory explaining why it
should work on celestial objects); the tower argument against Earth's motion was
a good argument on the physics of the day; Galileo won partly by rhetoric,
selective reporting, propaganda and writing in vernacular Italian. **His
opponents were not stupid, and by the standards of the time they were not even
unreasonable.**

Therefore: "anything goes" is not a positive methodology. It is the observation
that **no rule can be imposed in advance without sometimes blocking progress.**

**The uncomfortable part that critics skip:** Feyerabend was largely right about
the history. **The part critics are right about:** his political conclusion — a
free society should separate science and state as it separates church and state
— does not follow from the methodological premise, and he never adequately
answered why, if no method is privileged, science's track record so exceeds
alternatives.

## Where the field landed

| Position | Content | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Laudan (1983)** | "The Demise of the Demarcation Problem." No necessary-and-sufficient criterion exists; the term "pseudoscience" does emotional not epistemic work | Mainstream |
| **Cluster / family resemblance** | Pigliucci & Boudry (2013): demarcation survives as a multi-dimensional, fuzzy, graded notion. Astrology fails on many dimensions at once | Growing |
| **Merton's norms (1942)** | Communism (shared results), Universalism, Disinterestedness, Organized Skepticism. Describes an *ethos*, not a method | Influential, empirically shaky — Mitroff (1974) documented Apollo scientists operating on counter-norms: secrecy, particularism, emotional commitment |
| **Mayo's severe testing** | *Error and the Growth of Experimental Knowledge* (1996); *Statistical Inference as Severe Testing* (2018). A claim is warranted to the degree it has passed tests it would probably have failed if false | **The strongest live rehabilitation of the Popperian intuition.** Operationalises "risky test" statistically |
| **Sociology of scientific knowledge** | Latour & Woolgar's *Laboratory Life* (1979); the Strong Programme (Bloor, Barnes). Study science as social practice, symmetrically explaining true and false beliefs | Valuable ethnography; the strong constructivist reading is widely rejected, including by many in the field |

## Steelmanning the constructivists

The Sokal hoax (1996) and the "grievance studies" affair (2018) are usually
deployed to dismiss this literature wholesale. **Both have the exact
methodological flaw this notebook keeps flagging: no control group.** Sokal
placed one nonsense paper in one non-peer-reviewed journal issue. Boghossian,
Lindsay and Pluckrose got 7 of 20 papers accepted with no comparison arm in
other fields. Neither establishes a differential rate.

And the core observation is correct and important: **what counts as an
acceptable result is negotiated by people with careers, instruments they trust
for social reasons, and funders.** Harry Collins's *experimenter's regress* is
the sharpest version: to know your apparatus works you need to know the right
answer; to know the right answer you need a working apparatus. Collins
documented this in the gravitational-wave community around Joseph Weber's
disputed 1960s-70s detections — and the regress was eventually broken, by LIGO
in 2015, through instrumental convergence and independent detection. **The
regress is real and it is escapable. Both halves matter.**

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Science is defined by falsifiability" | Popper's criterion is a useful heuristic that fails as a definition, for at least five independent reasons. Almost no philosopher of science holds it as stated |
| "Kuhn showed science is irrational" | He explicitly denied this. He argued theory choice uses values (accuracy, scope, simplicity, fruitfulness, consistency) that don't compose into an algorithm — which is not the same as arbitrary |
| "Feyerabend was an enemy of science" | He was an enemy of methodological monism and of science's political authority. His historical case against clean method is largely accurate |
| "The scientific method" | There is no singular method. Astronomy cannot experiment; palaeontology cannot repeat; particle physics has 5-sigma thresholds; ecology has neither. These are different epistemic enterprises sharing an ethos |
| "Occam's razor is a law of nature" | It is a preference. It has no justification that isn't circular, and it has been wrong repeatedly — the simplest account of the solar system was geocentric until it wasn't, and quantum mechanics is not simpler than classical |
| "Correlation is not causation, therefore correlational studies are worthless" | Correlational evidence with adequate design (natural experiments, instrumental variables, Mendelian randomisation, dose-response, temporal ordering) supports causal inference. Smoking and lung cancer was established without a single RCT — over R.A. Fisher's objections |

# How Theories Die

> **Status: Tier 1 for the mechanism, Tier 2 for the sociology.** That theories
> are never refuted by a single observation is logically established
> (Duhem-Quine) and historically confirmed. *Why* particular theories died when
> they did — evidence, generational turnover, or funding — is contested, and the
> empirical work on it is thinner than the confident quotations suggest.

---

## The mechanism: nothing is ever refuted alone

**Pierre Duhem** (*The Aim and Structure of Physical Theory*, 1906): a physics
experiment tests a whole system — hypothesis, auxiliary assumptions, instrument
theory, background physics. When the prediction fails, logic tells you *the
conjunction* is false. It does not tell you which conjunct to reject.

**W.V.O. Quine** ("Two Dogmas of Empiricism", 1951) generalised it to all
belief: knowledge is a web that meets experience only at the edges. "Any
statement can be held true come what may, if we make drastic enough adjustments
elsewhere in the system." No statement is immune to revision — not even logic.

**The practical consequence:** when your theory fails, you have a menu.

| Move | Legitimate example | Illegitimate example |
|---|---|---|
| Blame an unseen entity | Le Verrier posits Neptune from Uranus's orbit (1846) — found within a year | Vulcan, posited from Mercury's perihelion — never existed; resolved by general relativity in 1915 |
| Blame the instrument | Michelson-Morley's null result questioned repeatedly, correctly, for decades | N-rays: Blondlot's apparatus "worked" even with the prism removed |
| Add an auxiliary hypothesis | Lorentz-FitzGerald contraction, which turned out to be *right* as kinematics | Ptolemaic epicycles in their late phase |
| Restrict the theory's scope | Newtonian mechanics as a low-velocity limit | "It works, but only when a sceptic isn't watching" |

**There is no rule distinguishing the columns in advance.** Lakatos's
progressive/degenerating distinction is the best attempt and it is
retrospective. This is the single most important structural fact about how
theories die.

## Planck's principle — and what the research actually shows

The quotation, from Max Planck's *Scientific Autobiography* (published 1948):

> "A new scientific truth does not triumph by convincing its opponents and
> making them see the light, but rather because its opponents eventually die,
> and a new generation grows up that is familiar with it."

Usually compressed to "science advances one funeral at a time." **It is quoted
as though it were established. It has been tested, twice, with different
methods and partly conflicting results.**

**Hull, Tessner & Diamond (1978), *Science*.** They took British scientists'
acceptance of Darwinism after 1859 and asked whether age predicted resistance.
It did — very weakly. Younger scientists accepted it somewhat faster, but age
explained little of the variance, and most older scientists converted within a
decade. **Direct test of Planck's principle: largely negative.**

**Azoulay, Fons-Rosen & Graff Zivin (2019), *American Economic Review*, "Does
Science Advance One Funeral at a Time?"** A different design: 452 elite life
scientists who died prematurely while active. After each death, in that
subfield, publications by the star's collaborators fell sharply (~20%), while
publications by *non-collaborators who had never worked in the subfield* rose
(~8%) — and the entrants' papers were disproportionately highly cited and
disproportionately *did not* cite the deceased. **Strongly positive — but for a
different mechanism.**

**The reconciliation, and it is the interesting part:** individual scientists do
change their minds reasonably often. What doesn't change while the eminent
person lives is **who gets funded, who gets published, who gets hired, and which
questions are askable.** Planck diagnosed a psychological effect; the data point
to a structural one. *(Caveat: the Azoulay design is limited to elite life
sciences and to premature deaths, which is a specific and possibly unusual
sample.)*

## Case studies: correct ideas that were rejected

### Continental drift (Wegener, 1912-1915 → accepted ~1965)

Alfred Wegener, a meteorologist, proposed continental displacement in 1912 and
in *Die Entstehung der Kontinente und Ozeane* (1915). Evidence: coastline fit,
matching fossil distributions across oceans (*Glossopteris*, *Mesosaurus*),
matching geological formations, palaeoclimate indicators.

**Why it was rejected, honestly:** he had no mechanism. His proposed forces —
*Polflucht* and tidal drag — were shown by Harold Jeffreys to be many orders of
magnitude too weak to move continents through oceanic crust. **Jeffreys was
right.** The 1926 American Association of Petroleum Geologists symposium in New
York was hostile, and Rollin Chamberlin's complaint that accepting Wegener meant
forgetting everything learned in seventy years captures the tone.

**What changed it:** not persuasion. New instruments and new data — palaeomagnetism
in the 1950s (Runcorn's polar wander curves), Hess's seafloor-spreading
hypothesis (1962), the Vine-Matthews-Morley interpretation of magnetic striping
(1963), and Wilson's transform faults (1965). **A mechanism arrived, and the
field converted in about five years.**

**The honest lesson is not "the establishment was closed-minded."** It is that
**a correct phenomenon with a wrong mechanism is genuinely hard to distinguish
from a wrong phenomenon**, and the demand for a mechanism was a reasonable
scientific demand that happened to delay a true theory by fifty years. Wegener
died on the Greenland ice in 1930, aged 50.

### Puerperal fever (Semmelweis, 1847-1865)

Covered in detail in leaders/profiles/ignaz-semmelweis.md. The compressed
version: Ignaz Semmelweis found the maternal mortality in the Vienna General
Hospital's doctor-staffed First Clinic ran roughly 10% against roughly 4% in the
midwife-staffed Second Clinic; Jakob Kolletschka's 1847 death from a
dissection-room scalpel cut showed the same pathology; chlorinated lime
handwashing cut mortality to ~1-2%. He published *Die Ätiologie...des
Kindbettfiebers* only in 1861, wrote polemically, called opponents murderers,
and died in an asylum in 1865 at 47 — from sepsis, after a beating.

**Why it failed to take, and none of it is flattering to anyone:** no germ
theory existed to make "cadaverous particles" intelligible; the claim implied
that respectable physicians were killing their patients; Semmelweis's own
statistical presentation was poor and delayed by fourteen years; and his
personal conduct made him easy to dismiss. **Being right is not sufficient. It
is not even close to sufficient.**

### Peptic ulcers and *Helicobacter pylori* (1982-1994)

Robin Warren observed spiral bacteria in gastric biopsies from 1979; Barry
Marshall joined in 1981; the organism was cultured in 1982 (accidentally, after
plates were left over a five-day Easter break — the standard 48-hour protocol
had been killing it). *Lancet*, 1984. Marshall drank a culture in 1984 and
developed acute gastritis. Nobel Prize, 2005.

**Prior art nobody remembers:** Giulio Bizzozero described spiral organisms in
dog stomachs in 1893. John Lykoudis, a Greek general practitioner, treated
ulcers with antibiotics through the 1950s-60s, was refused publication and fined
by the Athens medical association. **He was right and it made no difference at
all**, which is the part the inspirational retelling omits.

**Why resistance was not simply dogma:** the initial evidence was correlational;
Koch's postulates were unmet; and there is a real confounder — most infected
people never develop ulcers (roughly 10-15% do). Once randomised trials arrived,
the field moved fast: the NIH consensus conference endorsed antibiotic treatment
in 1994. **Twelve years, not fifty.**

### Others worth the line

- **Barbara McClintock**, transposable elements, 1940s-50s: work was not so much rejected as unassimilable until molecular tools existed. Nobel 1983. *(The "she was ignored for decades" story is partly a retrospective construction — see Nathaniel Comfort's revisionist work.)*
- **Prions.** Stanley Prusiner's protein-only infectious agent (1982) violated the central dogma and was widely doubted. Nobel 1997.
- **Ignoring the reverse:** for every case above there are unrecorded thousands where the outsider was simply wrong. **No list of those exists, which is precisely the survivorship problem.**

## Case studies: wrong ideas that died, and how

| Theory | Life | How it actually ended |
|---|---|---|
| **Phlogiston** | Stahl, ~1703 → 1780s | Not by a crucial experiment. Lavoisier's quantitative work made mass-gain-on-calcination decisive **once you accepted the gravimetric standard he was promoting**. Priestley never accepted it and died a phlogistonist in 1804. A genuine instance of Planck's principle |
| **Luminiferous aether** | ~1800s → ~1920s | **Michelson-Morley (1887) did not kill it.** Lorentz and FitzGerald absorbed the null result with a contraction hypothesis — a textbook auxiliary rescue that turned out to be right as kinematics. The aether became *superfluous* with special relativity (1905), which is a different death. Lorentz never fully let go |
| **Caloric** | 1780s → 1850s | Killed by mechanical-equivalent-of-heat measurements (Rumford 1798, Joule 1840s) plus a better theory. Rumford's cannon-boring experiment is usually told as decisive; it wasn't, for fifty years |
| **Miasma** | Antiquity → 1880s | John Snow's Broad Street pump (1854) is celebrated; it did not convince the Board of Health at the time. Miasma died with bacteriology (Koch's cholera vibrio, 1883-84) |
| **Lysenkoism** | 1930s → 1964 | **Did not die scientifically at all.** It died when Khrushchev fell and Lysenko lost political protection. Genetics had been banned as bourgeois pseudoscience from the August 1948 VASKhNIL session. Vavilov, the world's leading plant geneticist, was arrested in 1940 and died in prison in 1943. The doctrine was exported to China and contributed to Great Leap famine mortality |
| **N-rays** | 1903-1904 | René Blondlot at Nancy; roughly 300 papers by ~120 authors. Robert W. Wood visited in 1904, covertly removed the aluminium prism from the apparatus, and Blondlot's assistant continued reporting the spectrum. Wood published in *Nature*. **The fastest death in the file — because it was a perception claim, cheaply testable** |
| **Cold fusion** | March 1989 → ~1990 | Fleischmann and Pons announced by press conference before publication. Failed replications within months; DOE panels in 1989 and 2004 both negative. *(A Google-funded replication programme published in Nature in 2019 found no evidence of the effect — while producing useful materials science. Low-energy nuclear reaction research continues at the margins.)* |
| **Piltdown Man** | 1912 → 1953 | Orangutan jaw, human cranium, stained and filed. Exposed by Weiner, Le Gros Clark and Oakley using fluorine dating. Never fitted well with later finds (Dart's Taung child, 1924) and **delayed acceptance of African origins for human evolution by decades** |

**Pattern across the table: the speed of death tracks the cheapness of the
test, not the weight of the evidence.** N-rays died in a year because anyone
with a prism could check. Continental drift took fifty because nobody could see
the seafloor.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Michelson-Morley disproved the aether" | It produced a null result that was absorbed by an auxiliary hypothesis for eighteen years. Einstein's own account of its influence on him is disputed |
| "Semmelweis was ignored because doctors were arrogant" | Partly. Also: he waited fourteen years to publish, wrote abusively, and had no theory that made his data intelligible. **Both things are true** |
| "Wegener was right and everyone else was blind" | He was right about drift and wrong about mechanism. His critics' mechanical objections were correct. The field converted within about five years of a workable mechanism appearing |
| "Marshall drank the bacteria and everyone believed him" | The self-experiment is memorable and was not the decisive evidence. Randomised eradication trials were. And Lykoudis had done the therapeutic work twenty years earlier to no effect whatsoever |
| "Science advances one funeral at a time" | Half true, and the mechanism is wrong in the popular telling. Individual conversion is more common than assumed (Hull 1978); the funerals matter because they free up *positions and funding* (Azoulay 2019) |
| "Phlogiston was stupid" | It was a good unifying theory that organised combustion, calcination and respiration for eighty years. Judging superseded theories as obviously foolish is the single most common error in popular history of science |
| "Being persecuted means you might be Galileo" | Galileo was also, in the relevant sense, an establishment insider with powerful patrons. And the base rate of persecuted-and-right is very low. See below |

## The control group problem, stated plainly

This file lists correct ideas that were rejected. **That list exists because
they were eventually vindicated.** There is no comparable list of the confident
heterodox claims that were rejected and stayed rejected because they were
wrong — Velikovsky, Reich's orgone, Sheldrake's morphic resonance, and a very
long tail of names nobody wrote down.

**The base rate for "rejected outsider turns out to be right" is low.** How low
is unmeasurable, because the denominator is unrecorded. The honest position is
that **"they laughed at Galileo" carries no evidential weight in either
direction**, and any argument resting on it is doing rhetoric.

The closest thing to an ex-ante marker — weak, offered without confidence:

- Does the claim make **risky novel predictions**, or only re-explain known data?
- Does the proponent **engage the strongest counter-evidence**, or only weak versions?
- Does the proponent **accept the rest of the field's results**, or require everything to be wrong at once?
- Is there **an independent group** who could check it cheaply, and have they?

Wegener, Semmelweis and Marshall score well on all four. Most cranks fail the
third immediately.

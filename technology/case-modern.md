# Case: Three Modern Technologies Nobody Talks About

> **Status: TIER 1** on the basic facts of all three (dates, diffusion,
> geographic and demographic patterns). **TIER 2** on the magnitude of causal
> attribution in each case, which is where all the interesting disputes are.
> **TIER 2–3** on the political consequences of air conditioning.

**Written from general knowledge without live source verification.** The
Levinson cost figures, the Goldin & Katz effect sizes, and the Sun Belt
population numbers are the priority verification targets here.

The selection principle: three technologies with very large documented
second-order effects and very low share of public attention. **If your list of
important 20th-century technologies is "computers, antibiotics, nuclear," you
have missed the ones that restructured where people live, what work they do,
and who is in the labour force.**

# 1. The shipping container

**Marc Levinson, *The Box: How the Shipping Container Made the World Smaller
and the World Economy Bigger* (2006). If you read one book from this notebook,
read this one.**

## What happened

- **Malcom McLean** (a trucking operator, not a shipping man — note the
  spelling: one 'l') converted a tanker and sailed the ***Ideal-X*** from
  Newark to Houston on **26 April 1956** with 58 containers on deck.
- The innovation was not the box. Boxes existed. **The innovation was the
  system**: standard dimensions, corner castings, the twist-lock, purpose-built
  cranes, purpose-built ships, chassis, and matching rail cars — plus the
  institutional work of getting everyone to agree.
- **Keith Tantlinger designed the twist-lock corner fitting and McLean released
  the patent royalty-free.** This is the decisive move in the entire story and
  it is a pure standards play: giving away the interface to make the standard
  universal, then owning the operating business. Cross-ref
  power-and-technology.md.
- ISO standardisation ran roughly 1961–1970, settling on 20ft and 40ft lengths
  and the corner-casting geometry.
- **The Vietnam War was the accelerant.** McLean's Sea-Land won military
  contracts to supply Cam Ranh Bay, and — crucially — filled the empty
  backhaul with Japanese exports. The transpacific container trade was
  effectively subsidised into existence by US military logistics.

## What it did

**It made distance cheap.** Before containerisation, loading break-bulk cargo
was enormously labour-intensive: gangs manhandling barrels, sacks and crates
into ships' holds, item by item. Ships spent more time in port than at sea.

Levinson's often-quoted figures put the cost of loading loose cargo at
something like **$5.86 per ton versus roughly $0.16 per ton containerised**.
**Flag: these are widely repeated, load-bearing, and I have not verified
them.** Even discounted heavily, the order of magnitude is the point.

| Consequence | Detail | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| **Port geography rewritten** | New York's break-bulk piers died; Newark/Elizabeth rose. London's docks closed; Felixstowe and Rotterdam rose. Ports need deep water and land, not proximity to the city | 1 |
| **Dock labour destroyed** | Longshore employment fell by very large fractions in every containerised port. The ILWU's 1960 **Mechanization and Modernization Agreement** (Harry Bridges) traded job numbers for benefits and guaranteed income — an unusually competent union response, and it still meant the jobs went | 1 |
| **Manufacturing became footloose** | When shipping is nearly free relative to product value, the location decision is driven by labour cost, regulation and tax. **This is the precondition for offshoring.** Without cheap freight, the wage differential is not exploitable | 1–2 |
| **Just-in-time and global supply chains** | Reliable, schedulable, low-loss freight made multi-country production of a single object viable | 1–2 |
| **Trade volume** | Bernhofen, El-Beshir & Kneller (2016) estimated containerisation's effect on trade exceeded that of trade-liberalisation agreements over comparable periods | 2 |
| **Theft collapsed** | Pilferage from docks was endemic and priced into insurance. A sealed box ended it. Rarely mentioned, economically significant | 2 |
| **Fragility** | Efficient, low-inventory global chains are brittle. The 2020–2022 disruptions and the *Ever Given* (Suez, March 2021) are second-order costs of the same optimisation | 1 |

## Why this is the notebook's best case

**The container has no ideology, no charismatic founder narrative, no
manifesto, and no cultural presence — and it plausibly restructured the world
economy more than the internet did.** The political consequences are enormous:
the deindustrialisation of Western manufacturing regions, the rise of East
Asian export manufacturing, and a large share of the resulting politics, all
run partly through a steel box.

**The general lesson: the technologies with the largest second-order effects
are frequently the ones with the lowest narrative salience, because boring
infrastructure does not generate stories.** Ask what is boring and ubiquitous.

# 2. The contraceptive pill

## What happened

- FDA approved **Enovid** for contraception in **1960**.
- Legal access was restricted and staggered: **Griswold v. Connecticut (1965)**
  established a right for married couples; **Eisenstadt v. Baird (1972)**
  extended it to unmarried people.
- **The natural experiment:** between roughly 1969 and 1974, US states lowered
  the age of majority and passed "mature minor" laws at different times, giving
  young unmarried women legal access at different ages in different states.
  This staggered variation is what makes causal identification possible.

## Goldin and Katz

**Claudia Goldin & Lawrence Katz, "The Power of the Pill" (*Journal of
Political Economy*, 2002).** Exploiting the state-level variation, they found
that early legal access to the pill for young unmarried women was associated
with:

- **Higher enrolment in professional programmes** — law, medicine, dentistry,
  MBA.
- **Later age at first marriage.**
- **Higher subsequent labour force participation and earnings.**

**Martha Bailey (2006)** found parallel effects on labour supply.

## The mechanism, which is the interesting part

**It is not simply "fewer babies."** Other contraception existed. The specific
properties that mattered:

| Property | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **Reliable** | Reduced the *variance* of the fertility outcome, not just the mean |
| **Female-controlled** | Decoupled the decision from negotiation with a partner |
| **Decoupled from the sexual act** | Allowed planning rather than in-the-moment decisions |

Together these made a **long-horizon investment decision safe**. Medical school
is a seven-year commitment. Under high fertility uncertainty, the expected
return on that investment for a woman was heavily discounted — not because of
any belief about capability but because of a real probability of interruption.
**Reduce the interruption risk and the investment becomes rational.** Goldin's
further argument is that the pill also raised the age at marriage, which
thickened the marriage market for career-oriented women who delayed — a
*social* second-order effect operating through everyone else's timing.

The observed magnitudes are large: US women's share of entrants to law and
medical schools moved from single-digit or low percentages around 1970 to
roughly a third or more within about a decade; median age at first marriage for
US women rose from around 20.8 in 1960 to roughly 23 by 1980. **Verify these.**

## The honest caveats

**This is a Tier 2 attribution, not Tier 1, and the file would be dishonest
without this section.**

- **Massive confounding.** The same period contains the women's movement, Title
  VII (1964) and Title IX (1972), no-fault divorce spreading from 1969,
  *Roe v. Wade* (1973), a shift in labour demand toward services and
  cognitive work, and rising female college attendance already underway.
  Disentangling these is genuinely hard.
- **Identification is contested.** Caitlin Myers (2017) argues that variation in
  abortion access explains more of the outcomes attributed to the pill in some
  specifications, and that the legal-access coding used in the original work is
  disputable.
- **Direction of causation on demand.** The pill was developed because Margaret
  Sanger and Katharine McCormick funded Gregory Pincus to build it. **The
  demand preceded the technology.** A society that did not want this would not
  have built it — cross-ref how-technology-changes-society.md.
- **Different countries, different outcomes.** The same pill in Ireland
  (contraception restricted until 1980, fully liberalised 1985), Japan (the
  combined pill was not approved until **1999**, roughly forty years late,
  while Viagra was approved in months — a fact worth sitting with), and Sweden
  produced very different trajectories. **Same technology, different society,
  different result.** Japan is the cleanest available demonstration in this
  notebook that a society can simply decline a technology for decades.

# 3. Air conditioning

## What happened

- **Willis Carrier, 1902**, Sackett-Wilhelms lithographic plant, Brooklyn.
  **The purpose was humidity control to keep paper dimensionally stable for
  colour printing.** Human comfort was not the goal. (Note the loop back to
  case-printing.md: the technology that reorganised the American South was
  invented to fix a printing problem.)
- Commercial and cinema use in the 1920s–30s; **window units from the late
  1940s and 1950s** made residential diffusion possible.
- Diffusion in US housing went from negligible in 1950 to near-universal in the
  South within a few decades.

## Documented effects

| Effect | Detail | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| **Heat mortality collapsed** | Barreca, Clay, Deschênes, Greenstone & Shapiro (2016) found the mortality effect of hot days in the US fell dramatically over the 20th century and attributed the decline substantially to residential AC. **This is the best-identified result in the file and it is a very large humanitarian number** | 1 |
| **Sun Belt population growth** | Florida roughly 2.8m (1950) to over 21m today; Arizona roughly 750k to over 7m; Texas and Nevada similarly. **AC is one of several causes** — also interstate highways, air travel, defence spending, cheap land, and Social Security making retirement migration viable | 1 on the growth, 2 on AC's share |
| **Political consequence** | US House seats and Electoral College votes reapportion with population. Decades of Rust Belt → Sun Belt transfer. **The partisan composition of the US Congress and Senate is materially different because hot places became habitable** | 2 |
| **Productivity in heat** | Substantial literature (Hsiang; Graff Zivin & Neidell; Somanathan et al. on Indian manufacturing) showing output falls measurably on hot days in non-cooled settings. Cooling recovers it | 1–2 |
| **Architecture died** | Sealed glass boxes replaced courtyards, thick masonry, high ceilings, deep verandas, shutters, cross-ventilation and orientation-to-prevailing-wind. **Climate-adaptive vernacular architecture was abandoned across the hot world within about two generations** | 1 |
| **Singapore** | Lee Kuan Yew repeatedly named air conditioning as the most important invention for tropical development and made it a public-sector priority. Cross-ref `leaders/`. Whether the causal claim is right, the *belief* shaped policy | 2 |

## The second-order effects that matter most

1. **The technology made itself necessary.** Once buildings are designed
   assuming AC — sealed, glazed, deep-plan, no thermal mass — they are
   *uninhabitable without it*. A power failure in a modern Phoenix or Gulf
   tower block during a heatwave is a mass-casualty event in a way that a
   power failure in a 1900 courtyard house was not. **This is a general and
   under-appreciated pattern: adaptation to a technology destroys the fallback,
   converting a convenience into a life-support dependency.** Cross-ref
   `risk/risk-management.md` on redundancy.
2. **It is a warming loop.** AC consumes large and growing quantities of
   electricity, ejects heat into the street (worsening urban heat islands), and
   historically used refrigerants that were first ozone-destroying and then
   potent greenhouse gases. Demand for cooling rises with temperature. **The
   adaptation increases the problem it adapts to** — Ellul's self-augmentation,
   with a physical mechanism.
3. **The distribution is inverted.** AC penetration is lowest where wet-bulb
   temperatures are most dangerous — South Asia, the Sahel, parts of Southeast
   Asia. **The technology that made hot regions habitable is least available in
   the hottest ones.** This is arguably the largest single equity issue in
   climate adaptation and it is a story about a 1902 humidity controller.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular story | Correction |
|---|---|
| **"The internet made the world flat"** | The container did far more of that work, twenty years earlier, and the internet did *not* make geography irrelevant — agglomeration in superstar cities intensified. See predicting-technology.md |
| **"Globalisation was caused by trade agreements"** | Freight cost collapse plausibly did more. Policy and technology are complements here, and the technology is systematically under-credited |
| **"The pill liberated women"** | Too simple in both directions. It made a long-horizon investment safe, which mattered enormously — but it arrived into a movement that had demanded it, alongside legal changes doing independent work. Japan's forty-year delay shows the technology alone decides nothing |
| **"Air conditioning is a comfort technology"** | It is a mortality technology, a labour-productivity technology, a demographic technology and, in the US, an electoral technology |
| **"Adaptation solves the problem"** | AC is the counterexample. It solved heat locally, increased heat globally, destroyed the passive alternatives, and is least available where most needed |
| **"McLean got rich from the container"** | He built and lost fortunes; his later venture (United States Lines) went bankrupt in 1986. The value went to the shippers, the retailers, the exporting economies, and ultimately consumers. **The inventor rarely captures the surplus** |

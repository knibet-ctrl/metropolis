# Case: The Printing Press

> **Status: TIER 1** that printing spread rapidly in Europe after c. 1450 and
> that the Reformation's diffusion depended on it. **TIER 2** on Eisenstein's
> specific causal mechanisms (fixity, standardisation, cumulative correction)
> versus Johns's critique. **TIER 1** on the comparative fact that China and
> Korea printed earlier at scale with different consequences — **this is the
> single strongest argument against technological determinism in the entire
> notebook.**

**Written from general knowledge without live source verification.** Incunabula
counts, print-run figures and Reformation pamphlet numbers are from memory and
are the kind of figures that get inflated in retelling. Treat every number here
as an order-of-magnitude claim.

---

## What actually happened, minimally

- Johannes Gutenberg, Mainz, c. 1450s. The 42-line Bible c. 1455. His
  contribution was not "printing" but a **system**: a hand mould for casting
  interchangeable metal type quickly and to consistent height, an oil-based ink
  that adhered to metal, and an adapted screw press.
- Diffusion was fast by any pre-modern standard. Presses in dozens of European
  cities within twenty-five years; Venice became the major centre. Surviving
  *incunabula* (printed before 1501) number roughly 28,000–30,000 distinct
  editions — **the total copy count is usually estimated in the range of eight
  to twenty million and this is a genuinely uncertain figure.**
- Gutenberg himself went bankrupt, losing the business to his financier Johann
  Fust in a 1455 lawsuit. **The inventor did not capture the value. This is the
  rule, not the exception** — cross-ref power-and-technology.md.

## Eisenstein's argument

**Elizabeth Eisenstein, *The Printing Press as an Agent of Change* (1979).**
The book that made this a field. Her claim is not "printing spread knowledge" —
she is sharper than that. She identifies specific properties of print culture
that manuscript culture lacked:

| Property | What manuscript culture couldn't do | What print enabled |
|---|---|---|
| **Fixity** | Every copy drifted; texts degraded with each copying | Thousands of identical copies; a text could be *referred to* precisely |
| **Dissemination** | Copies were scarce and localised | Wide, fast, simultaneous |
| **Standardisation** | Page layout, pagination, indexes varied per copy | Page numbers, indexes, tables of contents became meaningful and shareable |
| **Cumulative correction** | Corrections did not propagate; error accumulated | Errata, revised editions, and reader feedback that improved successive printings |
| **Data preservation** | Astronomical tables and diagrams corrupted badly under copying | Tables, star charts and anatomical figures could be reproduced exactly |
| **Cross-referencing** | Comparing texts required physically gathering rare manuscripts | A scholar could hold contradictory authorities open on one desk |

**Her strongest single claim is about images and tables, and it is the least
disputed.** Verbal text degrades gracefully under manuscript copying; a
scribe's errors are usually detectable. **Diagrams do not.** An anatomical
illustration or a star position copied by hand five times is worthless.
Vesalius's *De humani corporis fabrica* (1543) and Copernicus's *De
revolutionibus* (1543) are unthinkable as manuscripts. This is the point where
printing's contribution to science is hardest to argue away.

Her second-order claim is the interesting one: **the juxtaposition of
contradictory authorities in one place created the sense of a "problem" to be
resolved.** A scholar in 1300 who encountered one authority accepted it. A
scholar in 1550 could have Ptolemy, Aristotle, an Arabic commentary and a new
observation on the same table, and the contradictions became visible,
intolerable and productive. Cross-ref `knowledge/what-is-science.md`.

## Johns's critique

**Adrian Johns, *The Nature of the Book* (1998).** The most serious challenge,
and it is not a quibble.

Johns's argument: **fixity was not an intrinsic property of print. It was a
social achievement, contested, incomplete, and it took two centuries.**

Evidence he marshals:
- Early modern printing was chaotic. Piracy was endemic. Unauthorised, garbled
  and outright fabricated editions circulated freely. Authors regularly
  disowned printed versions of their own work.
- Readers of the period did **not** treat print as reliable. They were often
  more suspicious of a printed book than of a manuscript from a known hand,
  because they had no way to verify provenance.
- Fixity had to be *manufactured* through institutions: the Stationers'
  Company in London, licensing, guild registration, the Royal Society's
  practices of registering and attesting, the reputational economy of
  particular printing houses.

The Eisenstein–Johns exchange in the *American Historical Review* (2002) is the
canonical statement of both sides.

**Where I think the balance sits — flagged as my judgement, not consensus:**
Johns is right that fixity was constructed and that Eisenstein treats print as
an agent rather than a tool. Eisenstein is right that print made fixity
*achievable at all* — you cannot build a Stationers' Company around
hand-copying, because the underlying operation does not produce identical
objects. **Print supplied a necessary affordance; institutions converted it
into a reliable property.** That is the general shape of nearly every case in
this notebook.

## The Reformation as the first mass-media event

Luther posted or circulated the 95 Theses in 1517. What followed is the best
documented instance of a technology amplifying a pre-existing conflict.

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Volume | Luther was the most-published author of the era. German-language pamphlet (*Flugschriften*) output rose by roughly an order of magnitude in the early 1520s |
| Format innovation | Short, cheap quarto pamphlets — 8 to 16 pages, readable in an hour, affordable to an artisan. **The format mattered as much as the press.** Andrew Pettegree's *Brand Luther* (2015) argues Luther effectively invented the mass-market pamphlet |
| Language | German vernacular, deliberately. His Bible translation (New Testament 1522, complete 1534) is a founding text of standard modern German |
| Visual | Woodcuts by Cranach's workshop — crucial for the illiterate majority. The propaganda was substantially pictorial |
| Reach past literacy | Pamphlets were read aloud in taverns, workshops and markets. **Print reached far past the reading population** |
| Branding | A recognisable typographic style and the Luther Rose. Wittenberg became a printing town on the back of it |

**The counterfactual test that makes this convincing:** Jan Hus made
substantially similar theological arguments a century earlier (burned 1415).
Hussitism became a regional Bohemian movement and stayed there. Wycliffe
similarly. **Luther's ideas were not more correct or more popular; they were
more copyable.**

**But note what the press did not do.** It did not decide the outcome. Catholic
authorities printed too, and printed a great deal. The Reformation's political
result was settled by the Schmalkaldic War, the Peace of Augsburg (1555,
*cuius regio, eius religio*), and eventually the Thirty Years' War — that is,
by princes and armies. **Print determined the speed and reach of the schism,
not its resolution.** The technology set the tempo; power set the outcome.

## The counter-case: China and Korea

**This is the most important section in the file. Read it before accepting any
determinist story anywhere.**

| | China | Korea | Europe |
|---|---|---|---|
| Woodblock printing | From the Tang; the Diamond Sutra of **868 CE** is the earliest dated printed book | Also early, from China | Late 14th c. for block books |
| Movable type | **Bi Sheng, ceramic, c. 1040** (recorded by Shen Kuo). Later wooden and metal | **Metal movable type by the 13th c.; the *Jikji* was printed at Heungdeok temple, Cheongju, in 1377** — roughly 78 years before Gutenberg | Gutenberg, c. 1450s |
| Scale of print industry | **Very large.** Ming and Qing commercial publishing was a major industry — examination texts, medical and agricultural manuals, popular fiction, gazetteers | State-controlled, prestige-oriented, limited commercial sector | Rapidly commercial from the start |
| Political structure | Unified empire, single orthodoxy, capable censorship | Centralised Joseon state; printing largely a royal monopoly | **Fragmented — hundreds of polities. A banned book crossed a border in a day** |
| Script | Thousands of characters — movable type is genuinely less economical than woodblock | Hangul existed from 1443 but literati wrote in Chinese characters | ~26 letters. Movable type is overwhelmingly efficient |
| Religion | No single institution monopolising salvation, hence nothing to schism from in the European sense | Confucian orthodoxy | **A single universal church with a mass constituency and an available doctrinal grievance** |

### What this case actually proves, stated precisely

**Not** "China had printing and nothing happened." That is a false and slightly
racist commonplace. Chinese print culture was enormous and consequential: it
underpinned the examination system, standardised a canon across a continent,
produced a large popular fiction market, and disseminated agricultural and
medical technique widely. It did a great deal.

**What it proves is narrower and more damaging to determinism:**

> **The same core capability — cheap reproduction of text — produced a
> Reformation, a scientific communication network and a vernacular print
> market in Europe, and produced a consolidated imperial orthodoxy and a
> commercial fiction market in China. The technology did not select the
> outcome. The pre-existing political, religious and linguistic structure
> did.**

**Three further points, all of which cut against lazy versions of the story:**

1. **The alphabet matters, and this is a material constraint, not a social
   one.** Movable type's economics depend on character-set size. Woodblock was
   the *rational* choice for Chinese, not a failure to innovate. Determinism
   fails, but materialism does not — the material facts pushed the two
   traditions toward different techniques.
2. **Korea is the sharper case than China**, because Korea had *metal movable
   type*, the exact Gutenberg technique, decades earlier — and it stayed a
   state and elite instrument. **Same technique, different institutions,
   different outcome.** There is no confound about character sets that can
   rescue determinism here, because the technique was identical.
3. **The Ottoman comparison is usually told wrong.** The claim that the
   Ottomans "banned printing until 1727" is misleading: Jewish, Armenian and
   Greek presses operated in Ottoman territory from the late 15th century. The
   restriction was specifically on printing in Arabic script, and its motives
   (scribal guild interests, the status of the Quranic text, calligraphic
   norms) are debated. **Do not use this as a simple example of religious
   obscurantism blocking technology. It is more interesting than that.**

## What printing actually did — the honest list

Sorted by confidence:

| Effect | Tier | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Made identical copies cheap | 1 | The only truly first-order effect |
| Enabled reliable reproduction of diagrams, tables and numerical data | 1 | The strongest scientific channel |
| Vastly accelerated the Reformation's diffusion | 1 | The Hus counterfactual is convincing |
| Standardised vernacular languages | 1–2 | Luther's German, the King James Bible, Italian via Tuscan. Printers picked a dialect and froze it |
| Created the author as a legal and commercial category | 2 | Leads to copyright — Statute of Anne, 1710 |
| Enabled cumulative correction across editions | 2 | Eisenstein's claim; Johns disputes the mechanism |
| Increased literacy | 2 | Direction certain, causal ordering contested — Protestantism's *demand* for lay reading is arguably the bigger driver, and that runs the causation the other way |
| Caused the Scientific Revolution | 2–3 | Necessary condition at most. Contested |
| Undermined the authority of oral and manuscript tradition | 2 | Also destroyed the classical arts of memory |
| Caused the rise of the nation-state via print-vernaculars | 3 | Benedict Anderson's *Imagined Communities* (1983) "print-capitalism" argument. Elegant, influential, weakly evidenced as a causal claim |

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular story | Correction |
|---|---|
| **"Gutenberg invented printing"** | He invented an economical metal-type *system* for alphabetic scripts. Printing was ~600 years old and metal movable type ~80 years old |
| **"Printing democratised knowledge"** | In the short run it **concentrated** it. Presses were capital-intensive; printers were licensed; censorship regimes (the Index, 1559; English licensing) were built specifically in response. Broad access took centuries |
| **"Printing spread ideas, therefore progress"** | It spread whatever was popular. The *Malleus Maleficarum* (1487) was a print bestseller and the European witch trials — roughly 40,000–60,000 executions, peaking c. 1560–1630 — were a print-era phenomenon, not a medieval one. **Printing amplified the witch panic as efficiently as it amplified Luther** |
| **"The 95 Theses went viral because Luther nailed them to a door"** | The door story is late and possibly apocryphal (Melanchthon reports it after Luther's death). The diffusion mechanism was the printers of Leipzig, Nuremberg and Basel reprinting without permission |
| **"China invented printing but didn't do anything with it"** | Factually false. Chinese print output was vast. What China lacked was a Reformation to have, not a press to have it with |
| **"Print culture had fixity"** | Contested. Johns argues fixity was an institutional achievement, not a property of the technology |
| **"More information makes people better informed"** | The witch-hunt point again, and cross-ref `knowledge/` and `existence/attention.md`. **Reproduction cost falling does not select for truth.** It selects for reproducibility |

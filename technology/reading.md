# Reading

> **Status: TIER 2 on the recommendations themselves** — this is a judgement
> list, not a canon. **TIER 1 that the correctives section matters more than
> the main list**, because most of the widely read books in this field are
> more confident than the evidence supports.

**Written from general knowledge without live source verification.** Titles,
authors and years are from memory. Check publication details before citing.
Where I am unsure of a year I have said so rather than guessing precisely.

**How to use this list: read one primary case study and one corrective from the
same subtopic. Reading only the famous book in any of these areas will leave
you confidently wrong.**

## Start here — if you read four things

| Book | Why |
|---|---|
| **Marc Levinson, *The Box* (2006)** | The best single demonstration of a second-order effect in the language. A steel box, no ideology, and it restructured world manufacturing. It also teaches the standards-and-value-capture lesson without ever announcing it |
| **David Edgerton, *The Shock of the Old* (2006)** | **The most important corrective in the notebook.** Argues that technology history is written about *innovation* when it should be written about *use* — and that the technologies that mattered most in the 20th century were old ones, widely used, not new ones. Rickshaws, corrugated iron, the bicycle, horses in WWII |
| **Robert Allen, *The British Industrial Revolution in Global Perspective* (2009)** | The most serious economic explanation of the most-studied case, and a model of how to make a technological argument quantitative |
| **E.P. Thompson, *The Making of the English Working Class* (1963)** | The cost side, written from below, by someone who read what the losers actually wrote. The Luddite chapters cure the caricature permanently |

## Theory and frameworks

| Source | Why |
|---|---|
| **Langdon Winner, "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" (1980)**; *The Whale and the Reactor* (1986) | The founding statement that design choices settle political questions. Read it *with* the Joerges critique below — the Moses bridges example is the weakest part |
| **Bijker, Hughes & Pinch (eds.), *The Social Construction of Technological Systems* (1987)** | SCOT's canonical statement. The bicycle case dissolves "the better design obviously won" reasoning permanently |
| **Thomas Hughes, *Networks of Power* (1983)** | Electrification across three countries. The source of "technological momentum" — young systems are malleable, mature ones behave as if deterministic. **The most defensible position in the whole theoretical debate** |
| **Lewis Mumford, *Technics and Civilization* (1934)**; "Authoritarian and Democratic Technics" (1964) | The megamachine idea — that the first great machine was made of people — is genuinely original and relocates the question from artefacts to organisation |
| **Jacques Ellul, *The Technological Society* (1954/1964)** | Unfalsifiable and frequently right about the pattern. Read for the self-augmentation argument: every technical fix generates the next problem |
| **Bruno Latour, *Science in Action* (1987)** | ANT at its most readable. Take the method, leave the metaphysics |
| **James C. Scott, *Seeing Like a State* (1998)** | Not shelved as technology, but the best book on why states need legibility and what standardisation destroys. Essential for power-and-technology.md |
| **Lynn White Jr., *Medieval Technology and Social Change* (1962)** | Read as the **clearest statement of a position that was subsequently demolished**. The stirrup thesis is a masterclass in how a beautiful causal chain survives its own refutation |

## Printing

| Source | Why |
|---|---|
| **Elizabeth Eisenstein, *The Printing Press as an Agent of Change* (1979)** | The book that created the field. The argument about diagrams and tables — that images degrade catastrophically under hand copying — is the strongest and least disputed part |
| **Adrian Johns, *The Nature of the Book* (1998)** | **The essential corrective.** Fixity was not a property of print; it was an institutional achievement built by the Stationers' Company and the reputational economy of printers, over two centuries |
| **The Eisenstein–Johns exchange, *American Historical Review* (2002)** | Both positions at full strength in one place. Faster than reading both books |
| **Andrew Pettegree, *Brand Luther* (2015)** | The Reformation as a media and *format* story. Luther effectively invented the cheap mass-market pamphlet, and Wittenberg became a print town on the back of it |
| **Benedict Anderson, *Imagined Communities* (1983)** | "Print-capitalism" and national consciousness. Enormously influential; hold the causal claim loosely |
| **Anything reliable on Chinese and Korean print history** | I am not confident enough in specific titles to recommend one, and this is the largest gap in my own knowledge. **The China/Korea comparison is the load-bearing argument in case-printing.md, and I have sourced it from general knowledge rather than from specialist literature.** This is where the notebook is most likely to be wrong |

## The Industrial Revolution

| Source | Why |
|---|---|
| **Robert Allen (2009)**, as above | The high-wage-economy explanation |
| **Judy Stephenson's papers on 18th-century construction wages** | **The critique that most damages Allen.** The wage series are contractor day-rates including overhead, not worker earnings |
| **Jane Humphries, *Childhood and Child Labour in the British Industrial Revolution* (2010)** | Family income, women's and children's work, and why the "high wage" story looks different when you count the whole household |
| **E.A. Wrigley, *Energy and the English Industrial Revolution* (2010)** | The organic-economy / mineral-economy distinction. The clearest statement of the energy transition as the core event |
| **Kenneth Pomeranz, *The Great Divergence* (2000)** | Coal location and New World "ghost acreage." Read with Broadberry's GDP reconstructions arguing for much earlier divergence |
| **Joel Mokyr, *The Enlightened Economy* (2009); *A Culture of Growth* (2016)** | The Industrial Enlightenment — useful knowledge circulating and being tested. The best version of the ideas-based explanation |
| **Deirdre McCloskey, *Bourgeois Dignity* (2010)** | The explicitly anti-materialist explanation: what changed was how commerce was *talked about*. Hard to test; worth steelmanning |
| **Charles Feinstein (1998) on real wages** | The pessimist revision that largely displaced Lindert & Williamson. **The load-bearing citation for the standard-of-living section** |
| **Kevin Binfield (ed.), *Writings of the Luddites*** | Their own documents. The fastest available cure for the caricature |
| **Eric Hobsbawm, "The Machine Breakers" (1952)** | "Collective bargaining by riot." Short, and reframes the entire episode |

## Modern cases

| Source | Why |
|---|---|
| **Marc Levinson, *The Box* (2006)** | As above. Also excellent on the port labour negotiations, which are usually skipped |
| **Claudia Goldin & Lawrence Katz, "The Power of the Pill" (*JPE*, 2002)** | The natural experiment and the identification strategy. Read the paper, not the summaries |
| **Martha Bailey (2006)** on the pill and female labour supply | The complement to Goldin & Katz |
| **Caitlin Myers (2017)** on abortion access vs contraception access | The corrective. Argues abortion access does more of the work than the pill in some specifications |
| **Barreca, Clay, Deschênes, Greenstone & Shapiro (2016)** on temperature and mortality | The best-identified result on air conditioning, and a very large humanitarian number |
| **Vaclav Smil, *Energy Transitions* (2010/2017)**; *Energy and Civilization* (2017) | The definitive statement that energy transitions are slow and enormous. **Note that recent solar and battery deployment has partly falsified the strong version of his rate prior** — read him with that flag |

## Unintended consequences

| Source | Why |
|---|---|
| **William Stanley Jevons, *The Coal Question* (1865)** | The original. Chapter 7 is the one everyone cites and few read |
| **Steve Sorrell / UKERC, *The Rebound Effect* (2007)** | The systematic review that gives the 10–30% direct-rebound figure |
| **Duranton & Turner, "The Fundamental Law of Road Congestion" (*AER*, 2011)** | The cleanest identification in the file. Elasticity ≈ 1.0 |
| **Autor, Levy & Murnane (2003)**; **Autor & Dorn (2013)** | The task framework and job polarisation. The single most useful frame for automation |
| **Acemoglu & Restrepo (2020)** on robots, with **Graetz & Michaels (2018)** | Read both. They disagree and both are competent. This is what a genuinely open empirical question looks like |
| **Sam Peltzman (1975)**, with **Crandall & Graham (1984)** | The risk-compensation claim and the correction that found partial rather than full offset |
| **Higney, Hanley & Moro (2022)** meta-analysis of lead and crime | **The corrective.** Strong publication bias; corrected effects much smaller than headline claims |
| **Charles Perrow, *Normal Accidents* (1984)** | Not cited in the main files but should have been. Tight coupling plus interactive complexity makes accidents a *system property* rather than an operator failure. Cross-ref `risk/` |
| **Sellen & Harper, *The Myth of the Paperless Office* (2002)** | Why paper consumption rose after email. A study in why the obvious substitution didn't happen |

## Power, standards and forecasting

| Source | Why |
|---|---|
| **Robert Caro, *The Power Broker* (1974)** | Infrastructure as a weapon, at 1,200 pages. Read it knowing that the specific bridge-height claim rests on one interview |
| **Bernward Joerges, "Do Politics Have Artefacts?" (1999)** | The demolition of the Moses bridges story. Short and essential |
| **Petra Moser's work on 19th-century World's Fairs and patents** | The finding that patent-free countries innovated comparably but **specialised differently** is the most interesting result in the patent literature |
| **Boldrin & Levine, *Against Intellectual Monopoly* (2008)** | The strong anti-patent case. Read with **Selgin & Turner (2011)**, who dismantle their Watt-patent claim specifically |
| **Cohen, Nelson & Walsh (2000)**, the Carnegie Mellon survey | The solid finding: appropriability mechanisms are industry-specific and patents rank low outside pharma and chemicals |
| **Paul David, "The Dynamo and the Computer" (*AER*, 1990)** | **The most useful eight pages in the notebook.** The forty-year lag and why the reorganisation was the innovation |
| **Paul David, "Clio and the Economics of QWERTY" (1985)**, with **Liebowitz & Margolis, "The Fable of the Keys" (1990)** | **Read as a pair, in that order.** The founding text of path dependence and its demolition. The best available lesson in checking the flagship example |
| **Everett Rogers, *Diffusion of Innovations* (1962)** | The S-curve and adopter categories. Old, still the reference |
| **Griliches (1957)** on hybrid corn | Adoption tracks profitability, not information. Cures a great deal of condescension about resistance to technology |
| **Farmer & Lafond (2016)** on technology cost forecasting | Wright's law beats time-based extrapolation out of sample across 50+ technologies. **The one genuinely predictive result in the field** |
| **Philip Tetlock, *Expert Political Judgment* (2005)** | Cross-ref `risk/forecasting.md`. Applies in full here |

## Computing and AI

| Source | Why |
|---|---|
| **Robert Gordon, *The Rise and Fall of American Growth* (2016)** | The strongest pessimist case: 1870–1970 was the exceptional century and IT does not compare. Long, repetitive, and correct about more than its critics allow |
| **Erik Brynjolfsson & Andrew McAfee, *The Second Machine Age* (2014)** | The optimist case, stated well. Read against Gordon |
| **Chad Syverson (2017)** on mismeasurement and the productivity slowdown | **The result that kills the "GDP just misses free digital goods" argument.** Under-cited and important |
| **Brynjolfsson, Rock & Syverson, "The Productivity J-Curve" (2021)** | The best pro-optimist mechanism: intangible investment during a GPT build-out understates measured productivity early |
| **Herbert Simon (1971)** on attention and information | The origin of the attention-economy insight, decades before anyone monetised it. Cross-ref `existence/attention.md` |
| **Boxell, Gentzkow & Shapiro (2017)**; **the 2023 Meta election studies** | The evidence against the strong internet-causes-polarisation story. **If you read one thing from this section, read these** |
| **Jonathan Haidt, *The Anxious Generation* (2024)**, with **Candice Odgers's *Nature* review (2024)** and **Orben & Przybylski (2019)** | Read as a set. This is what an unresolved question looks like when both sides are articulate and neither has the evidence their confidence implies |
| **Noy & Zhang (2023); Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond (2023); Dell'Acqua et al. (2023)** | The AI productivity RCTs. Read the methods sections — the tasks are narrower than the headlines |
| **Daron Acemoglu (2024)** on AI's macroeconomic effects | The most careful sceptical estimate. Note it is still an extrapolation from assumptions |

## Where I am least confident about this list

Stated plainly, because a reading list that does not disclose its gaps is a
liability:

- **Chinese and Korean print history.** I have not read specialist work and the
  China/Korea comparison carries more weight in this notebook than any other
  single argument. **If one thing here is wrong, it is most likely to be this.**
- **The AI literature is moving faster than any list can track.** Everything in
  that section will be stale quickly and some of it may already be superseded.
- **Non-Western technology history generally.** The notebook is heavily
  Anglo-European. Islamic, Indian, African and pre-Columbian technological
  history are almost absent, which is a real distortion and not a neutral one.
- **Military technology** is absent. Tilly's "war made the state and the state
  made war," gunpowder and the fiscal-military state, and nuclear weapons as a
  political technology all belong here and are missing.
- **Medicine and public health** are absent. Sanitation, vaccination and
  antibiotics arguably changed human life more than anything in this notebook,
  and the demographic transition is the largest second-order effect in history.

===============================================================================
WHAT THIS IS
===============================================================================
An honest map of what is actually known about how technologies change the
societies that adopt them — and, more usefully, how badly the standard stories
about that get told.

  What did the printing press actually do, given that China had printing
  four centuries earlier? Why did living standards stagnate for forty years
  after the Industrial Revolution began? Why did the shipping container
  reshape the world economy while almost nobody noticed? Why does efficiency
  so often increase consumption? Who captures the value of a new tool, and
  why is it so rarely the inventor or the user?

Written in the same discipline as leaders/, existence/, knowledge/ and risk/:
positions given fairly, disputes flagged as disputes, confidence marked, and a
standing refusal to pretend something is settled when it isn't.

**No live sources were used. This was written from general knowledge with no
verification against primary documents.** Names, dates and figures are given
because vague claims are unfalsifiable and therefore useless — but every
specific number here should be checked before you cite it. Several are from
memory and may be off. Where a figure is load-bearing, I say so.

===============================================================================
THE ONE IDEA THAT ORGANISES EVERYTHING
===============================================================================
**TECHNOLOGICAL DETERMINISM IS A TRAP, AND IT IS THE TRAP THIS ENTIRE FIELD
FALLS INTO.**

The seductive sentence is "technology X caused social change Y." It is almost
always wrong, and it is wrong in a specific, diagnosable way:

  1. Technologies ENABLE and CONSTRAIN. They do not dictate. They change the
     cost of doing things. What gets done depends on who wanted what already.

  2. The SAME technology produces DIFFERENT outcomes in different societies.
     This is not a caveat. It is the central empirical finding of the field,
     and printing in China versus printing in Europe is the proof.

  3. Outcomes depend on PRE-EXISTING SOCIAL STRUCTURE — property rights,
     literacy, political fragmentation, labour markets, religion, who is
     allowed to own what.

  4. But the opposite error is equally wrong. Pure social constructionism —
     "technology is just whatever society makes of it" — cannot explain why
     nobody built a functioning aeroplane in 1200, or why coal-fired steam
     had to happen where the coal was. **Materials, thermodynamics and cost
     curves are real constraints, and they are not negotiable.**

The defensible position is narrow and unglamorous: **technologies reshape the
menu of cheap actions; societies choose from the menu according to their
existing structure of power and belief; and the results are systematically
different from what anyone intended.** Everything in this notebook is an
elaboration of that sentence.

**Corollary you should hold onto:** the first-order effect of a technology is
the one it was designed for, and it is the least interesting thing about it,
because it is the only one anyone planned for.

TEN HEADLINE FINDINGS, COMPRESSED
===============================================================================

 1. **The same technology, different society, different outcome.** Korea had
    metal movable type by 1377 (the *Jikji*), China had commercial printing on
    an industrial scale for centuries before Gutenberg. Neither produced a
    Reformation. Europe did — because Europe had a religious schism with a
    mass constituency, a fragmented political order with no single censor, and
    vernacular alphabetic languages. **The press was necessary and nowhere
    near sufficient.**

 2. **Efficiency increases consumption more often than it reduces it.** Jevons
    saw it in coal in 1865. It is documented for lighting (price fell by
    orders of magnitude, consumption rose by more), for roads (Duranton &
    Turner 2011: elasticity of driving to road capacity ≈ 1.0), and probably
    for computing. Rebound is Tier 1; its size is Tier 2.

 3. **Growth arrived roughly forty years before wages did.** Allen's "Engels'
    pause": British output per worker rose from about 1800, real wages barely
    moved until the 1840s, and the profit share rose. **The people who were
    made worse off by industrialisation were not confused.**

 4. **The Luddites were not anti-technology.** They were skilled workers
    attacking specific machines used by specific employers to break wage
    rates, apprenticeship rules and product standards, after Parliament
    repealed the laws they had been appealing to. They lost. Handloom weavers'
    wages fell roughly 70-80% in a generation. **"Luddite" as an insult is a
    historical error doing political work.**

 5. **The shipping container probably restructured the world economy more
    than the internet did, and it has no ideology attached to it.** Malcom
    McLean, *Ideal-X*, 26 April 1956. Cost of loading cargo fell by more than
    an order of magnitude; ports moved; dock unions were destroyed;
    manufacturing became footloose. Almost nobody lists it among the
    century's important technologies.

 6. **The internet-causes-polarisation story is in serious empirical
    trouble.** Boxell, Gentzkow & Shapiro (2017) found US polarisation rose
    fastest among the demographics *least* online. The 2023 Meta election
    studies found three months of algorithm changes had approximately no
    effect. Echo chambers exist; their causal weight is much smaller than the
    discourse assumes.

 7. **Adoption is not a function of quality.** VHS beat Betamax on the
    attribute buyers were actually choosing (recording length, licensing).
    Google Glass died of social rejection, not capability. Nuclear power
    stalled almost everywhere while France built 56 reactors in 15 years.
    **The technical question is rarely the binding constraint.**

 8. **QWERTY is a double lesson.** It is the canonical path-dependence
    example — and Liebowitz & Margolis (1990) showed the evidence for Dvorak's
    superiority traces largely to Dvorak's own studies. So: the flagship case
    for lock-in is weak, *and* lock-in is nonetheless real (railway gauge,
    mains voltage, US customary units). Be suspicious of your favourite
    example, not of the phenomenon.

 9. **Whoever sets the protocol captures value for centuries.** Qin Shi Huang
    standardised the script, weights, coinage and axle widths in 221 BCE and
    China is still shaped by it. TCP/IP beat OSI. ISO container dimensions
    beat everyone's proprietary boxes because Tantlinger's twist-lock patent
    was released royalty-free. Standards are the most under-rated form of
    power in this notebook. Cross-ref `leaders/`.

10. **Surveillance capability outlives the government that builds it.** The
    Dutch population registers were built for benign administrative purposes
    and recorded religion. The Netherlands had among the highest Jewish death
    rates in occupied Western Europe. **Every dataset is eventually inherited
    by someone you did not vet.**

===============================================================================
HONEST CAVEATS
===============================================================================
- **Nothing here was source-verified.** Dates and named people are more
  reliable than percentages; percentages are more reliable than decimal places.
- **Historians dispute nearly every single-cause technology story in this
  notebook.** Where I give a mechanism, assume there are three rival
  mechanisms with respectable defenders, and check.
- **Survivorship bias is severe.** We study technologies that were adopted, in
  societies that left records, and then generalise about "technology." See
  the-control-group.md, which exists to undermine the rest.
- **The AI file is the least reliable in the notebook and marks itself as
  such.** It is being written inside the event it is describing.
- **I have deliberately not resolved several live debates** (patents, risk
  compensation, lead and crime, smartphones and adolescent mental health).
  Where I do not resolve something, that is a finding, not an evasion.

## The core event: an energy transition

Strip away everything else and one thing distinguishes what happened in Britain
after roughly 1760 from every previous economic expansion:

**Humanity stopped being limited to energy flows arriving in real time and
started drawing on a stock.**

E.A. Wrigley's framing (*Continuity, Chance and Change*, 1988; *Energy and the
English Industrial Revolution*, 2010) is the clearest:

| | **Organic economy** (everywhere, before) | **Mineral-energy economy** (Britain, after) |
|---|---|---|
| Energy source | Current photosynthesis: food, fodder, firewood, plus wind and water | Coal — stored photosynthesis, hundreds of millions of years of it |
| Binding constraint | **Land.** Everything competed for acreage: food, fuel, fodder, fibre | Extraction and transport cost |
| Growth ceiling | Hard. More output requires more land, and land is fixed | Soft. Vastly larger, not infinite |
| Historical pattern | Smithian growth: gains from trade and specialisation, then stagnation | Sustained per-capita growth |

**Every previous efflorescence — Song China, the Dutch Golden Age, Renaissance
Italy, the Abbasid economy — ran into the land constraint and stopped.** The
Dutch case is instructive because they partially escaped it with peat, and got
further than anyone before running out.

**Consequently: the "why Britain, why then" question is substantially a
question about coal, and the honest form of the answer is that Britain had
large, shallow, high-quality coal deposits close to navigable water and close
to its cities.** That is geology, not genius.

## The steam story, and why it is over-told

The standard sequence: Newcomen's atmospheric engine (1712, for pumping water
out of mines), Watt's separate condenser (patented 1769, commercialised with
Boulton from 1775), rotary motion (1780s), high-pressure engines (Trevithick,
early 1800s), locomotives (Stephenson, *Rocket* 1829).

**The correction, which is well established and not widely known:**

- **Water power remained dominant in British manufacturing well into the 19th
  century.** Early textile mills were water-powered — that is why they are in
  Pennine valleys. Arkwright's Cromford (1771) was water-driven.
- **Nicholas Crafts's estimates put steam's contribution to British
  productivity growth at very modest levels before roughly 1830, rising
  substantially only mid-century** as high-pressure engines and railways
  arrived. The steam engine's decisive contribution was arguably *transport*
  (rail and steamship), not the factory.
- Growth rates during the "revolution" were slow by modern standards. Crafts
  and Harley's revisions put British per-capita growth in the late 18th century
  at well under 1% a year. **The revolution was a change in the trend rate, not
  a boom.**

**So the file's first correction: "steam engine → factories → Industrial
Revolution" is a schoolbook chain that specialists abandoned decades ago.** The
energy transition is real; the specific attribution to Watt is inflated.

## Allen's high-wage explanation

**Robert C. Allen, *The British Industrial Revolution in Global Perspective*
(2009).** The most influential single explanation of the last twenty years, and
the one that makes the invention question an *economic* question rather than a
cultural one.

The argument:

1. Britain c. 1700–1800 had **unusually high wages** relative to Continental
   Europe and China, and **unusually cheap energy** (coal at the pithead in
   Newcastle was extraordinarily cheap).
2. That combination — expensive labour, cheap capital and energy — makes
   labour-saving, energy-using machinery **profitable to develop and adopt in
   Britain and unprofitable elsewhere.**
3. The evidence: early spinning machinery was barely profitable even in Britain
   and clearly unprofitable in France or India at their wage levels. Someone in
   Delhi looking at a spinning jenny in 1780 would have been correct to decline
   it.
4. Once developed and improved, the machinery became profitable everywhere, and
   the technology diffused.

**Why this is a strong argument:** it explains invention *and* non-adoption
with one mechanism, it is quantitative, and it dissolves the need for any claim
about British cleverness or Chinese conservatism. It also predicts the pattern
of *which* technologies were developed — labour-saving, fuel-hungry ones.

**The serious objections, and they are serious:**

| Objection | Who | Force |
|---|---|---|
| The British wage series is wrong | **Judy Stephenson** — the construction wages Allen relies on are contractor day-rates including overhead and profit, not what workers received. Correcting this **substantially lowers** the British wage premium | **Strong. This is the most damaging critique** |
| The wage data ignores women and children | **Jane Humphries** — family income and the availability of cheap child labour change the incentive calculation entirely, and cut against a "high wage" story | Strong |
| Induced innovation is too neat | Invention is not that responsive to relative prices; much early machinery came from tinkerers with poor economic information | Moderate |
| It understates supply-side factors | Mokyr: you need the *capability* to build the machines, which requires an artisanal and scientific culture | Moderate — probably complementary rather than contradictory |

**Verdict: Tier 2. Allen's mechanism is probably part of the answer and the
wage premium it rests on is under active attack.**

## The Great Divergence

**Kenneth Pomeranz, *The Great Divergence* (2000).** The claim: the most
advanced regions of China (the Yangzi Delta) and Europe (England, the
Netherlands) were **comparably developed** as late as roughly 1750 — similar
life expectancy, market development, consumption, commercialisation. Divergence
was late and turned on two contingencies:

1. **Coal location.** Britain's coal was near its industrial cities; China's
   major coal was in Shanxi, far from the Yangzi Delta, with terrible transport.
2. **"Ghost acreage."** The New World supplied Britain with land-intensive
   goods (cotton, sugar, timber) without using British land, relieving the
   organic economy's binding constraint. Slave-worked plantations are not
   incidental to this argument; they are load-bearing.

**The objections:**

| Objection | Substance |
|---|---|
| **The divergence was earlier** | Broadberry and co-authors' GDP reconstructions put England ahead of the Yangzi Delta well before 1750, possibly by 1700 or earlier. If true, the whole "surprising late divergence" framing collapses. **This is the central empirical dispute** |
| **Agrarian institutions, not geography** | Robert Brenner & Christopher Isett: English agrarian capitalism (large tenant farms, wage labour, competitive leases) versus Chinese family-farm smallholding produced different growth dynamics long before coal mattered |
| **Involution** | Philip Huang: the Yangzi Delta was in agricultural involution — more labour per acre for falling marginal returns — not a parallel path to modernity |
| **Institutions and property rights** | North & Weingast; Acemoglu & Robinson: the Glorious Revolution (1688) constrained the crown and secured property rights. **Counter-objection: Britain's growth take-off is a century after 1688, which is a long lag for a decisive cause** |
| **Culture and ideas** | Mokyr's "Industrial Enlightenment" — a European "Republic of Letters" that made useful knowledge circulate and be tested. McCloskey's "bourgeois dignity" — a change in how commerce was *talked about*. Both are hard to test |

**Where this leaves us: nobody has won.** The honest summary is that coal
location, New World resources, high wages, agrarian institutions, political
fragmentation and a culture of useful knowledge are all plausibly necessary and
none is demonstrably sufficient. **Treat any confident single-cause account of
the Industrial Revolution — including the ones in this file — as advocacy.**

## What actually happened to living standards

**This is the section that matters most politically and is most often
misreported in both directions.**

### The optimist/pessimist debate

- **Optimists** (Lindert & Williamson, 1983): English real wages roughly
  doubled between 1820 and 1850. Industrialisation delivered quickly.
- **Pessimists** (Charles Feinstein, 1998, the more widely accepted modern
  revision): real wages rose far less — **on the order of 30% across the whole
  period c. 1780–1850**, that is, roughly 0.4% a year across seventy years, with
  almost all of the gain after 1840.

### The non-wage evidence, which is worse

| Indicator | What it shows |
|---|---|
| **Heights** | Anthropometric data (Floud, Fogel, Harris, Hong) show average heights **stagnating or falling** for cohorts born roughly 1820–1850. Falling stature during rising GDP is a strong signal that something is wrong |
| **Urban mortality** | Life expectancy in Manchester and Liverpool in the 1840s was catastrophic — figures in the region of the high twenties to low thirties, against rural England in the low forties. **Chadwick's 1842 Sanitary Report** documented it in detail |
| **Hours** | Working hours rose. The pre-industrial year had far more holy days. Annual hours worked plausibly rose by hundreds |
| **Child labour** | Increased in the early phase before the Factory Acts (1833, 1844, 1847) |
| **Autonomy** | Time discipline replaced task orientation — E.P. Thompson's "Time, Work-Discipline and Industrial Capitalism" (1967). Not measurable in wages, and this is the transformation contemporaries complained about most |

### Engels' pause

**Robert Allen's term** for the central fact: from roughly 1800 to the 1840s,
British **output per worker rose while real wages were roughly flat, and the
profit share of national income rose sharply.**

> **Growth arrived approximately forty years before wages did. That is longer
> than a working life.**

The implication is unavoidable and should be stated plainly: **a person born in
1790 into a textile district could work a full career inside the most
productive economy in world history and die no better off than their parents,
while national output per head rose substantially.** The gains were real and
they went somewhere else first.

**Caveat, and it is important: the pessimist case does not show that
industrialisation was net-negative.** It shows the gains were delayed by
decades and distributed with extreme unevenness. By 1900 British living
standards were transformatively higher. **Both facts are true and the argument
is about which one you are allowed to mention.**

## The Luddites

**The single most misused word in technology discourse, and correcting it is
the point of this section.**

### What actually happened

- 1811–1816. Nottinghamshire (framework knitters), Yorkshire (croppers /
  shearmen), Lancashire (weavers). Attacks on specific machinery, some arson,
  some assault, at least one killing (William Horsfall, mill owner,
  Yorkshire, 1812).
- "General Ludd" was a fictitious signatory on letters — a deliberate
  collective anonymity, not a leader.
- The state response was enormous: **thousands of troops deployed in the
  Midlands and North — reportedly more soldiers than Wellington had in the
  Peninsula at the time**, a figure often quoted and worth verifying. The
  **Frame Breaking Act 1812** made machine-breaking a capital offence. Byron's
  maiden speech in the Lords (February 1812) opposed it. The York trials of
  January 1813 produced seventeen executions.

### What they were actually objecting to

**Not machinery as such.** The demands were specific and they were about the
terms of trade:

| Grievance | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Wage-cutting via machinery** | Wide frames used to make cheap "cut-up" stockings — pieces cut from a knitted sheet rather than fashioned — undercutting skilled work and degrading the product |
| **Unapprenticed labour** | Machines operated by workers who had not served apprenticeships, breaking the trade's entry controls |
| **Loss of legal protection** | They had been petitioning under existing law — the Framework Knitters' Charter (1663) and the apprenticeship clauses of the Statute of Artificers. **Parliament repealed the apprenticeship clauses in 1814 and refused to enforce minimum-wage provisions.** They tried the legal route first and it was closed |
| **No right to organise** | The **Combination Acts (1799, 1800)** made collective bargaining illegal. Machine-breaking was, in Eric Hobsbawm's 1952 phrase, **"collective bargaining by riot"** — the only bargaining instrument left |

### They were right about themselves

**Handloom weavers' weekly earnings collapsed over roughly a generation — from
something like 20+ shillings in the first years of the century to around 5–6
shillings by the 1830s.** Hundreds of thousands of people in a skilled,
respected trade were reduced to destitution. **There was no retraining scheme,
no compensation, and no transfer of any part of the aggregate gain.**

E.P. Thompson, *The Making of the English Working Class* (1963), is the
foundational treatment; Kevin Binfield's *Writings of the Luddites* collects
their own documents, which is the fastest cure for the caricature.

### Why this matters beyond the history

**"Luddite" as an insult encodes a specific and false proposition: that
opposition to a technology is opposition to progress, and therefore
irrational.** The actual Luddites were:

- correct about the effect on themselves,
- not opposed to machinery in general,
- making a distributional claim, not a technical one,
- and were answered with capital punishment rather than argument.

**Every subsequent labour response to automation should be read with this in
mind.** When dockworkers, taxi drivers or illustrators object, the question to
ask is not "are they against progress" but "**who is capturing the surplus, and
is any of it being shared with the people bearing the cost**." That is a
different question with a different answer.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular story | Correction |
|---|---|
| **"Watt's steam engine caused the Industrial Revolution"** | Water power dominated early factories; steam's productivity contribution was modest before roughly 1830. The transport applications mattered more than the factory ones |
| **"It was a sudden explosion of growth"** | Per-capita growth was under 1% a year for decades. It was a change in the *trend*, barely visible to contemporaries |
| **"Industrialisation quickly raised living standards"** | Roughly forty years of flat real wages, falling heights, and appalling urban mortality first. Engels' pause is the mainstream position now |
| **"The Luddites hated technology"** | They were skilled workers objecting to specific uses of machinery to break wage rates, product standards and apprenticeship, after their legal remedies were removed |
| **"Britain industrialised because of British institutions/culture/genius"** | All contested. Coal geology and New World land are doing a great deal of the work in the best current accounts, and the wage evidence underpinning the leading economic explanation is under attack |
| **"China stagnated"** | Pomeranz's whole point is that the Yangzi Delta was comparably developed until late. Contested — Broadberry says otherwise — but "stagnation" is not the state of the debate |
| **"The workers who resisted were on the wrong side of history"** | They were on the losing side. Those are different. Their descendants benefited; they did not |

      reconstructed GDP. Is it converging, or are the data too weak to settle?

# How Technology Changes Society — The Frameworks, Steelmanned

> **Status: TIER 2 throughout.** These are interpretive frameworks, not
> findings. None is falsifiable in the way an empirical claim is. What *is*
> Tier 1 is the negative result: **strong technological determinism is
> contradicted by the comparative evidence, and strong social constructionism
> cannot explain why some things are impossible.** Everything else is
> positioning between those two failures.

**Written from general knowledge without live source verification.** Attribution
of specific arguments to specific scholars is from memory and should be checked
before quoting.

---

## The two failure modes

Every account of technology and society fails in one of two directions.

| | **Determinism** | **Constructionism** |
|---|---|---|
| Core claim | Technology develops by its own logic and reshapes society | Technology is shaped by social interests; its "effects" are social choices |
| Strength | Explains why societies with similar tech converge on similar forms; takes material constraint seriously | Explains why identical tech produces different outcomes; explains design choices |
| Fatal weakness | **Cannot explain China vs Europe on printing.** Cannot explain why Japan gave up the gun | **Cannot explain why nobody flew in 1200.** Tends to dissolve all constraint into discourse |
| Political effect | Fatalism. "You can't stop progress" | Voluntarism. Underestimates how hard things are |
| Who holds it | Popular writing, tech industry, Marx in his cruder readings | Academic STS, sociology of science |

**The truth is not "somewhere in the middle" in a lazy way.** The truth is that
the two operate at different levels: material constraint sets the *outer
boundary* of the possible and the *relative costs* inside it; social structure
determines *which* of the possible things happen. Neither is a residual of the
other.

## Hard determinism

**Claim:** technology is the primary driver of historical change, developing
according to an internal technical logic, with social forms following.

**Steelman — and it deserves one, because it is usually dismissed too fast:**

- Some technologies really do impose narrow ranges of viable social
  organisation. You cannot run a nuclear reactor with a loose confederation of
  volunteers. You cannot operate a continent-scale rail network without
  standardised time — and indeed **standard time zones were created by
  railways** (UK railway time from the 1840s, US standard time 1883, adopted
  by the railroads before any government mandated it). The clock beat the
  legislature.
- Technical constraints genuinely are non-negotiable. Steam engines had to be
  near coal because coal is heavy. Early textile mills had to be on
  fast-flowing water. No amount of social preference changes that.
- The convergence evidence is real: industrialising societies with very
  different ideologies — Meiji Japan, Bismarck's Germany, the Soviet Union,
  South Korea — converged on strikingly similar factory organisation, urban
  form and demographic transition.
- Lynn White Jr.'s *Medieval Technology and Social Change* (1962) — the stirrup
  thesis: stirrups enabled mounted shock combat, which required expensive
  knights, which required land grants to support them, which is feudalism.
  **This is the most famous determinist argument in medieval history and it has
  been largely demolished** (the chronology doesn't work — stirrups arrive well
  before the military and tenurial changes; Bernard Bachrach and others took it
  apart) — but it is the clearest possible statement of the form.

**Why it fails:** the comparative evidence. Printing, gunpowder, the compass
and the blast furnace were all Chinese first, by centuries, and produced
different social outcomes there. Japan had matchlock firearms in quantity from
the 1540s, used them decisively at Nagashino (1575), and then the Tokugawa
regime substantially restricted their production and use for two centuries
(Noel Perrin, *Giving Up the Gun*, 1979 — **note that Perrin's account is
disputed by specialists as overstating the completeness of the abandonment**;
the direction is right, the totality is not). A society *can* decline a
technology it possesses.

## Soft determinism

**Claim:** technology does not dictate outcomes, but it strongly conditions the
probability distribution over them, and once adopted it has momentum that is
very hard to reverse.

This is where most careful historians actually sit, including many who would
reject the "determinist" label. Thomas Hughes's concept of **technological
momentum** (from *Networks of Power*, 1983, on electrification) is the best
version: a young technological system is socially malleable; a mature one, with
sunk capital, trained personnel, standards, regulations and dependent
industries, becomes socially rigid and starts to behave *as if* deterministic.

**This is probably the most defensible position in the file.** It explains:
- Why early design choices matter enormously and later ones don't (path
  dependence).
- Why the same technology looks constructed early and determining late.
- Why "we could just stop using cars" is technically true and practically
  false.

## SCOT — the Social Construction of Technology

**Bijker, Hughes & Pinch, *The Social Construction of Technological Systems*
(1987); Pinch & Bijker's foundational 1984 paper.**

Core apparatus:

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Relevant social groups** | The groups who attach meaning to an artefact — users, makers, regulators, opponents |
| **Interpretive flexibility** | Early on, different groups disagree about what the thing *is for* and what counts as it working |
| **Closure / stabilisation** | Disagreement ends — either by rhetorical redefinition of the problem, or by one group winning |
| **Wider context** | Later addition, after criticism that SCOT ignored structural power |

**The canonical case is the bicycle.** The high-wheeler ("penny-farthing") was
not a failed safety bicycle. For young athletic men it was a *success* — it was
fast and it signalled daring. For women in skirts and for older riders it was
unusable. The "safety bicycle" with equal wheels and pneumatic tyres (Dunlop,
1888) won not because it was objectively better but because the relevant social
group shifted and the definition of "a good bicycle" changed with it. Air tyres
were initially mocked as ugly before being redefined as fast when they started
winning races.

**Steelman:** this is genuinely correct about design history and it dissolves a
lot of retrospective "obviously the better design won" nonsense. See
the-control-group.md, where the same move destroys the Betamax story.

**Why it fails on its own:** SCOT is very good at explaining *design closure*
and very bad at explaining *consequences*. It has almost nothing to say about
why a society with railways ends up different from one without. Critics
(notably Langdon Winner, "Upon Opening the Black Box and Finding It Empty",
1993) charged that SCOT systematically omits (a) consequences, (b) the groups
excluded from the negotiation entirely, and (c) any evaluative standpoint. If
everyone relevant is at the table, who counts as relevant? The people with no
power to shape the technology are exactly the people it will be done to.

## Actor-network theory

**Latour, Callon, Law. *Science in Action* (1987); *Reassembling the Social*
(2005).**

Core moves: **generalised symmetry** (explain success and failure with the same
vocabulary — do not say true theories won because they were true); **actants**
(non-humans have agency in the network — the scallops, the door-closer, the
speed bump as a "sleeping policeman"); **translation** (enrolling allies,
human and not, into a network).

**Steelman:** ANT's real contribution is refusing the human/technical split at
the start of the analysis rather than at the end. A working technology *is* a
successfully assembled network of people, materials, institutions, standards
and habits. That is why "the technology works in the lab" predicts so little,
and it is a genuinely useful corrective.

**Why it fails as a general account:** it is a description language, not a
theory. It generates no predictions, resists any notion of structural power
(class, state, capital dissolve into flat networks of associations), and its
prose style has done real damage to the field's credibility. Ask an ANT account
"so would the outcome have been different without the artefact?" and you
frequently get no answer, because the question presupposes a separation ANT
denies.

## Mumford — authoritarian and democratic technics

**Lewis Mumford, *Technics and Civilization* (1934); "Authoritarian and
Democratic Technics" (1964); *The Myth of the Machine* (1967–70).**

The distinction:

| | **Democratic technics** | **Authoritarian technics** |
|---|---|---|
| Scale | Small, distributed | Large, centralised |
| Control | Many hands, local | Few hands, remote |
| Failure mode | Local, recoverable | Systemic, catastrophic |
| Relation to skill | Enhances the operator | Requires the operator to be interchangeable |
| Examples he gives | Hand tools, the small farm, craft workshops | Pyramid-building, the "megamachine", nuclear power |

Mumford's "megamachine" idea — that the first great machine was made of
*people*, the organised labour armies of Bronze Age god-kings, and that its
components were bureaucracy, army and priesthood — is genuinely original and
underrated. It relocates the question from artefacts to organisation.

**Steelman:** the underlying claim is not romanticism. It is that **the scale
and centralisation of a technology have political implications independent of
who owns it**, because centralised systems require centralised control to
operate safely. Nuclear power really does require a permanent security
apparatus and cannot be run democratically at the operational level. That is a
substantive claim, not an aesthetic preference.

**Where it fails:** the categories are too clean and Mumford stacked them.
Vaccines, antibiotics, water treatment and the electricity grid are large,
centralised, expert-controlled and unambiguously liberating. Small-scale
technics are compatible with brutal local hierarchy. The correlation Mumford
asserts between scale and domination is much weaker than he claims.

## Ellul — autonomous technique

**Jacques Ellul, *La Technique* (1954), English as *The Technological Society*
(1964).**

Ellul's object is not machines. It is **technique**: the totality of methods
rationally arrived at and having absolute efficiency in every field of human
activity. Bureaucratic procedure, advertising, propaganda, management science,
and machinery are all technique.

Claims: technique is **self-augmenting** (each solution generates the problems
requiring the next technique); **universal** (it colonises every domain,
including leisure, religion and politics); **autonomous** (it is not steered
from outside, because every proposed constraint gets evaluated by efficiency
criteria, which is technique judging itself); and **monistic** (you cannot
adopt the parts you like).

**Steelman, which is stronger than people expect:** Ellul predicts something
specific and observable — that any critique of a technical system will be
answered with a technical fix rather than with a decision to stop, and that the
fix will generate the next problem. That pattern is extremely well attested.
CFCs replaced toxic refrigerants and destroyed the ozone layer; HFCs replaced
CFCs and turned out to be potent greenhouse gases. Traffic congestion is
answered with more lanes, which induces more traffic. Attention capture is
answered with screen-time features built by the attention-capturing company.

**Where it fails:** it is unfalsifiable as stated, and the Montreal Protocol is
a real counterexample — a technical system was identified as harmful and
*stopped* by political decision, internationally, quickly. Ellul's framework
has no room for that, which means the framework is wrong, not that Montreal
didn't happen. Leaded petrol is a second counterexample. Both took decades and
both required a coalition of interest, but both happened.

## Winner — "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" and the Moses bridges

**Langdon Winner, *Daedalus*, 1980; collected in *The Whale and the Reactor*
(1986).**

Winner distinguishes two ways artefacts can be political:

1. **Specific designs settle a political question** — the arrangement embodies
   a choice about who gets what.
2. **Inherently political technologies** — systems whose operation *requires* a
   particular kind of social organisation regardless of ownership (his example:
   nuclear power, following Mumford).

The second claim is the stronger one and the more defensible.

### The Moses bridges — read this carefully, it is the best teaching case here

Winner's opening example: Robert Moses built the Long Island parkway overpasses
unusually low, allegedly so that buses could not pass under them, keeping poor
and Black New Yorkers away from Jones Beach. He took it from Robert Caro's
*The Power Broker* (1974).

**The critique** (Bernward Joerges, "Do Politics Have Artefacts?", 1999; Steve
Woolgar & Geoff Cooper, 1999):

| Winner's claim | The objection |
|---|---|
| Low bridges excluded buses | Commercial vehicles including buses were **banned from the parkways by law** anyway — the bridge height was redundant to the legal restriction |
| Therefore excluded poor/Black New Yorkers | Bus and rail services to Jones Beach existed by other routes |
| The heights were anomalous | Parkway overpass heights were within normal ranges for the type |
| Documented intent | Rests substantially on a **single Caro interview** with a former Moses associate |

**The honest position, and it has three parts:**
- Moses's racism and his use of infrastructure as a weapon are documented at
  length and independently by Caro. That is not in dispute.
- **Whether the bridge heights specifically were designed for that purpose is
  unproven**, and the story has been repeated for forty years as established
  fact. It should not be.
- **Winner's thesis survives losing this example**, and his *second* example is
  better: the mechanical tomato harvester developed at UC Davis required
  breeding a tougher, blander tomato, cost far more than small growers could
  finance, and was followed by a collapse in the number of California tomato
  growers (thousands down to a few hundred over roughly two decades). That is
  a well-documented case of a design choice with distributive consequences,
  publicly funded, with no one ever having voted on it.

**Meta-lesson, and it is the most important one in this file: the flagship
example of a good idea is frequently the weakest evidence for it.** Same
structure as QWERTY (see the-control-group.md) and the stirrup. Check the
flagship.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular framing | Correction |
|---|---|
| "Technology is neutral; it depends how you use it" | It changes the *relative cost* of actions. That is not neutral. But this doesn't mean artefacts have intentions — it means cost structures have distributive consequences |
| "You can't stop progress" | Japan and firearms; the Montreal Protocol; leaded petrol; human germline editing; supersonic overland flight; CFCs. **Societies decline and reverse technologies regularly.** The claim is ideology, not history |
| "The market picks the best technology" | See the-control-group.md. The market picks on the attribute buyers optimise, which is often not quality |
| "Social construction means it's all arbitrary" | No serious SCOT scholar claims this. Interpretive flexibility closes; materials constrain |
| "The Moses bridges prove artefacts have politics" | The bridges are the weakest part of Winner's case. Cite the tomato harvester instead |
| "The stirrup created feudalism" | Chronologically doesn't work. Cited constantly anyway |
| "Determinism vs constructionism is a spectrum and I'm in the middle" | They answer different questions. Materials set the boundary and the cost gradients; society picks within them. "The middle" is a dodge |

## Open questions

- [ ] Is there a rigorous way to separate the "outer boundary of the possible"
      (material) from "which possibility was realised" (social), or does the
      boundary itself move with social investment in a way that makes the
      distinction circular?
- [ ] Hughes's technological momentum predicts systems get less malleable with
      age. Is there a measurable proxy for momentum — sunk capital, employment,
      standards count — that would let this be tested rather than asserted?
- [ ] Winner's "inherently political technologies": which ones genuinely
      require a specific social form, versus merely correlate with one under
      current institutions? Nuclear power is the test case and I do not think
      it is settled.
- [ ] Has anyone systematically catalogued *abandoned* technologies — cases
      where a society possessed a capability and declined it? This would be the
      key dataset against determinism and I am not aware of a good one.
- [ ] Does ANT ever produce a prediction that a simpler framework wouldn't?
- [ ] Ellul's self-augmentation claim: is the pattern of technical-fix-generates-
      next-problem actually more common than technical-fix-solves-problem, or
      is it selection bias in what gets written about?

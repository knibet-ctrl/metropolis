# Rules and Enforcement

Formal and informal, and what happens where neither works.

> **Status: TIER 2 overall.** The deterrence findings (certainty over severity) are TIER 1. The historical cases — Maghribi traders, the law merchant, the Sicilian mafia — are TIER 2 and several are actively disputed by economic historians. Dunbar's number is TIER 3 and weaker than its ubiquity suggests.

## Two families of enforcement

| | Informal | Formal |
|---|---|---|
| Enforcer | Peers, community, counterparties | Specialised third party (court, regulator, police) |
| Sanction | Gossip, exclusion, refusal to deal, shame, violence | Fines, damages, imprisonment, licence revocation |
| Requires | Identifiability, communication, repeat interaction, shared norms | Legibility, records, coercive capacity, procedural legitimacy |
| Cost | Very low per case; high fixed cost in maintaining social density | High per case; scales to strangers |
| Scale ceiling | Roughly the group within which reputation circulates | In principle unbounded |
| Failure mode | Ostracism of the innocent; in-group protection of the guilty; conformity pressure; feuds | Capture, delay, cost, formalism, unenforceable judgments |
| Speed | Immediate | Slow |

Most real systems are hybrids, and the interesting cases are where one substitutes for the other.

## Reputation: what it actually requires

Reputation only disciplines behaviour when three conditions hold simultaneously:

1. **Identifiability.** You can tell who did it, and that identity persists across encounters. Anonymity or cheap identity replacement kills it — the "cheap pseudonyms" problem in online markets, and the reason review systems fight so hard over account verification.
2. **A communication channel.** Information about the defection reaches future counterparties. This requires both a physical channel and a **shared vocabulary of grievance** — people must agree on what counts as a violation, or the report is noise.
3. **Consequence.** Future counterparties must be willing and able to act on the information. If everyone needs the defector's services regardless, reputation is just talk.

Break any one and reputation stops working. Most reputation-system failures — in online marketplaces, professional bodies, academic misconduct handling — are failures of condition 3, not condition 2. Everyone knows; nobody can afford to act.

**Gossip is enforcement infrastructure.** It is usually framed as a vice; functionally it is the transmission layer of an informal enforcement system, and societies invest heavily in it. Its characteristic pathologies — inaccuracy, in-group bias, and its use as a weapon against low-status members — are the costs of the mechanism, not corruptions of it.

## Scale, and the Dunbar caveat

Robin Dunbar (1992, 1993) regressed neocortex ratio on group size across primates and extrapolated to humans, arriving at roughly 150 as the number of individuals with whom one can maintain stable social relationships. He supported it with a range of anthropological and historical group sizes — Neolithic villages, Hutterite settlements, military companies, Christmas card lists.

**The caveat that is almost never mentioned:** a reanalysis (Lindenfors and colleagues, around 2021) argued that the statistical extrapolation is unsound and that the confidence interval around the human estimate is enormous — plausibly spanning from a handful to several hundred. The point estimate of 150 has no strong claim to precision.

**What survives is the qualitative claim, which is the one that matters here:** there is *some* ceiling on the number of people about whom you can hold individuated social knowledge, and it is far below the size of a city, a corporation or a state. Above that ceiling, reputation-based informal enforcement cannot cover all interactions, and something else must take over. Whether the ceiling is 90 or 290 does not change the argument. Do not build a management theory on the specific number.

## The personal-to-impersonal transition

This is, in my view, one of the most under-appreciated transitions in civilisational history, and it is the reason the folder exists.

**Personal exchange:** you trade with people you know, or with people known to people you know. Enforcement is reputational. Cheating is deterred because it terminates a valuable relationship and a stream of future ones. The system is robust, cheap, and **caps the size of the economy** — you cannot specialise deeply if you can only trade within a network of a few hundred people.

**Impersonal exchange:** you trade with strangers you will never meet again, across jurisdictions, at scale. This requires machinery that does not exist naturally: standardised contract forms, courts that will enforce against a party you have no relationship with, ways of measuring quality without inspection (grades, standards, certification, brands), limited liability, credible money, bankruptcy procedures that allocate losses predictably, and impartial officials who do not favour their own kin.

North's argument is that this transition is what made modern economies possible, that it is historically recent and rare, and that it is **not a natural development** — every component had to be built and most societies never built them. When you see a society where business is done through family, clan or connections, that is not a cultural quirk; it is a rational adaptation to the absence of impersonal enforcement machinery.

**Intermediate institutions bridged the gap.** Merchant networks, guilds, diasporas, bills of exchange and correspondent banking all extended personal-style enforcement further than face-to-face contact allowed, by chaining reputational links.

## The historical cases, and the disputes about them

**Greif on the Maghribi traders.** Avner Greif (1993, 1994) studied eleventh-century Jewish traders from the Maghrib operating around the Mediterranean, using the Cairo Geniza documents. His account: they solved the agency problem — how do you trust an overseas agent handling your goods? — with a **multilateral reputation mechanism**. A coalition circulated information about cheating agents by letter; a cheater was blacklisted by the entire coalition, not just the injured party. He contrasted this "collectivist" arrangement with the "individualist" Genoese, who used formal legal enforcement, and argued the two paths had long-run consequences — collectivist mechanisms scale poorly to strangers, individualist ones support impersonal exchange.

**The dispute:** Jeremy Edwards and Sheilagh Ogilvie (around 2012) reexamined the Geniza evidence and argued that the Maghribis routinely used **formal legal institutions** — courts, notarised contracts, legal agents — and that the evidence for an exclusive multilateral reputation coalition is thin. Greif responded robustly. This is a genuine and unresolved dispute among economic historians, and the Maghribi case is cited far more confidently in secondary literature than the primary dispute warrants.

**The law merchant.** Milgrom, North and Weingast (1990) modelled the *lex mercatoria* at the medieval Champagne fairs: private judges kept records of merchants' dispute histories, merchants could query the record cheaply before trading, and judgments were enforced by the threat of losing access to the fairs. An elegant demonstration that private ordering can support impersonal exchange.

**The dispute:** Ogilvie (*Institutions and European Trade*, 2011) argued extensively that merchant guilds were primarily **rent-seeking cartels** — restricting entry, fixing prices, excluding outsiders and buying privileges — and that their contribution to trade efficiency has been substantially overstated by a literature predisposed to find efficiency explanations. **This is the strongest general critique of the private-ordering literature and it should be applied by default:** when you find an institution that appears to solve a coordination problem, check who is excluded and who collects the rents.

**Ellickson, *Order Without Law* (1991).** Robert Ellickson studied cattle-trespass disputes in Shasta County, California. Ranchers resolved damage from straying cattle through **norms of neighbourliness** — informal accounting of who owed whom, resolved over time — and largely ignored the actual legal rules, which they frequently misunderstood. Notably, the legal rules differed between open-range and closed-range districts, and behaviour barely differed. Invoking law was itself a norm violation, marking you as not a neighbour. The lesson: **within a dense repeat-play community, informal enforcement dominates and formal law is close to irrelevant** — the "shadow of the law" model overstates law's reach.

**Bernstein on diamonds.** Lisa Bernstein (1992) documented that the New York diamond industry — historically concentrated in an Orthodox Jewish community with the Diamond Dealers Club at its centre — operated on oral agreements sealed with *mazal u'bracha*, with disputes going to internal arbitration rather than courts. Enforcement rested on reputational bonds and community membership. She made a similar study of cotton-industry arbitration. **The important limit:** these systems depend on a bounded, socially dense community with high exit costs. As the diamond trade globalised and dispersed, the mechanism weakened — which is exactly the prediction.

## Where the state fails: private protection

**Diego Gambetta, *The Sicilian Mafia: The Business of Private Protection* (1993).** The central move is to treat the mafia not as a criminal conspiracy, a subculture, or a form of alternative government, but as **an industry that sells a specific commodity: protection and the enforcement of agreements**. Its customers include legitimate businesses that need contracts enforced, debts collected, competition regulated, and property rights defended, and who cannot get these from the state.

The historical origin story: after the abolition of feudalism in Sicily in the early nineteenth century, property was privatised and redistributed on a large scale, creating widespread demand for the protection of new property rights — while the Bourbon and then Italian state had negligible enforcement presence on the island. **Demand for enforcement without a supplier creates a supplier.** Supporting econometric work has linked mafia presence to sulphur mining areas (Buonanno, Durante, Prarolo, Vanin) and to citrus-producing areas — high-value, easily-stolen, export-dependent crops requiring protection — drawing on the 1876 Franchetti and Sonnino investigation. Oriana Bandiera studied land fragmentation and protection demand.

**Federico Varese** made the same argument for the **Russian mafia** after 1991: rapid privatisation created private property with no functioning enforcement — courts that could not enforce judgments, police that could not protect — and organised crime supplied *krysha* ("roof"). Same mechanism, different century, which is what makes it a mechanism rather than a story about Sicily.

**Gambetta's *Codes of the Underworld*** extends this to the signalling problem criminals face: how do you advertise criminal competence to accomplices without advertising it to police? The answer involves costly, hard-to-fake signals — which is why prison time, tattoos and demonstrated violence function as credentials.

**The general principle: enforcement demand does not disappear when the state fails to supply it.** It is met by whoever can — mafias, militias, clan elders, gangs, informal arbitrators, or armed creditors. This is why removing a bad enforcement institution without replacing it is usually worse than reforming it, and it is the recurring error in [the-control-group.md](the-control-group.md).

## Deterrence: the one robust practical finding

The best-supported operational result in the enforcement literature, associated particularly with Daniel Nagin's reviews:

**Certainty of punishment deters; severity does very little.**

Increasing the probability of being caught has substantial deterrent effects. Increasing sentence length, for a given probability, has small to negligible marginal effects. Related findings: **swiftness** matters (long delays between offence and sanction weaken the association); police *presence* deters more than police *numbers*; and the deterrent effect of imprisonment is largely incapacitation rather than deterrence at the margin.

This maps directly onto Ostrom's principles 4 and 5: **accountable monitoring** (raising certainty) plus **graduated sanctions** (which are actually applied, precisely because they are small enough to be socially bearable). A rule with a draconian penalty that nobody imposes has *less* deterrent effect than a rule with a trivial penalty that is always imposed.

The organisational corollary: a policy with severe stated consequences that are never enforced is worse than no policy, because it teaches everyone that the rule system is theatre.

## Steelman: the case for formalism

Informal enforcement has an appreciative literature and a serious dark side. The case *for* formal, impersonal, procedural law:

- **Informal systems protect insiders and punish outsiders.** Ostracism is a weapon, and it lands hardest on the low-status, the different and the newly arrived. Formal law's procedural rigidity is precisely what protects the unpopular defendant.
- **Norm enforcement has no appeal mechanism.** Ellickson's neighbours were fine; a norm-enforcing community that decides you are a witch, a collaborator or a deviant is not.
- **Guilds and coalitions extract rents.** Ogilvie's critique generalises: private ordering tends to become a cartel.
- **Informal systems cannot handle strangers**, which caps prosperity and mobility.
- **Blood feud is an informal enforcement system.** It works, in the sense that it deters. Its costs are visible in every society that had one.

The honest position: informal enforcement is cheap, fast and unaccountable; formal enforcement is expensive, slow and reviewable. Neither dominates. Systems that work usually have informal enforcement handling volume and formal enforcement handling the hard cases and constraining the informal system's excesses.

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"Strong laws make honest societies."** Certainty and swiftness of enforcement matter far more than the content or severity of the law. Most societies with terrible outcomes have adequate laws on the books.
- **"The mafia is a criminal conspiracy."** It is better modelled as a firm in the protection industry, and it exists where the state under-supplies enforcement. This is not exculpatory; it is diagnostic, and it predicts where such organisations will emerge.
- **"Dunbar's number is 150."** The point estimate is not well identified. The qualitative ceiling is what matters.
- **"Medieval merchants show markets are self-regulating."** The private-ordering literature has a systematic selection problem and Ogilvie's cartel critique is unresolved. Some private ordering was efficiency-enhancing; some was rent extraction wearing the same clothes.
- **"Reputation systems solve trust online."** Only where identity is costly to replace, information travels, and users can afford to act on it. Cheap pseudonymity defeats the first, fragmented platforms defeat the second, and market concentration defeats the third.
- **"More surveillance means more compliance."** Up to a point, and then monitoring begins to substitute for the norm rather than support it. See [organisational-trust.md](organisational-trust.md).

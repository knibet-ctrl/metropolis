# The Questions

Disaggregating what "trust" means before asking whether it is rising or falling.

> **Status: TIER 1 on the distinctions themselves — that these are separate constructs measured by separate instruments is not seriously disputed. TIER 4 on several popular framings that collapse them.**

> Written from general knowledge on 2026-09-01 without live source verification. Names, dates and figures are recalled, not checked.

---

## The central confusion

Two sentences appear constantly in public discussion, usually in the same paragraph, usually as if they were the same claim:

- *"Trust in institutions is declining."*
- *"People trust each other less than they used to."*

These are **separate empirical claims about different objects, measured by different instruments, with different and partly contradictory evidence.** One can be true while the other is false. In the United States since roughly 1973, the first is largely true for some institutions and flat for others; the second is true but is substantially a **cohort replacement** effect rather than individuals changing their minds. In much of continental Europe, neither trend is clearly present.

Almost every bad argument in this domain comes from sliding between the following five things.

---

## The five things

| # | Construct | Plain definition | Typically measured by | Unit that has it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Generalised interpersonal trust** | Belief that unknown members of your society will not exploit you | One survey item: "most people can be trusted" vs "you can't be too careful" (WVS/GSS/ESS) | Individual belief, aggregated to society |
| 2 | **Particularised trust** | Belief that *your* people — kin, clan, co-ethnics, colleagues — will not exploit you | Radius-of-trust batteries; named-group items | Individual belief about a specific group |
| 3 | **Institutional confidence** | Belief that an organisation will perform its function competently and impartially | "How much confidence do you have in…" (Gallup, Eurobarometer, OECD Trust Survey) | Attitude toward an organisation |
| 4 | **Trustworthiness** | Whether people *actually* behave honestly when they could profitably cheat | Behavioural: lost-wallet returns, trust-game return rates, tax compliance, audit studies | Behaviour, not belief |
| 5 | **Cooperation** | Whether a joint surplus is actually produced | Public goods games; observed collective action; resource-system durability | Group outcome |

And a sixth that is often smuggled in:

| 6 | **Compliance** | Whether people follow rules | Enforcement statistics, evasion rates | Behaviour under a sanction regime |

Compliance is the one most often mistaken for trust. **High compliance under heavy surveillance is not trust; it is deterrence.** A society with excellent tax compliance produced by an efficient revenue service and a society with excellent tax compliance produced by a strong norm of contribution look identical in the compliance data and behave completely differently when enforcement weakens.

## Why the distinctions bite

**Belief ≠ behaviour.** Glaeser, Laibson, Scheinkman and Soutter (2000) ran an experiment that ought to be better known: they found the standard attitudinal trust question predicted subjects' own **trustworthiness** better than it predicted their **trusting behaviour**. If that holds, the WVS trust item is partly a measure of how honest *you* are, not how honest you think others are. That inverts a great deal of interpretation built on that item.

**Trust in the trust game is not only trust.** Sapienza, Toldrà-Simats and Zingales argued that the amount sent in the Berg–Dickhaut–McCabe (1995) investment game confounds beliefs about the trustee with the sender's own risk preference and social preferences. Bohnet and Zeckhauser separately showed **betrayal aversion**: people demand a higher probability of a good outcome when the risk comes from a person than when it comes from a lottery. So the standard laboratory measure is at minimum a three-part composite.

**"Most people" may not mean the same thing everywhere.** Delhey, Newton and Welzel (2011) argued that respondents in different societies fill in different reference groups for "most people". In some East Asian and Middle Eastern samples the implied radius looks narrower than in Nordic samples. If true, a chunk of the famous cross-national trust gradient is a translation artefact, not a difference in trust. **This is the single biggest threat to the entire comparative literature and it is not resolved.**

**Institutional confidence is not a single thing.** In the US Gallup series, confidence in Congress fell catastrophically (from roughly 40%+ in the 1970s to high single digits at its worst), confidence in the press fell substantially, confidence in the military rose then plateaued, and confidence in small business and the police moved on entirely different paths with sharp demographic splits. Aggregating these into "institutional trust" destroys the signal.

## Mapping each question to its evidence

| Question people ask | What it actually decomposes into | Evidence quality | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Is trust declining?" | Which construct? Which country? Which institution? Which cohort? | Good for US #1 and #3; patchy elsewhere | TIER 2 as posed; TIER 4 if left unspecified |
| "Do high-trust societies grow faster?" | #1 correlates with growth. Does #1 cause it, or does good institutional quality cause both? | Robust correlation; causal identification weak and contested | TIER 2 |
| "Is my team high-trust?" | Almost always means #5 (does it cooperate) and #4 (is it safe to be honest here) | Decent within-organisation measures; weak external validity | TIER 2 |
| "Are people basically good?" | Malformed. Conflates #4 with a moral claim, and behaviour is heavily situation-dependent | — | TIER 4 |
| "Does diversity reduce trust?" | Which trust? Whose? At what geographic scale? Net of deprivation? | Contested; effects small and scale-specific; see below | TIER 2 |
| "Has social media destroyed trust?" | Conflates #1, #3, affective polarisation and media consumption | Weak for strong causal versions | TIER 3 |
| "Should I trust this person?" | A decision problem, not a measurement problem. Depends on stakes, exit options, repeat play, and observability | — | Well-formed but individual |

## The diversity question, handled carefully

Robert Putnam's 2007 "E Pluribus Unum" lecture reported that in more ethnically diverse American neighbourhoods, residents reported lower trust in *everyone*, including their own group — "hunkering down". This is one of the most politically weaponised findings in the field, in both directions.

What the subsequent literature indicates, as best I can recall it:

- The effect is **concentrated at small geographic scales** (neighbourhood, not nation) and largely affects **local, neighbourhood-directed trust rather than generalised trust in strangers**.
- Effect sizes are **small** and shrink substantially when economic deprivation and residential instability are controlled — the meta-analytic work (Dinesen, Schaeffer and Sønderskov, around 2020) found the diversity–trust association close to negligible for generalised trust.
- Results outside the US are weaker and inconsistent.
- Contact quality moderates it: mixed neighbourhoods with high inter-group contact do not show the effect.

**Where this is a values disagreement, not a factual one:** even if the small negative local effect is real, it does not settle any policy question, because the policy question involves weighing it against economic gains, humanitarian obligations, rights claims and dynamic effects over generations. People who agree completely on the coefficient disagree completely on the policy. Say that out loud rather than pretending the coefficient decides.

## Questions worth asking instead

Better-formed replacements for the usual questions:

1. **What is the cost to an individual here of behaving honestly when cheating is available and unobserved?** (Trustworthiness as an incentive question.)
2. **Can defectors be identified, and can information about them travel?** (The precondition for reputation.)
3. **Is there a low-cost, low-status-cost channel for reporting that something is wrong?** (Error correction — the mechanism that recurs in every case in this library.)
4. **Who monitors, and who pays the monitor?** (The Andersen/ratings-agency question. It explains more corporate failures than character does.)
5. **If everyone else defects, what does a unilateral cooperator get?** (Distinguishes a moral failing from a coordination equilibrium — the difference determines whether exhortation or structural change is the right intervention.)
6. **Is the form present without the function?** (The transplant question.)

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"Trust is a national character trait."** The strongest single predictor of measured trust within a country is not ethnicity or culture but *perceived impartiality of public institutions* — courts, police, bureaucratic street-level administration. Character-trait framings survive because they are memorable, not because they are well supported.
- **"Trust has been declining everywhere since the 1960s."** This is an Americentric generalisation. Nordic countries show flat-to-rising generalised trust across the same period. The universal-decline narrative is a US story exported as a human one.
- **"The trust question measures trust."** It substantially measures the respondent's own trustworthiness, their risk tolerance, and their interpretation of "most people". It is a useful instrument and a badly named one.
- **"Low trust is a symptom of moral decay."** More often it is a rational reading of a genuinely untrustworthy environment. In a society where officials do extract bribes, low trust is *accurate*. Treating it as a defect of the population inverts cause and effect and licenses interventions aimed at the wrong target.
- **"High trust is always good."** High-trust environments are more efficient *and more exploitable*. Ponzi schemes, affinity fraud and insider capture flourish in high-trust settings precisely because verification is skipped. Madoff worked a high-trust community. The optimum is not maximum trust; it is trust calibrated to actual trustworthiness.

## Open questions

- [ ] Does the "most people" item measure the same construct across languages? Is there a validated behavioural anchor that makes cross-national comparison defensible?
- [ ] Is the US decline in generalised trust genuinely cohort replacement, and if so what changed in the formative environment of successive cohorts?
- [ ] Can trustworthiness (#4) be measured at national scale cheaply and behaviourally? Lost-wallet field experiments are the best attempt; are they contaminated by observation effects?
- [ ] What is the correct radius decomposition — is there a stable structure to whom people trust, or is it entirely context-generated?
- [ ] Does declining institutional confidence have any measurable behavioural consequence, or is it an attitude that costs nothing to express?
- [ ] Is "critical citizenship" (Norris) empirically separable from erosion of legitimacy, or is that distinction unfalsifiable comfort?
- [ ] Does high generalised trust increase vulnerability to fraud enough to be measurable in victimisation data?

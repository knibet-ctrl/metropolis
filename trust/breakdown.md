# Breakdown

How trust collapses, why the collapse is asymmetric, and what the evidence on decline actually supports.

> **Status: TIER 2 for the asymmetry and spiral mechanisms. TIER 2/3 for the institutional-trust decline narrative, which is measurement-fragile and more country-specific than usually presented. TIER 3 for strong causal claims about misinformation, where the research community is genuinely split.**

> Written from general knowledge on 2026-09-01 without live source verification.

---

## The asymmetry

Trust builds slowly and collapses quickly. This is not a psychological quirk; there are at least four structural reasons, and they compound.

| Mechanism | Description |
|---|---|
| **Diagnosticity asymmetry** | A single act of betrayal is highly informative about untrustworthiness; a single act of honesty is weakly informative about trustworthiness, because dishonest people behave honestly whenever it is cheap. Bayesian updating is therefore inherently lopsided |
| **Negativity bias in attention and memory** | Negative, trust-destroying events are more salient, more memorable, more newsworthy and weighted more heavily. Paul Slovic set this out clearly in "Perceived Risk, Trust, and Democracy" (1993) as the **asymmetry principle** |
| **Self-sealing distrust** | Trust is tested by extending it. Distrust means withholding, which removes the interactions that could generate disconfirming evidence. **Distrust protects itself from refutation in a way trust does not** |
| **Verification asymmetry** | Confirming trustworthiness requires many observations; confirming untrustworthiness can require one. The evidence-gathering costs are wildly unequal |

Slovic's further point, which matters for regulators and institutions specifically: trust-destroying events are more visible than trust-building ones (an accident is news; a safe year is not), sources of bad news are seen as more credible than sources of good news, and once distrust is established it inhibits the contact needed to rebuild.

**Practical consequence:** any system that depends on trust needs deliberate trust-*maintenance* mechanisms, because the natural drift is downward. Reliability compounding slowly and one visible betrayal resetting it is the default dynamic, not the pathological one.

## Distrust spirals

The generic structure appears at every scale.

1. A takes a defensive action.
2. B cannot distinguish defence from preparation for aggression.
3. B takes a defensive action.
4. A reads B's action as confirming the original suspicion.
5. Return to 1, with higher amplitude.

In international relations this is the **security dilemma** (John Herz, 1950; developed by Robert Jervis, 1978): measures that increase one state's security decrease another's, and the offence–defence balance determines how vicious the dynamic gets. In organisations, Roderick Kramer described **paranoid cognition** — hypervigilance, over-attribution of intent, and rumination — as a predictable consequence of low status and uncertainty rather than a personality trait: people who feel scrutinised and cannot get feedback generate malign explanations.

**What breaks a spiral:** costly, unambiguous, unilateral signals that are hard to explain as tactical. Charles Osgood's GRIT (graduated reciprocation in tension reduction) formalised this — announce a concession, make it, invite reciprocation, and continue for a defined period regardless of response. It maps onto the generous-tit-for-tat result in [why-cooperation-exists.md](why-cooperation-exists.md): under noise, the strategy that recovers is the one that forgives more than strict reciprocity would.

## Polarisation

The clearest measured change in Western democracies is not ideological but **affective**: Americans' stated warmth toward the opposing party has fallen far more than their positions have diverged.

- Iyengar, Sood and Lelkes (2012) documented the rise in affective polarisation using feeling thermometers and social-distance items (would you be unhappy if your child married someone from the other party — a measure that moved enormously between the 1960s and the 2010s).
- Iyengar and Westwood (2015) found, in implicit-association and allocation tasks, that partisan discrimination **exceeded** racial discrimination on some measures — a striking and much-replicated result, though the interpretation is disputed because partisanship is a chosen identity that people believe it is legitimate to judge.

**The complication that cuts against the standard internet explanation:** Boxell, Gentzkow and Shapiro (2017) found that polarisation in the US rose *fastest among the oldest demographic groups*, who use the internet and social media least. If social media were the primary driver, the age gradient should run the other way. This does not exonerate social media — it may work through elite and mass-media channels rather than direct exposure — but it rules out the simplest version of the story.

Other candidate drivers with reasonable support: **partisan sorting** (parties becoming ideologically, racially and geographically homogeneous, so that party now bundles many identities); cable and talk-radio fragmentation, which predates the internet; primary systems that reward extremity; and geographic sorting. Note that polarisation trends differ substantially across democracies, which is hard to reconcile with a global technological cause.

## Is institutional trust really collapsing?

This is the claim most in need of deflation. What can be said honestly:

**What is well supported:**

- In the **United States**, the Gallup confidence-in-institutions series (running since 1973) shows large declines for Congress, newspapers, television news, organised religion and the criminal justice system. Congressional confidence has at times fallen into single digits from around 40% in the mid-1970s.
- Trust in *some* institutions has become **sharply partisan-differentiated**, most visibly for science and public health after 2020, where aggregate stability masked divergence between partisan groups moving in opposite directions.
- Trust in **news media** has fallen across many countries, and this trend is more international than the others.

**What is much weaker than usually stated:**

- **The trend is not universal.** Eurobarometer and national series show flat or rising trust in several European countries over comparable periods, with sharp shocks (the eurozone crisis in southern Europe, for example) rather than secular decline. Nordic institutional trust has been high and stable.
- **It is institution-specific.** Militaries, police (in some countries), small businesses, scientists and universities have quite different trajectories. Aggregating destroys the signal, and the aggregate is what gets reported.
- **The measurement is genuinely hard.** Question wording changes; "confidence in the people running" an institution is not the same as trust in the institution; response rates for probability samples have collapsed over exactly the period in question, and differential non-response by the disaffected is a real threat to the time series in either direction.
- **The commercial trackers are weak evidence.** The Edelman Trust Barometer is widely quoted, and is based on non-probability online samples of a skewed population, produced by a public relations firm with an interest in the topic being urgent. It is not comparable to Gallup, ESS or Eurobarometer, and should not be cited alongside them as if it were.
- **"Critical citizens" (Pippa Norris, 1999).** Declining *deference* toward institutions may reflect rising education, rising expectations and better information, rather than eroding legitimacy. Citizens who support democratic principles strongly while rating their government's performance harshly are not a pathology. **Caveat: this distinction is comfortable and is at risk of being unfalsifiable** — it can absorb any observed decline. It needs behavioural rather than attitudinal tests (compliance, participation, willingness to accept adverse outcomes).

**Honest summary: "institutional trust is collapsing" is a US-weighted, institution-specific, measurement-fragile claim that is stated with far more confidence than the data support.** Something real is happening; it is not the thing usually described.

## Declining interpersonal trust

The US generalised-trust decline (GSS) is real, from roughly half in the early 1970s to around a third. The important structural finding: it appears to be substantially **cohort replacement** rather than within-person change. Successive birth cohorts enter adulthood with lower trust and retain their level; the aggregate falls as older cohorts die.

If that decomposition is right, it changes the question entirely. It is not "why did Americans become less trusting?" but "what changed in the formative environment of successive cohorts?" Candidate answers include rising inequality, declining residential stability, changed childhood socialisation, and — following the institutions-first logic in [high-trust-and-low-trust.md](high-trust-and-low-trust.md) — reduced experience of impartial, reliable public administration during formative years. None is established.

Putnam's *Bowling Alone* (2000) attributed the decline in associational life primarily to generational replacement and television. **The critique:** the measures conflate formal membership with actual social connection, and new forms of association that do not look like a bowling league or a Rotary club were not counted. Whether associational life declined or changed form is still argued.

## Misinformation, handled carefully

**This is the section where the gap between public discussion and the research literature is widest.** The strong claim — that online misinformation is a primary cause of polarisation and democratic decline — is substantially more contested among researchers who study it than public discussion suggests.

**Evidence for minimal measured effects:**

| Study | Finding |
|---|---|
| Allcott & Gentzkow (2017) | Estimated exposure to fake news in the 2016 US election was small in absolute terms — on the order of one remembered fake article per adult on average — and tiny relative to total news consumption |
| Grinberg et al. (2019, *Science*) | On Twitter during the 2016 campaign, roughly 0.1% of users accounted for around 80% of fake-news *sharing*, and about 1% accounted for 80% of *exposures*. Consumption is extraordinarily concentrated in a small, already-partisan, mostly older group |
| Guess, Nyhan & Reifler and related work | Same concentration pattern in browsing data; the modal person sees essentially none |
| Bakshy, Messing & Adamic (2015, *Science*) | On Facebook, individual choice about what to click reduced cross-cutting exposure more than the algorithm did. **Caveat: authored by Facebook researchers on a non-representative subsample of users who self-identified their politics** |
| The 2020 US election collaboration with Meta (published 2023 in *Science* and *Nature*) | Experimentally changing feeds — chronological ordering, removing reshares, reducing like-minded content — produced large changes in what people saw and **near-zero measured effects** on polarisation, issue attitudes or political participation over roughly three months |
| Budak, Nyhan, Rothschild and colleagues (2024, *Nature*) | A review arguing that the harms of online misinformation are widely misunderstood: exposure is low and concentrated, persuasion effects are small, and the evidence does not support the dominant public narrative |

**The steelman for the strong claim, which deserves to be taken seriously:**

- **Short horizons.** The experiments run for weeks or months. Attitude formation runs over years. Null results over three months do not rule out cumulative effects.
- **Wrong outcome variables.** Measured effects on individual attitudes miss **agenda-setting, elite behaviour and coordination**. Misinformation may matter mainly by changing what politicians believe they can say and what activists can mobilise around, not by converting individuals.
- **US and Twitter-centric.** The evidence base is heavily American and heavily platform-cooperative. The most severe documented harms are elsewhere: WhatsApp-driven rumour lynchings in India, and Facebook's role in Myanmar, where the UN independent fact-finding mission concluded the platform played a determining role in the incitement against the Rohingya. Those are not small effects.
- **Platform cooperation is a selection problem.** Studies conducted with platform data and platform co-authorship on platform-chosen questions have obvious limitations, whatever the integrity of the individual researchers.
- **Supply-side effects.** Even if few people are persuaded, the *existence* of a well-funded misinformation supply changes the incentives of mainstream actors and the cost of establishing shared facts.

**My reading:** the strong direct-persuasion claim is not supported. The weaker claims — that misinformation matters through elite cues, through mobilisation of an already-committed minority, and catastrophically in specific low-institutional-capacity contexts — survive. Public discussion has settled on the version with the least evidence.

**A related cautionary case: the backfire effect.** Nyhan and Reifler (2010) reported that corrections could *strengthen* the original misperception among those predisposed to believe it. This became conventional wisdom about the futility of fact-checking. Wood and Porter's "The Elusive Backfire Effect" (published around 2019) tested corrections across dozens of issues with large samples and found the effect essentially absent — corrections generally move beliefs in the right direction, including among partisans. Nyhan has publicly acknowledged the revision. **A widely-repeated finding about the hopelessness of correcting misinformation was itself misinformation that survived for a decade.**

## What actually predicts institutional trust

Where the evidence converges reasonably well:

| Predictor | Direction | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Perceived **procedural fairness** — being treated respectfully and impartially, independent of the outcome (Tom Tyler's work on legitimacy and policing) | Positive | Strong and unusually well replicated |
| **Corruption** experienced or perceived | Negative | Strong |
| **Performance** on core function (service delivery, safety) | Positive | Moderate |
| **Economic conditions** | Positive | Moderate; institutional trust is partly a mood variable |
| **Inequality** | Negative | Moderate; contested magnitude |
| Media consumption of political news | Ambiguous | Weak and channel-dependent |

Tyler's procedural justice result is the most actionable thing in this file: **people's willingness to accept and comply with authority depends more on whether they were treated fairly than on whether they got what they wanted.** That is a design instruction, not an attitude survey.

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"Trust everywhere is collapsing."** It is falling in some countries, for some institutions, on some measures. The universal-collapse story is a US story generalised without checking.
- **"Social media caused polarisation."** The age gradient runs the wrong way for the simple version, and cross-national trends diverge. Sorting, media fragmentation and primary systems have at least as good a claim.
- **"People are drowning in misinformation."** Exposure is extraordinarily concentrated; most people see almost none. The concentration is itself the important finding, and it points to different interventions than the mass-exposure model does.
- **"Correcting false beliefs backfires."** The backfire effect largely failed to replicate. Corrections generally work, modestly.
- **"Distrust is irrational."** In genuinely untrustworthy environments distrust is accurate. The pathology is *miscalibration*, in either direction, and over-trust has its own casualty list.
- **"Rebuilding trust requires communication."** Communication without changed behaviour accelerates the decline, because it supplies fresh evidence of the gap between stated and actual conduct. Trust is rebuilt by costly, verifiable, repeated action.

## Open questions

- [ ] Is the US generalised-trust decline genuinely cohort replacement, and if so what changed in successive formative environments?
- [ ] How much of the measured institutional-trust decline is an artefact of collapsing survey response rates and differential non-response?
- [ ] Can "critical citizenship" be distinguished from legitimacy erosion by any behavioural test?
- [ ] Do misinformation exposure effects accumulate over years in a way that short experiments cannot detect? What design would show this?
- [ ] Why does affective polarisation vary so much across democracies with similar media environments?
- [ ] What is the empirical half-life of trust after a betrayal, and does anything reliably shorten it besides costly verifiable behaviour?
- [ ] Does procedural justice generalise beyond policing and courts to regulators, employers and platforms?

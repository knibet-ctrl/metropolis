# The Control Group

Institutional failures and cooperation breakdowns. For each: which mechanism failed.

> **Status: TIER 1 for the facts of what happened. TIER 2 for the diagnoses — attributing a failure to a specific mechanism is an interpretive act and reasonable people assign different weights.**

> Written from general knowledge on 2026-09-01 without live source verification. Casualty figures in particular are contested in the primary literature; ranges below are recalled and should be treated as indicative.

---

## Why this file exists

Almost everything written about institutions studies successes. Ostrom studied systems that lasted centuries. Acemoglu and Robinson explain why some countries got rich. Management literature interviews firms that are currently doing well. **This is survivorship bias operating at civilisational scale**, and it produces advice that cannot be evaluated because the base rate is unknown.

The failures below are chosen because each one isolates a different mechanism. Read this file before [practices.md](practices.md).

## 1. Soviet collectivisation, 1929–1933

**What happened.** Stalin's forced collectivisation abolished private peasant farming, merged holdings into collective and state farms, and deported or killed the *kulaks* as a class. Grain procurement quotas were maintained and in places increased while output collapsed. Famine followed: the Holodomor in Ukraine (1932–33), with estimates commonly in the range of 3.5–5 million deaths there and higher figures for the USSR as a whole; and the Kazakh famine (*Asharshylyk*), which killed on the order of 1.5 million people — perhaps 38% of the Kazakh population — as pastoral nomads were forcibly sedentarised.

**Mechanisms that failed:**

| Mechanism | How it failed |
|---|---|
| **Local knowledge destroyed** | Peasant agriculture encoded generations of knowledge about specific soils, microclimates and timings. Central planning could not represent this, so it was overwritten. James Scott's *Seeing Like a State* (1998) calls this the destruction of *mētis* — practical, local, unarticulated knowledge — by **high modernist** schemes that value legibility over function |
| **Existing informal institutions abolished** | The *mir* / village commune was a functioning common-property institution with rules for land redistribution and mutual insurance. It was dismantled, not reformed |
| **Monitoring impossible, so terror substituted** | With no way to observe effort on a collective farm, the state substituted coercion for incentive. This produces compliance, not productivity — the distinction in [the-questions.md](the-questions.md) |
| **Error correction closed** | Reporting shortfalls was punished as sabotage or defeatism. Information flowed upward only in the direction that was safe to send, so the centre acted on a fiction |
| **No exit, no voice** | Internal passports restricted movement; there was no channel to contest quotas |

Scott's four conditions for catastrophe — an administrative ambition to make society legible, a high-modernist ideology, an authoritarian state willing to use coercion, and a prostrate civil society unable to resist — are all present. **The Great Leap Forward (1958–62) is the same pattern with a different vocabulary**, with excess-death estimates ranging from roughly 30 million (Yang Jisheng) to 45 million (Frank Dikötter), the higher figures contested.

## 2. State-building in Afghanistan, 2001–2021

**What happened.** Roughly $145 billion in US reconstruction funding over two decades, plus far more in military spending. A centralised constitution adopted in 2004. An Afghan National Defence and Security Force built to a nominal strength of several hundred thousand. In August 2021 that force collapsed in a matter of weeks. The Special Inspector General for Afghanistan Reconstruction (SIGAR) published a long series of reports documenting the failure in detail; the *Washington Post*'s "Afghanistan Papers" (2019) published interviews in which officials described knowing the effort was failing while reporting progress.

**Mechanisms that failed:**

| Mechanism | How it failed |
|---|---|
| **Form without function** | Ministries, a constitution, an electoral commission, an anti-corruption body — all present on paper. Pritchett, Woolcock and Andrews's **isomorphic mimicry** in its purest observed form |
| **Absorption capacity exceeded** | Money flowed far faster than institutions could use it, and the excess was captured. "Ghost soldiers" and ghost schools existed because payroll fraud was easier than delivery and the auditors were remote |
| **Accountability link severed** | A state funded almost entirely from outside does not need its citizens' consent or taxes. The rentier dynamic points state attention at donors, not at the population |
| **Wrong institutional design for the polity** | A highly centralised presidency with Kabul appointing provincial governors, imposed on a society organised around local and tribal authority. This maximised the value of capturing the centre and gave losers nothing |
| **Principal–agent chains too long** | US agency → prime contractor → subcontractor → local partner. Each link had different incentives and better local information than the level above |
| **Monitoring metrics gamed** | Progress reported in inputs and counts (schools built, troops trained) because outputs were unmeasurable. Multitasking failure at national scale |

## 3. Iraq, 2003

**What happened.** Two decisions by the Coalition Provisional Authority under Paul Bremer in May 2003: **CPA Order 1**, de-Ba'athification, removing senior party members from public employment; and **CPA Order 2**, dissolving the Iraqi army and security apparatus — on the order of 400,000 men, disbanded without pay, retaining their weapons and their organisational networks.

**Mechanisms that failed:**

- **The only functioning institutions were eliminated.** Party membership had been a requirement for professional advancement, so de-Ba'athification removed teachers, engineers, hospital administrators and civil servants along with regime loyalists. State capacity went to near zero overnight.
- **No credible commitment to the losers.** A political transition requires the outgoing group to believe they will not be destroyed. Nothing offered that assurance, so violent resistance was the rational option. This is the credible-commitment problem from [practices.md](practices.md) failing at maximum stakes.
- **The violence settlement was destroyed without replacement.** North, Wallis and Weingast's point: an extractive order contains violence by giving armed groups a stake. Removing the stake without removing the arms produces exactly what followed.
- **Enforcement vacuum filled by militias.** As predicted by the mechanism in [rules-and-enforcement.md](rules-and-enforcement.md): demand for protection and dispute resolution did not vanish; it was supplied by sectarian militias.

**Steelman:** defenders argue the army had already largely self-demobilised, that reconstituting it would have entrenched sectarian dominance, and that de-Ba'athification was politically necessary for Shia and Kurdish participation. These are serious points. They argue about *how* the transition should have been managed, not against the diagnosis that state capacity and credible commitment were both destroyed.

## 4. Structural adjustment, 1980s–1990s

**What happened.** IMF and World Bank lending conditional on liberalisation, privatisation, fiscal contraction and deregulation, applied across Latin America, Africa and later the post-Soviet bloc. John Williamson coined "Washington Consensus" in 1989 to describe a specific list, and spent years complaining that the term had been repurposed as a label for market fundamentalism he did not endorse.

**Mechanisms that failed:**

- **Institutional monocropping** (Peter Evans): one blueprint applied regardless of context.
- **Sequencing ignored.** Privatisation without competition law, bankruptcy procedure, securities regulation or contract enforcement does not create markets; it creates asset transfers to whoever is positioned to take them.
- **Complementary institutions absent.** Price liberalisation without functioning distribution; capital account opening without banking supervision.
- **Russia is the clearest case.** Voucher privatisation (1992–94) followed by loans-for-shares (1995) transferred the commanding heights of the economy to a small group at a fraction of value. The result was not a market economy but an oligarchic one, plus a state too weak to enforce the property rights it had created — which is why organised crime supplied enforcement (see Varese in [rules-and-enforcement.md](rules-and-enforcement.md)). Stuckler, King and McKee (2009, *Lancet*) linked rapid mass privatisation to increased male mortality; the finding was contested by Earle, Gehlbach and others on measurement and specification grounds. **The mortality magnitude is disputed; the institutional failure is not.**

**Steelman, which is stronger than the critics usually allow:** many of the adjusting countries had genuinely unsustainable fiscal and monetary positions, with hyperinflation, unpayable debt and collapsing terms of trade. The counterfactual is not "no crisis"; it is a differently-managed crisis. Some adjustments — macro stabilisation in particular — worked. The failures cluster in the structural and privatisation components, not the stabilisation ones, and that distinction is usually flattened by both sides.

## 5. Somalia after 1991

**What happened.** The Siad Barre state collapsed in 1991. No functioning central government for roughly two decades. Fourteen or more internationally-sponsored transitional authorities failed in succession.

**The genuinely interesting part is what functioned anyway:**

| What worked | Why |
|---|---|
| **Telecommunications** | Somalia developed cheap, widely-available mobile and international telephony — companies like Hormuud and Telcom Somalia — because the industry needs relatively little from a state and could arrange its own interconnection agreements |
| **Money transfer (*hawala*)** | Remittance networks moved very large sums on reputation and clan-backed obligation, with no formal enforcement |
| **Customary law (*xeer*)** | Clan-based dispute resolution with *diya*-paying groups holding collective liability for members' offences — a functioning tort and contract system, with elders as adjudicators |
| **Livestock export** | Continued substantially, with quality assurance handled by traders' reputational networks |

Peter Leeson's "Better off stateless" (2007) argued that on several indicators Somalia improved *relative to* the late Barre period — but the honest framing is that the baseline was a predatory state, so this is a comparison between two bad options, not evidence for statelessness. Life expectancy, security and any indicator requiring public goods stayed catastrophic.

**The comparison that carries the real lesson: Somaliland.** In the north, a series of locally-organised clan conferences between 1991 and 1997 — Borama in 1993 the most cited — produced a hybrid polity with an elected assembly and an upper house of elders, largely without external funding or design. It has held elections, including ones in which incumbents lost and left. It remains internationally unrecognised, and therefore received almost none of the aid poured into failed processes for Mogadishu.

**Mechanism contrast:** locally-generated legitimacy with local resources versus externally-conferred authority with external resources. The unfunded, unrecognised process worked; the funded, recognised ones did not. That is uncomfortable for the entire aid model and it is a single case, so do not over-extract from it — but it is the single strongest natural comparison available.

## 6. Enron, 2001

**What happened.** Special purpose entities used to move debt off balance sheet; mark-to-market accounting on long-dated contracts allowing profits to be booked from estimates; CFO Andrew Fastow running LJM partnerships that transacted with Enron while he was on both sides. Bankruptcy in December 2001. Arthur Andersen destroyed.

**Mechanisms that failed — this is a case about monitors, not about accounting:**

| Monitor | Why it failed |
|---|---|
| **Auditor** | Andersen's consulting revenue from Enron was comparable to or larger than its audit fee. **The monitored party paid the monitor and could fire it.** This is the structural flaw, not a lapse of professional ethics |
| **Board audit committee** | Waived the conflict-of-interest rules to permit the Fastow partnerships. Formally independent, functionally captive |
| **Analysts** | Employed by banks with investment banking relationships with Enron. Same payer problem |
| **Internal error correction** | Sherron Watkins raised concerns internally in 2001; they were routed to the general counsel — Enron's own outside counsel had helped structure the deals. The reporting channel led back to the people being reported on. Compare the ASRS design in [organisational-trust.md](organisational-trust.md), where reports go to a body with no enforcement power over the reporter |
| **Internal incentives** | "Rank and yank" forced-ranking performance review, with the bottom decile removed annually, which selects hard for aggressive reported numbers |

**The identical mechanism reappeared in 2008:** credit rating agencies were paid by the issuers whose securities they rated. Same structure, larger consequences, seven years later.

## 7. Wells Fargo, roughly 2002–2016

**What happened.** Aggressive cross-selling targets ("Eight is Great" — eight products per household). Employees opened on the order of 3.5 million accounts and cards customers had not authorised. A $185 million settlement with the CFPB and others in 2016, a $3 billion settlement in 2020, and the former CEO barred from the industry. Roughly 5,300 employees were fired over the conduct.

**Mechanism:** a textbook Holmström–Milgrom multitasking failure. The measurable dimension (products sold) was incentivised to the point where the unmeasurable dimensions (customer benefit, consent, honesty) were sacrificed. Compounded by a closed error channel — employees who reported the practices or called the ethics line described being terminated — and by the firing of thousands of low-level staff while the target system remained in place, which is the organisational equivalent of blaming the operator.

**Why it belongs here rather than in a fraud file: the *number* of people involved is the evidence.** No plausible theory of individual character explains 5,300 employees at one bank. The structure was the cause.

## 8. Boeing 737 MAX, 2018–2019 (and after)

**What happened.** MCAS, a flight control system added to compensate for handling changes from repositioned larger engines, was capable of repeatedly commanding nose-down trim based on input from a **single angle-of-attack sensor**, with no redundancy and no requirement for pilot familiarisation. Lion Air flight 610 crashed in October 2018 and Ethiopian Airlines flight 302 in March 2019. 346 people died.

**Mechanisms that failed:**

| Mechanism | How it failed |
|---|---|
| **Regulator captured / hollowed** | Certification tasks were substantially delegated to Boeing employees under the Organization Designation Authorization scheme. The regulator lacked the resources and the independence to check the work |
| **Commercial requirement drove a safety decision** | Avoiding simulator training for pilots was worth a great deal commercially — reportedly including per-aircraft rebate commitments to a major customer if simulator training proved necessary. This created a powerful reason to keep MCAS out of the manuals and out of differences training |
| **Error correction suppressed** | Engineers raised concerns; internal messages later disclosed showed employees discussing regulators and the aircraft in terms that indicate they knew and did not escalate effectively. The andon cord existed on paper and was not pullable in practice |
| **Culture change traceable to structure** | The 1997 McDonnell Douglas merger and the 2001 relocation of headquarters from Seattle to Chicago — physically separating executive leadership from engineering — are widely cited as the transition from an engineering-led to a finance-led organisation. The 2020 House Transportation and Infrastructure Committee report documented a pattern of production pressure over safety concerns |
| **The pattern did not stop** | The January 2024 Alaska Airlines door plug departure, where bolts were missing and **no record existed of the panel's removal and reinstallation**, indicated the quality and traceability failures were live, not historical. The FAA capped production in response |

Boeing is the cleanest available demonstration that [../leaders/](../leaders/) mechanism #13 is not a soft management concern. Its absence is measured in fatalities.

## 9. Form without function: the general case

| Transplanted form | Where | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Anti-corruption commissions** | Well over a hundred countries | Almost no measurable success outside Hong Kong and Singapore, where the reform was simultaneous, credible at the top, and paired with pay and regulatory simplification. Elsewhere: a new organisation inside the same equilibrium |
| **Central bank independence statutes** | Widely adopted from the 1990s | Independence on paper coexisting with fiscal dominance in practice where the underlying political settlement did not support it |
| **Constitutions with extensive rights guarantees** | Widely copied | Rights that no court will enforce and no executive fears |
| **Cooperative and mutual structures** | Development programmes worldwide | Externally-imposed cooperatives systematically underperform locally-generated ones, violating Ostrom's principle 3 by construction |
| **Microfinance** | Global, from the 1990s | The set of randomised evaluations published around 2015 across six countries found modest, real, but non-transformative effects — increased business investment, little change in consumption, income, health, education or women's empowerment. The claims made for it in the 1990s and 2000s were far in excess of the eventual evidence. The Andhra Pradesh crisis of 2010, with mass over-indebtedness and coercive collection, showed the failure mode when growth outran the informal enforcement the model depended on |
| **Corporate ethics programmes** | Everywhere | Enron had an award-winning code of ethics. The board waived it |

## The failure taxonomy

| Failure type | Signature | Cases |
|---|---|---|
| **Form without function** | Institution exists, produces no behaviour change | Afghanistan, anti-corruption commissions, ethics codes |
| **Monitor captured by the monitored** | Gatekeeper paid or appointable by the party it checks | Enron/Andersen, ratings agencies, FAA/ODA |
| **Error channel closed** | Bad news cannot travel upward at acceptable personal cost | Collectivisation, Wells Fargo, Boeing, Enron |
| **Multitasking distortion** | One measurable dimension maximised, others destroyed | Wells Fargo, quota systems, test-score gaming |
| **Enforcement vacuum** | Old enforcer removed, demand persists, worst supplier fills it | Iraq, post-Soviet Russia, Sicily 1860s |
| **Local knowledge overwritten** | Legibility imposed at the cost of function | Collectivisation, Gal Oya, engineer-built irrigation |
| **Sequencing error** | Reform applied before the complementary institution exists | Structural adjustment, mass privatisation |
| **No credible commitment to losers** | Those who must give up power get no assurance | Iraq de-Ba'athification |
| **Rents were containing violence** | Reform removes the settlement that kept the armed quiet | Iraq, several African transitions |

**The most common single failure across all nine cases is the closed error channel.** It appears in seven of them. That is the strongest empirical argument in this folder for prioritising error correction over almost everything else.

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"These failures were caused by bad or corrupt individuals."** In most cases the people were unremarkable and the structure was decisive. 5,300 fired Wells Fargo employees is a structural fact.
- **"The problem was insufficient oversight."** Enron, Boeing and Afghanistan all had extensive formal oversight. The problem was that the overseers were paid, appointed or informed by the overseen.
- **"Failed states need state-building."** Somaliland built a state with no money and no recognition; Afghanistan received $145 billion and did not. Resources are not the binding constraint; legitimacy and local ownership plausibly are.
- **"Collectivisation failed because communism doesn't work."** True but useless as a diagnosis. The specific and generalisable failures — destroying local knowledge, substituting coercion for incentive where monitoring is impossible, closing the information channel — recur in capitalist organisations and are visible in Wells Fargo and Boeing.
- **"Microfinance lifted millions out of poverty."** The randomised evidence found modest effects. The claim outran the evidence for roughly two decades and the correction is still not widely absorbed.
- **"We just need to implement best practice."** Every case in this file involves implementing something that worked somewhere else.

## Open questions

- [ ] What is the base rate of institutional transplant success? Nobody appears to have a denominator.
- [ ] Is the Somaliland–Somalia comparison generalisable, or is it a single case being asked to carry an ideological load?
- [ ] Can a monitor ever be structurally independent when the monitored party has any influence over its funding? What designs actually achieve this?
- [ ] Why did GM fail to propagate NUMMI's methods to its own plants for over a decade? That is a failure of internal transplantation with the answer available for free.
- [ ] Is "closed error channel" a genuinely distinct mechanism or a downstream symptom of the others?
- [ ] What would a proper evaluation of PDIA-style adaptive reform look like, and has anyone run one?
- [ ] How much of the structural adjustment failure was design and how much was implementation by governments with no interest in it succeeding?

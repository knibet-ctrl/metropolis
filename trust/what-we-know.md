# What We Know

The confidence ledger for trust, cooperation and institutions.

> **Status: this file is the tier assignment itself.** Everything below is my judgement of the evidential standing of claims made elsewhere in this folder.

> Written from general knowledge on 2026-09-01 without live source verification. Tier assignments are judgements, not consensus positions; a specialist would move several of them.

---

## Tier definitions

| Tier | Meaning | How to treat it |
|---|---|---|
| **TIER 1** | Established. Multiple methods, multiple settings, survives serious attack | Act on it |
| **TIER 2** | Contested but tractable. Real evidence, real disagreement, resolvable in principle by better data | Act on it provisionally; track the dispute |
| **TIER 3** | Speculative. Plausible mechanism, thin or fragile evidence | Hold as a hypothesis; do not build on it |
| **TIER 4** | Unanswerable as posed, or malformed | Reformulate the question |

## TIER 1 — Established

| Claim | Basis | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| One-shot prisoner's dilemmas have mutual defection as the dominant-strategy equilibrium | Formal result | Says nothing about how often real situations have this structure |
| Repetition with a sufficiently long shadow of the future makes cooperation sustainable — **and also makes almost everything else sustainable** | Folk theorem | Indeterminacy result, not a prediction |
| Cooperation collapses in known final periods; backward induction unravels it | Theory plus consistent experimental confirmation | Real and exploitable — "last year in office" is a genuine risk category |
| Most people are **conditional cooperators**: they contribute if they expect others to and withdraw on observing free-riding | Public goods experiments across many populations | Makes cooperation bistable; expectations are self-fulfilling |
| Long-enduring, self-governed common-pool resource institutions exist and are not rare | Ostrom's case corpus and coded databases | Selection on surviving cases |
| Hardin's tragedy describes **open access without governance**, not a governed commons | Definitional plus historical evidence on regulated commons | The open-access version is real and severe |
| **Certainty and swiftness of sanction deter; severity does very little at the margin** | Large criminology literature, replicated | Mostly criminal justice; generalisation to organisations is plausible but less tested |
| Kin selection explains nepotism gradients | Hamilton's rule; extensive comparative biology | Cannot explain cooperation with strangers |
| Direct and indirect reciprocity sustain cooperation where interaction repeats and reputation circulates | Theory, experiments, field cases | Requires identifiability plus a communication channel |
| **Procedural fairness predicts acceptance of authority more than outcome favourability** | Tyler and successors; unusually well replicated across settings | Best evidenced in policing and courts |
| Reputation requires identifiability, a communication channel, **and** counterparties able to act | Analytic plus repeated failure cases | Condition three fails most often and is least discussed |
| Institutional quality correlates strongly with income, growth and low corruption | Overwhelming correlational evidence | Correlation only — causality is TIER 2 |
| Strengthening incentives on measurable tasks degrades unmeasured ones | Holmström–Milgrom multitasking; abundant field confirmation | Direction certain; magnitude context-specific |
| Structures and systems change behaviour more reliably than exhortation does | NUMMI, Fisman–Miguel, deterrence literature, Wells Fargo | The most useful single finding in this folder |

## TIER 2 — Contested but tractable

| Claim | Where the dispute is | Which way I lean |
|---|---|---|
| Institutions cause growth (AJR) | Settler-mortality instrument quality (Albouy); exclusion restriction; human capital alternative (Glaeser et al.); East Asia | Institutions matter a great deal; the published magnitudes are far less precise than stated |
| **Institutions cause trust more than trust causes institutions** | Algan–Cahuc inherited-trust evidence points the other way | Institutions-first, but this is the least secure confident claim in the folder |
| Ostrom's eight design principles discriminate durable from fragile systems | Cox et al. (2010) broadly supportive; formulation of principles 1, 2, 4 needs splitting; no out-of-sample test | Supported, needs a denominator |
| Corruption is a coordination equilibrium rather than an aggregate of individual failings | Persson–Rothstein–Teorell vs the principal–agent orthodoxy | Strongly toward equilibrium; it explains the reform failure record |
| Psychological safety improves team learning and performance | Predominantly cross-sectional, self-report on both sides, common-method variance, little experimental work | Real, moderate, causally under-identified |
| Generalised trust raises growth | Knack–Keefer correlations; identification weak | Probably some effect; probably smaller than headline estimates |
| Motivation crowding-out is real | Narrow version (tangible expected rewards on interesting self-signalling tasks) survives; broad version does not | Narrow version yes; "incentives backfire" no |
| Historical events have persistent institutional effects centuries later | **Kelly's spatial autocorrelation critique threatens much of the genre** | Some results real (Dell's mita RD is well designed); many untested against the objection |
| Diversity reduces local trust | Small effects, neighbourhood scale, confounded with deprivation; meta-analysis close to null for generalised trust | Small, local, mostly not about generalised trust; politically over-used in both directions |
| Institutional trust is declining in Western democracies | US-heavy, institution-specific, wording-sensitive, confounded with response-rate collapse | Something real; much narrower than the narrative |
| Private ordering (guilds, law merchant, Maghribi coalition) was efficiency-enhancing | Ogilvie's rent-seeking critique; Edwards–Ogilvie vs Greif | Mixed; assume rent extraction until shown otherwise |
| Punishment sustains cooperation | Antisocial punishment (Herrmann et al.); net welfare often negative (Dreber et al.) | Works where civic norms and rule of law are strong; not universal |
| Team trust improves performance | Moderated heavily by task interdependence | Real where work is genuinely interdependent, small where it is not |

## TIER 3 — Speculative

| Claim | Why it is speculative |
|---|---|
| Ostrom's principles scale to global commons (climate) | Global carbon lacks boundaries, exclusion, individual monitoring and graduated sanctions — the preconditions the principles depend on. Polycentric climate governance is an argument by analogy |
| Moralising "big gods" expanded the scale of cooperation | The Seshat paper claiming the reverse sequence was retracted; the underlying question is open in both directions |
| Genetic group selection explains human cooperation | Fiercely contested; cultural group selection is more defensible |
| Social media caused affective polarisation | The age gradient runs the wrong way for the direct-exposure version; cross-national trends diverge |
| Misinformation is a primary driver of democratic decline | Measured direct effects are small; exposure is extremely concentrated. Long-horizon and elite-cue channels are plausible but untested |
| A specific number (150) bounds stable social relationships | The extrapolation's confidence interval is very wide; the qualitative ceiling survives, the number does not |
| PDIA and adaptive reform outperform conventional reform | Coherent diagnosis, promising cases, no controlled evidence |
| "Trust levels" are a stable, transportable individual disposition | The construct partly measures risk preference, betrayal aversion and own trustworthiness |
| Somaliland's bottom-up process is a generalisable model | One case, unusual conditions, carrying a heavy ideological load |
| Declining deference is "critical citizenship" rather than legitimacy erosion | Comfortable, currently near-unfalsifiable, needs behavioural tests |

## TIER 4 — Unanswerable or malformed

| Question | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| "Are humans basically cooperative or basically selfish?" | Conditional cooperation makes the dichotomy incoherent. Behaviour is structure-dependent |
| "Is trust rising or falling?" (unqualified) | Which construct, which country, which institution, which cohort. Four questions wearing one coat |
| "Which country has the best institutions?" | Depends entirely on the outcome weighted, and institutional indices are partly outcome-contaminated |
| "How much trust should I have?" (in general) | Trust is a decision under specific stakes, exit options and observability. There is no context-free answer |
| "Did culture or institutions come first?" | Co-evolutionary with feedback in both directions on different timescales. Well-posed only as: which is more manipulable over what horizon |
| "What is social capital?" | The construct bundles networks, norms, trust and civic participation, defined incompatibly by Bourdieu, Coleman and Putnam. Frequently unfalsifiable — it is measured by its effects and then used to explain them |
| "Would society be better with more or less trust?" | Under-specified: more trust in whom, about what, and matched to what actual trustworthiness |
| "Is my organisation's culture good?" | Not measurable as posed. Decomposes into answerable questions about error reporting, incentive structure and monitoring design |

## Things people most commonly get wrong

| Common belief | What the evidence indicates |
|---|---|
| Tit-for-tat is the optimal strategy | It never wins head-to-head, its tournament win was pool-dependent, and it fails under noise. Generous reciprocity is the robust family |
| The tragedy of the commons is inevitable | It is a prediction from a model with no communication, monitoring or rules. Ostrom documented hundreds of counterexamples |
| Repeated interaction solves cooperation | It makes cooperation *possible alongside everything else*. Institutions do the selection |
| Low-trust societies have bad culture | Low trust is usually accurate perception of a genuinely untrustworthy institutional environment |
| Corruption is a moral problem | It is usually a coordination equilibrium. This is why exhortation fails and simultaneous credible change sometimes works |
| Harsher penalties deter crime | Certainty and swiftness deter; severity barely moves the margin |
| Fines and incentives always work | Multitasking distortion is near-universal; crowding-out is real in a narrow band |
| Fines backfire (the daycare study) | Ten centres. Direction is a live hypothesis; magnitude is unverified; I am not aware of a well-powered direct replication |
| Psychological safety means being nice | It means dissent is cheap. Safety without accountability is the comfort-zone failure state |
| Project Aristotle proved it | Unpublished, single company, cross-sectional, self-reported on both sides |
| Bad outcomes come from bad people | 5,300 fired Wells Fargo employees; NUMMI's identical workforce; Fisman–Miguel's diplomats after 2002 |
| More oversight prevents disasters | Enron, Boeing and Afghanistan all had extensive oversight. The failure was that monitors were paid or appointed by the monitored |
| Trust in institutions is collapsing everywhere | US-weighted, institution-specific, wording-sensitive, confounded by response-rate collapse |
| Misinformation is drowning the public | Exposure is extraordinarily concentrated in a small, already-partisan minority |
| Correcting misinformation backfires | The backfire effect largely failed to replicate. Corrections generally work, modestly |
| Oxytocin is the trust hormone | The literature largely did not survive replication and pharmacological scrutiny |
| QWERTY proves lock-in | The specific case does not survive Liebowitz and Margolis. Use payment networks instead |
| Dunbar's number is 150 | Very wide confidence interval. The ceiling is real; the number is not |
| High trust is unambiguously good | High-trust environments are more efficient and more exploitable. Affinity fraud requires trust |
| Best practice can be adopted | Every failure in the control group involved adopting something that worked elsewhere |

## Strongest convergences across methods

The claims below are supported by evidence from at least three of: formal theory, laboratory experiment, field experiment, historical case, and organisational failure analysis. **These are the load-bearing findings.**

1. **Making error reporting cheap in status terms is the highest-leverage intervention available.** Appears in Edmondson's nursing units, the andon cord and NUMMI, aviation's ASRS design, and in seven of the nine control-group failures as an absence.
2. **The monitor must not be paid, appointed or informed exclusively by the monitored.** Enron/Andersen, ratings agencies, the FAA's ODA scheme, and Ostrom's principle 4 all state the same rule from different directions.
3. **Certainty beats severity.** Deterrence research, Ostrom's graduated sanctions, and the observation that unenforced draconian rules teach that the rule system is theatre.
4. **Structure beats exhortation.** NUMMI, Fisman–Miguel's diplomats, Singapore's discretion-reduction, and the entire failure record of anti-corruption training.
5. **The shadow of the future is the master variable.** Longer expected interaction moves every mechanism in the cooperative direction; known endpoints unravel them.
6. **Coordination equilibria do not move incrementally.** Corruption, norms of contribution, and expectations about others' behaviour are self-fulfilling and require simultaneous credible change.
7. **Form is easy and function is hard.** The transplant failure record is close to universal.

# Trust, Cooperation and Institutions

Why some societies and organisations work and others don't.

> **Status: mixed tiers.** This folder spans TIER 1 (game-theoretic foundations, Ostrom's empirical cases, deterrence certainty effects) through TIER 4 (whether "social capital" is a coherent construct at all). Each file carries its own tier marker.

> **Provenance warning — read this first.** Every file in this folder was written from general knowledge on **2026-09-01**, with **no live source verification**. Author names, publication years, sample sizes, effect sizes and case details are recalled from memory. Several are probably slightly wrong. Nothing here should be cited without checking the primary source. Where I am unsure, I say so inline rather than smoothing it over.

## Why this folder exists

"Trust" is one of the worst-behaved words in social science. It is used to mean at least five different things, measured by at least four incompatible instruments, and invoked to explain outcomes ranging from GDP growth to marital satisfaction to why your standup meeting is bad. It is also politically loaded: the same body of institutional-quality research is used to argue for free markets, for social democracy, for colonial apologia and for reparations.

This folder tries to do three things:

1. **Disaggregate.** Separate the distinct phenomena that get bundled under "trust".
2. **Rank by evidence.** Say plainly what is established, what is contested, what is speculation and what is a malformed question.
3. **Include the failures.** Most writing on institutions studies survivors. The control group file is the corrective.

## Ten compressed headline findings

1. **Institutions appear to cause trust more than trust causes institutions.** The popular culturalist story — that some peoples are simply more trusting and therefore get better government — is at best half right and probably runs mostly backwards. Impartial, predictable administration generates generalised trust; the reverse pathway is weaker and harder to identify. Contested; see [high-trust-and-low-trust.md](high-trust-and-low-trust.md).
2. **Elinor Ostrom empirically refuted the general form of the tragedy of the commons.** Hardin's 1968 model correctly describes *open access without governance*. It does not describe a commons, which is by definition governed. Hundreds of centuries-old resource institutions exist and Ostrom catalogued what they share.
3. **The popular account of tit-for-tat is wrong.** It won Axelrod's tournaments on aggregate score against a particular submitted strategy pool, never beat any single opponent, and degrades badly under noise. Robust cooperation requires forgiveness beyond strict reciprocity.
4. **Corruption is usually a coordination equilibrium, not an aggregate of bad characters.** In a society where everyone expects bribery, refusing to bribe is individually costly and socially useless. This is why anti-corruption exhortation fails and why abrupt, simultaneous, credible structural change occasionally works.
5. **Reputation systems require identifiability plus a communication channel.** Where either fails — anonymity, scale, mobility, no shared language for grievance — reputation stops disciplining behaviour and something else must. The transition from personal to impersonal exchange is one of the most underrated civilisational thresholds.
6. **Monitoring can destroy the motivation it was meant to supplement.** Real, replicated in some forms, but weaker and more context-dependent than the popular "fines backfire" story implies. The famous daycare-fine study is a ten-centre field experiment, not a law of nature.
7. **Psychological safety and the andon cord are the same mechanism at different scales:** making error reporting cheap in status terms. Both work by lowering the personal cost of surfacing bad news. This is [../leaders/](../leaders/) mechanism #13.
8. **Trust is asymmetric.** It builds slowly and collapses fast, and distrust is partly self-sealing: it removes the contact that would generate disconfirming evidence. This asymmetry is structural, not a psychological quirk.
9. **"Institutional trust is collapsing" is a much weaker claim than it sounds.** The decline is US-heavy, institution-specific, sensitive to question wording, and confounded with the healthy growth of critical, non-deferential citizenship. Some institutions in some countries show no trend.
10. **Transplanting institutional form without function is the single most repeated failure in the control group.** Anti-corruption commissions, constitutions, central bank statutes, cooperative structures and audit regimes have all been copied worldwide with near-zero effect where the underlying enforcement and incentive structure was absent.

## Honest caveats

- **Replication.** Large parts of the social capital and behavioural economics literature have replication problems. The oxytocin–trust literature has substantially failed. Priming, ego depletion and several classic "trust is fragile/boostable" demonstrations did not survive. Effect sizes in the experimental economics literature that did replicate shrank by roughly half. Treat any single striking experiment in this folder as a hypothesis, not a fact. See [../knowledge/the-replication-crisis.md](../knowledge/the-replication-crisis.md).
- **Measurement.** The dominant cross-national trust instrument is one survey question about "most people". It is not clear that respondents in different societies interpret "most people" as the same reference group — a problem that could account for a large share of apparent cross-national differences.
- **Selection on the dependent variable.** Ostrom studied enduring institutions. Acemoglu–Robinson explain divergence with institutions. Both risk fitting a story to survivors. The control group file exists to partially correct this.
- **Politics.** Institutional-quality research is used to support opposing programmes: libertarian (property rights and constraint on the executive), social-democratic (universal impartial welfare administration produces trust), and post-colonial (extraction explains persistent underdevelopment). Where the disagreement is about values rather than facts, I flag it explicitly rather than pretending the evidence settles it.
- **Spatial autocorrelation.** A substantial share of the historical-persistence literature — "event in 1400 explains outcome in 2010" — may not survive proper correction for spatial correlation. This is a live and serious methodological objection, not a fringe one.

The inherited-trust-of-migrants evidence points the other way and I have given it a steelman, but a specialist would probably push back further.
- Effect sizes throughout are recalled, not checked. Several are probably wrong in the first significant figure.
- The misinformation section reflects a genuine and unresolved split among researchers. I have leaned toward the minimal-effects camp because the measured evidence supports it, but the null results are mostly short-horizon, US-centric and platform-cooperative, which is a real limitation.
- The Boeing and Wells Fargo diagnoses are reconstructions from public reporting, not from primary investigative documents.
- I have not covered: international relations and inter-state trust, trust in AI systems, blockchain/"trustless" claims, or the legal-origins literature (La Porta et al.) in any depth. All are relevant gaps.

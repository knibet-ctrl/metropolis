# Practices

What actually follows, at three scales, ranked by evidence strength.

> **Status: TIER 2 overall.** The underlying mechanisms are better established than the practices derived from them. Anything below marked *weak* is an inference from theory rather than a tested intervention, and should be treated accordingly. There is very little randomised evidence for organisation-level trust interventions and none at all for most of the personal ones.

> Written from general knowledge on 2026-09-01 without live source verification.

---

## The one finding that organises everything else

**Structural solutions beat exhortation.** This is the recurring result across every folder in this library and it is the strongest thing here.

| Exhortation | Structural equivalent | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| "Speak up if you see a problem" | A cord anyone can pull that stops the line, with no status cost | Toyota; NUMMI |
| "Report ethical concerns" | Reports go to a body with no power to punish the reporter | Aviation ASRS (NASA, not FAA) |
| "Be honest about your numbers" | Auditor paid and appointed by someone other than the audited | The inverse: Enron/Andersen, ratings agencies |
| "Don't take bribes" | Fewer discretionary decision points; pay that makes honesty affordable; prosecution that reaches the top | Singapore, Hong Kong ICAC, Georgia 2004 |
| "Take safety seriously" | Regulator with independent resources and no delegation to the regulated | The inverse: FAA ODA and the 737 MAX |
| "Trust each other" | Longer tenure, repeated interaction, visible history, transparent handover | Shadow-of-the-future effects |
| "Be a better team" | Same people, different production system | NUMMI: same workforce, worst plant to among the best |

The diagnostic question is always: **if the person I am exhorting behaves exactly as I ask, what does it cost them?** If the honest answer is "their job, their standing, or their bonus", the exhortation will fail and no amount of sincerity fixes it. Change the cost.

## Scale 1: Personal relationships

Weakest evidence base in this file. Most of it is extrapolation from mechanisms, not from tested interventions. Ranked honestly.

| Practice | Rationale | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Be reliable on small things at high frequency** | Trustworthiness is inferred from a base rate. Many small kept commitments are more informative than one large one, and cheap to supply | Moderate — follows from diagnosticity asymmetry; not directly tested |
| **Forgive more than strict reciprocity requires** | Under noise, tit-for-tat enters echo cycles. Generous reciprocity outperforms. Operationally: assume the first apparent slight was noise | Moderate — strong theoretical and simulation basis, weak field evidence in humans |
| **Extend trust in small increments, deliberately** | Trust is only tested by extension. Distrust is self-sealing: it removes the evidence that could disconfirm it | Moderate — the self-sealing mechanism is well argued |
| **Repair fast and explicitly after a rupture** | Asymmetry means damage accrues immediately; delay compounds it | Weak–moderate. **Caveat: the Gottman literature on repair attempts and the "five to one" ratio is widely cited and methodologically criticised — the predictive-accuracy claims came from models fitted to the same samples they predicted.** Treat the direction as sensible, the numbers as unsupported |
| **Increase the shadow of the future where you want cooperation** | Longer expected relationships change the payoff structure | Moderate — the mechanism is TIER 1 |
| **Notice when you are in a last-period game** | Departures, terminal illness, final transactions, one-off dealings with strangers — behaviour predictably degrades | Moderate |
| **Calibrate rather than maximise** | Optimal trust matches actual trustworthiness. Both over-trust (fraud victimisation) and under-trust (foregone cooperation) are costly | Weak as a practice, sound as a frame |
| **Distinguish "can't" from "won't"** | Competence-based and integrity-based trust have different recovery paths: apology and evidence of learning works for competence failures, far less well for integrity failures | Moderate — reasonably supported in the organisational trust-repair literature |

**Do not:** attempt to build trust through communication alone. Statements without corresponding behaviour supply evidence of the gap between the two, which accelerates the decline.

## Scale 2: Organisations

Best-supported scale, mostly through failure analysis rather than positive trials.

### Tier A — do these first

| Practice | What it does | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Build an error channel that does not run through the people being reported on** | Removes the structural reason bad news does not travel. This is the single highest-leverage item in the folder | Strong. ASRS design; the inverse in Enron, Boeing, Wells Fargo |
| **Separate the monitor's pay and appointment from the monitored** | Removes the mechanism behind most large governance failures | Strong. Enron/Andersen, ratings agencies, FAA ODA — all the same structure |
| **Before adding any metric, name what it will cause people to sacrifice** | Multitasking distortion is close to universal. If you cannot name the sacrificed dimension, you have not thought about it | Strong. Holmström–Milgrom plus an extensive failure record |
| **Make sanctions certain and small rather than severe and rare** | Certainty deters; unenforced severe rules teach that rules are theatre | Strong. Deterrence literature; Ostrom principle 5 |
| **Run blameless post-mortems with a written, circulated output** | Separates causal analysis from blame allocation, which is what allows honest causal analysis | Moderate. Widely adopted, effect size not well measured |
| **Track near-misses, not just incidents** | Incidents are rare and lagging; near-misses are frequent and leading. A rising near-miss report rate is usually good news | Moderate |

### Tier B — well founded, less directly evidenced

| Practice | Rationale |
|---|---|
| **Convert norms into affordances wherever possible** | A norm depends on the current manager's disposition; an affordance survives them. Ask of every desired behaviour: what would the andon cord for this be? |
| **Pair psychological safety with explicit accountability** | Edmondson's 2×2. Safety alone produces the comfort zone, which is a distinct failure mode with its own costs |
| **Keep teams together longer** | Lengthens the shadow of the future and lets reputational information accumulate. Constant reorganisation resets cooperation to the one-shot game |
| **Give decisions an owner and a written rationale** | Decision journals make error correction possible by preserving what was actually believed at the time, defeating hindsight reconstruction |
| **Use pre-mortems** | Gary Klein's technique: assume the project has failed and ask why. Reframing from prediction to explanation reliably surfaces objections that a "any concerns?" question does not |
| **Match trust investment to task interdependence** | The meta-analytic moderator. In genuinely parallel work, low trust costs little and effort is better spent elsewhere |
| **Watch for terminal-period behaviour** | Departing executives, sunsetting units, final contracts. Structure incentives to extend past the endpoint — deferred compensation, clawbacks, reputational stakes |

### Tier C — commonly recommended, weakly evidenced

Trust falls, offsites, personality typing, values statements, ethics training as a standalone, and engagement surveys with no consequence attached. **Enron had an award-winning ethics code.** These are not harmful in themselves; they are harmful when they substitute for the Tier A items and consume the credibility budget that a real change would need.

## Scale 3: Policy and institutional design

### Ostrom's design principles as a working checklist

Use this to diagnose any shared resource — a fishery, an aquifer, a shared codebase, a common budget, a professional standards regime.

| # | Diagnostic question | Red flag |
|---|---|---|
| 1a | Are the resource's boundaries clear? | "Everyone can use it" |
| 1b | Is membership defined — who is in, who is out, how do you join? | No membership concept at all |
| 2a | Do the rules fit the actual local conditions? | Rules copied from another site |
| 2b | Are benefits proportional to contributions? | Heavy users contribute the same as light ones |
| 3 | **Can the people affected change the rules?** | Rules set externally and unamendable — the most common violation, and the one that guarantees non-compliance |
| 4a | Is anyone monitoring the resource's condition? | No measurement |
| 4b | **Are monitors accountable to the users, or are they the users?** | Monitoring by a distant principal with no stake |
| 5 | Are sanctions graduated and actually applied? | Either no sanction, or one so severe nobody imposes it |
| 6 | Is there a fast, cheap, local way to settle disputes? | Only a slow formal process nobody uses |
| 7 | Does external authority recognise the right to self-organise? | Local rules routinely overridden |
| 8 | For large systems, is governance nested across scales? | One body responsible for everything |

Two warnings. First, **the checklist cannot be installed top-down** — imposing rules to satisfy principle 3 violates principle 3. It is a diagnostic, not a construction kit. Second, **improving the physical infrastructure can destroy the institution**, as in the Nepalese irrigation cases where concrete head-works removed the head-enders' dependence on tail-enders' labour and dissolved the bargain that had sustained cooperation. Ask what mutual dependence your improvement removes.

### Credible commitment

The general problem: you want to promise something, but you will have an incentive to break the promise later, and everyone knows it, so the promise is worth nothing today. Kydland and Prescott (1977) formalised this as **time inconsistency**; Schelling's *The Strategy of Conflict* (1960) catalogued the solutions. The mechanisms:

| Device | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Remove your own option** | Make the promise irreversible | Constitutional entrenchment; burning bridges |
| **Delegate to someone with different preferences** | Take the decision out of your hands | Central bank independence; independent inspectorates |
| **Post a hostage** | Something valuable you lose if you defect | Escrow; performance bonds; deferred compensation with clawback |
| **Make it public and specific** | Reputational cost of breaking becomes visible | Published targets with dates; pre-registration |
| **Automate** | Remove discretion at the moment of temptation | Rules-based fiscal policy; automatic stabilisers |
| **Repeat and expose** | Convert one-shot into repeated play | Long tenure; published track records |

**The honest caveat:** North and Weingast (1989) argued the Glorious Revolution constrained the Crown and made English sovereign debt credible, lowering borrowing costs. Later work (Clark, Sussman and Yafeh, Murrell and others) found interest rates did not fall promptly and the story is more complicated than the canonical version. **The credible-commitment mechanism is sound; its most famous historical illustration is contested.**

### Reform design

| Principle | Rationale | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Diagnose the equilibrium before choosing the instrument** | If corruption is a coordination equilibrium, monitoring and training will fail. If it is genuinely a principal–agent problem, they will work. These require opposite interventions | Moderate–strong |
| **Break coordination equilibria simultaneously and visibly, not incrementally** | Expectations do not move gradually. Hong Kong ICAC, Singapore, Georgia 2004 | Moderate. **Uncomfortable caveat: this requires concentrated power, which is dangerous for other reasons. The trade-off is real and unresolved** |
| **Reduce the number of discretionary decision points** | Fewer opportunities to extract, fewer places to monitor. The most underrated anti-corruption instrument | Moderate |
| **Build capability before load** | Premature load bearing destroys the legitimacy of a new institution before it can develop | Moderate |
| **Never remove an enforcement institution without a replacement** | The demand persists and the worst available supplier fills it. Iraq 2003; post-Soviet Russia; Sicily in the 1860s | Strong |
| **Ask what the existing rents are containing** | In limited access orders, elite rents are what prevents violence. Opening access without a substitute settlement invites civil war | Moderate |
| **Design for procedural fairness explicitly** | Acceptance of authority depends more on being treated fairly than on outcome favourability | Strong within policing and courts |
| **Assume transplants fail; adapt from a local problem instead** | The transplant failure record is close to universal. PDIA is the best articulated response, though its own evidence is thin | Moderate on the diagnosis, weak on the remedy |
| **Prefer universal and impartial to targeted and discretionary** | Impartial administration predicts trust; discretion creates both extraction opportunities and perceived unfairness. **Note this is where the evidence meets a values disagreement about redistribution — the impartiality finding does not by itself decide the size of the state** | Moderate |

## The anti-checklist: what not to do

1. **Do not run an integrity campaign in a system where honesty is individually costly.** You will punish the people who comply.
2. **Do not add a metric without naming its sacrifice.**
3. **Do not route the whistleblowing channel through the management chain it exists to check.** This is theatre with a legal-defence function.
4. **Do not treat a coordination equilibrium as a character problem**, or you will fire people and reproduce the outcome. Wells Fargo fired 5,300 and kept the quotas.
5. **Do not copy an institutional form without asking what enforces it.**
6. **Do not remove an enforcement institution, however bad, without a replacement.**
7. **Do not confuse compliance with trust.** Compliance under surveillance tells you nothing about what happens when surveillance lapses.
8. **Do not let severe unenforced rules stand.** They teach that the rule system is decorative, which is worse than having no rule.
9. **Do not assume more transparency helps.** Publicising corruption that is never punished increases cynicism.
10. **Do not build on a single striking study.** Given the replication record, downgrade everything you learned from one memorable experiment.

## Where the popular story is wrong

- **"Build trust first, then you can cooperate."** Usually backwards. Structure the interaction so cooperation is safe and repeated, and trust follows as an inference from observed behaviour. Trust is the output, not the input.
- **"Culture eats strategy for breakfast."** Culture is largely downstream of structure — of what gets rewarded, what gets punished, and what can safely be said. NUMMI changed the culture by changing the production system with the same workforce.
- **"Hire for values."** Selection matters, and it is far weaker than incentive and information structure. Wells Fargo's 5,300 were selected by a normal hiring process.
- **"Leaders build trust by being authentic."** Trust is built by reliability under observation over time. Authenticity is neither necessary nor sufficient, and is unfalsifiable as a construct.
- **"Best practice can be adopted."** Adopt the *diagnosis*, never the form. Every failure in [the-control-group.md](the-control-group.md) involved adopting something that worked somewhere else.
- **"Transparency is the disinfectant."** Only when paired with a functioning enforcement channel. Alone it is a mechanism for publicising impunity.
- **"Trust falls and offsites."** No credible evidence. The absence of harm is not the presence of benefit, and they consume the credibility that a real structural change would need.

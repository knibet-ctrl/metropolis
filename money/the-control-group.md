> in every single case.** There is no control group for a national economy.

## Why failures are the most informative cases

Success is overdetermined and easy to narrate backwards. Failure, especially
*predicted* failure, is where a framework can actually be scored. Each case
below is chosen because a specific mechanism was claimed in advance and can be
checked against what happened.

---

## 1. Soviet central planning and the calculation problem

**The claim, made in advance.** Ludwig von Mises, "Economic Calculation in the
Socialist Commonwealth" (1920): without markets for **capital goods**, there
are no prices for them; without prices there is no common unit; without a
common unit, choosing between production techniques is not a hard problem —
it is not a well-posed problem at all.

**The best counter-argument.** Oskar Lange (1936-37) replied that a Central
Planning Board could simulate the market: announce prices, observe surpluses
and shortages, adjust by trial and error. On the blackboard this works, and
most economists in the 1940s thought Lange had won.

**Hayek's reframing (1945), which is the decisive move.** The problem is not
computation. It is **knowledge**. The data the planner needs do not exist in
transmissible form — they are dispersed, tacit, local, and continuously
changing. And they are held by people whose incentive under planning is to
misreport them. Lange's board can solve equations it can never be given.

**What happened.** Soviet growth was genuinely fast in the extensive phase
(roughly 1928 through the 1950s): mobilising labour from agriculture to
industry, building capital stock, and achieving real technical feats — Sputnik,
1957. **Paul Samuelson's textbook projected Soviet GDP overtaking the US, with
the projected crossover date pushed back in successive editions.** It is a
standing embarrassment and a warning about extrapolating extensive growth.

Then TFP growth collapsed. The system could accumulate inputs and could not
improve their use. Kornai's *Economics of Shortage* (1980) diagnosed the
mechanism: **soft budget constraints** meant enterprises never faced failure,
so they hoarded inputs and labour; **gross output targets** produced the
classic pathologies (the factory told to produce tonnes of nails producing one
enormous nail); **storming** at the end of each planning period wrecked
quality; and the **ratchet effect** meant any enterprise that over-performed
had its target raised, so nobody over-performed.

**Be careful with the attribution.** Soviet failure is overdetermined: war,
terror, autarky, an unaccountable political system and central planning are
entangled and cannot be separated with one observation. **The strongest
evidence for the specifically economic claim is the transition:** Chinese
agricultural output rose sharply within a few years of the household
responsibility system from 1978, with the *same* land, the *same* people, and
different price and residual-claimant arrangements.

**The darkest evidence:** the famines. Soviet 1932-33 (roughly 5-7 million
deaths) and China's Great Leap Forward 1959-61 (estimates range enormously,
roughly 15-45 million). Both occurred under systems that suppressed price
signals *and* suppressed the information flow that would have revealed the
disaster. Amartya Sen's observation stands: no substantial famine has occurred
in a functioning democracy with a free press.

## 2. The gold standard and the Great Depression

**Close to TIER 1 — one of the most robust findings in macroeconomic history.**

Mechanism (Eichengreen, *Golden Fetters*, 1992; Bernanke and James): under a
fixed gold parity, a country losing gold must deflate. There is no independent
monetary policy and no exchange rate adjustment. Deflation is transmitted
internationally and reinforced, because every country's defensive response
makes every other country's position worse.

**The natural experiment — and it is unusually clean:**

| Country | Left gold | Recovery began |
|---|---|---|
| Britain | September 1931 | 1932 |
| United States | April 1933 | 1933 |
| Gold bloc (France, Belgium, Netherlands, Switzerland, Poland) | 1935-36 | Last |

**Recovery order matches exit order.** Multiple countries, staggered timing,
same mechanism. This is about as good as macro evidence gets.

**Steelman for the gold standard, because it deserves one.** It removed
discretionary debasement, imposed fiscal discipline, and the classical era
(roughly 1870-1914) had low average inflation and facilitated enormous capital
flows on stable terms.

**And against.** That era also had frequent banking panics, no lender of last
resort in the US until 1913, and a severe deflation from 1873 to 1896 that
drove agrarian political revolt — Bryan's "Cross of Gold" speech (1896) is
about exactly this. It functioned partly because of the Bank of England's
management and a capital-flow and labour-migration regime that no longer
exists. **A restored gold standard would not be the 19th-century one.**

**The deeper lesson, which generalises:** any rigid commitment removes an
adjustment valve. The pressure does not vanish; it reappears somewhere else,
usually as unemployment or a political crisis.

## 3. The euro: a design flaw identified in advance

**Mundell's optimum currency area criteria (1961)** ask when sharing a
currency makes sense: labour mobility, fiscal transfers, price and wage
flexibility, and correlated shocks. **The eurozone met few of them, and this
was said publicly before launch** — Feldstein (1997), Friedman, Krugman, and a
1998 statement by a large group of German economists.

**The design gaps:**
- Monetary union without banking union or fiscal union
- No lender of last resort for sovereigns — a member state borrows in a
  currency it cannot issue, which makes it structurally more like a US state
  or an emerging market than like the UK or Japan (De Grauwe's point)
- The **doom loop**: banks hold their own sovereign's bonds; the sovereign
  backstops the banks; each drags the other down
- No exit mechanism, making any speculation about exit self-amplifying

**What happened, 2010-12.** Greece's deficit was revised in October 2009 from
about 3.7% to 12.7% of GDP, eventually higher still — the misreporting was
real. Ireland's September 2008 blanket bank guarantee converted a banking
crisis into a sovereign one overnight. Portugal, Spain and Cyprus followed.
Greek output fell roughly 25% peak to trough; youth unemployment exceeded 50%.

**The resolution is the most instructive part.** On 26 July 2012 Mario Draghi
said the ECB would do "whatever it takes." Outright Monetary Transactions was
announced in September. **Spreads collapsed without OMT ever purchasing a
single bond.** The binding constraint had been the absence of a credible
backstop, not fundamentals. This is very strong evidence for the
multiple-equilibria view of sovereign debt crises.

**The forecasting failure with the largest human cost.** The IMF's programmes
assumed fiscal multipliers around 0.5. Blanchard and Leigh (2013) — *published
by the IMF's own chief economist* — found actual multipliers in the range of
roughly 0.9 to 1.7 during that period. Austerity contracted output about twice
as much as the models assumed, which raised debt/GDP ratios and required more
austerity. **The institution audited itself and published the error, which is
to its credit and does not undo the outcome.**

**Fair counter:** the counterfactual is unknown. Disorderly exit, redenomination
and bank runs might have been worse. And Greece's pre-crisis fiscal
misreporting is a fact, not an invention.

## 4. Structural adjustment and the Washington Consensus

**The term** was coined by John Williamson in 1989 to describe ten specific
reforms he observed Latin American technocrats converging on — fiscal
discipline, tax reform, competitive exchange rates, trade liberalisation,
property rights, and so on. It was then used to mean "market fundamentalism
imposed by Washington," a usage **Williamson himself objected to for the rest
of his life.** Both the defenders and the attackers are frequently arguing
about a term that means different things to each.

**Honest scorecard:**

| Worked | Failed |
|---|---|
| Ending hyperinflations. Bolivia 1985 is the clean case | Rapid financial liberalisation with weak supervision — repeatedly produced crises |
| Removing severely overvalued exchange rates | Sequencing: privatising into the absence of competition or regulation created private monopolies |
| Ending the worst price distortions | The African "lost decades" of the 1980s-90s under adjustment programmes are a real black mark |
| Fiscal stabilisation where deficits were genuinely unsustainable | Social spending cuts with no protection for the poorest |

**The decisive comparative fact:** the East Asian successes did **not** follow
the prescription. Korea, Taiwan, Singapore and later China used directed
credit, capital controls, state-owned enterprises and export discipline —
documented by Wade, Amsden, and Ha-Joon Chang's *Kicking Away the Ladder*
(2002), which shows that today's advocates of free trade industrialised behind
protection.

**Steelman the other side:** most adjusting countries were in genuine crisis
with no alternative source of financing. The counterfactual was not "no
adjustment" but "disorderly default." And much conditionality was never
implemented by the governments that signed it.

**Russia, 1992**, as the extreme case: price liberalisation in January 1992,
voucher privatisation, then loans-for-shares (1995-96) which transferred major
assets to a small group. GDP fell roughly 40%. **Male life expectancy fell
from about 64 in 1990 to about 57 by 1994** — a mortality crisis with few
peacetime precedents. China's gradualism versus Russia's shock therapy is one
of the great natural experiments, and the interpretation remains contested
because the initial conditions were wildly different: China was roughly 70%
rural with underused agricultural labour; Russia was urban, industrialised,
and had no peasant sector to release.

## 5. Japan's lost decades — and the myth about them

**The bubble:** the Nikkei peaked at 38,915 on 29 December 1989. Tokyo land
valuations reached the point where the Imperial Palace grounds were nominally
worth more than the state of California. The BOJ tightened sharply in 1989-90.

**The policy error that mattered most was not monetary.** Banks kept insolvent
borrowers alive rather than recognising losses — **zombie lending**
(Caballero, Hoshi and Kashyap, 2008). Credit stayed allocated to failing firms,
which suppressed both the exit of bad firms and the entry of good ones. **The
failure was in *reallocation*, not in the money supply.** Deflation set in
from about 1998.

Compounding errors: a consumption tax rise in 1997 that killed a recovery; a
premature rate rise in 2000; and fiscal policy that was large in total but
repeatedly reversed.

**Krugman's "It's Baaack" (1998)** identified the liquidity trap problem and
the credibility paradox: to escape, a central bank must *credibly promise to
be irresponsible*, which a credible central bank cannot easily do.

**The corrective, which is itself a widely-missed point.** Measured **per
working-age adult**, Japanese growth over the "lost decades" is roughly
comparable to the US and better than much of Europe. Unemployment stayed low;
life expectancy rose; social cohesion held. **A large part of "Japan's lost
decades" is a demographic story mislabelled as a growth failure.** The
financial crisis and the zombie-lending failure were real. The
national-catastrophe framing is not.

## 6. Confidently wrong: a scorecard

The point of this section is not to mock. It is that **each of these was a
public, dated, checkable claim by serious people, and the pattern in the
failures is legible.**

| Prediction | By whom, when | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Soviet GDP would overtake the US | Samuelson's textbook, multiple editions | Never happened; the crossover date kept moving |
| No crisis coming; the Great Moderation is structural | Most of the profession, ~2004-07 | 2008. **The Queen asked at the LSE in November 2008 why no one saw it coming; the British Academy's written reply is a remarkable document of institutional self-examination** |
| Financial innovation had dispersed risk safely | Greenspan and others | It had concentrated and obscured it |
| QE would cause currency debasement and inflation | Open letter to Bernanke, *WSJ*, November 2010, ~23 signatories | Core inflation ran below target for most of the following decade |
| Growth collapses above 90% debt/GDP | Reinhart & Rogoff (2010) | Spreadsheet error plus weighting choices; corrected, the cliff disappears (Herndon, Ash & Pollin, 2013). See banking-and-crises.md |
| Fiscal multipliers around 0.5 | IMF programme assumptions, 2010-12 | ~0.9-1.7 (Blanchard & Leigh, 2013). Self-corrected |
| LTCM's models captured the risk | Two Nobel laureates on the board | 1998 |
| 2021 inflation is transitory | The Fed, the ECB and most forecasters | Powell retired the word on 30 November 2021 |
| Disinflation would require a deep recession and years of high unemployment | Many, including Summers, 2022 | Inflation fell to near target with no recession and little unemployment rise |
| Population growth would outrun food supply | Malthus (1798); Ehrlich, *The Population Bomb* (1968) | Norman Borlaug and the Green Revolution. Simon won the 1980-90 Simon-Ehrlich wager on all five metals — **though the result is sensitive to the decade chosen, which Simon's fans omit** |
| Britain would exhaust its coal | Jevons (1865) | Substitution. Jevons's *paradox* — efficiency raising total consumption — survives and is the better idea |
| Resource limits by ~2000 | Popular retellings of *Limits to Growth* (1972) | **The caricature is unfair.** The book presented scenarios, not dated predictions; the specific exhaustion dates people cite are not in it. Its standard run's broad shape has been argued both ways |

**Note the shape: two of these are opposite errors made within a decade of each
other by overlapping groups of people. That is the single most important fact
about macroeconomic forecasting in this notebook.**

## What the failures have in common

| Pattern | Cases |
|---|---|
| **A crucial mechanism omitted from the model** | 2008 (no financial sector in the models); RBC; Lange's planning board (no incentive to report truthfully) |
| **A rigid commitment removing an adjustment valve** | Gold standard 1929-33; the euro; currency pegs generally |
| **Confidence out of proportion to evidence** | 90% debt threshold; multiplier of 0.5; transitory; QE inflation |
| **Incentives aligned against dissent** | Rajan dismissed at Jackson Hole 2005; ratings agencies paid by issuers; Soviet enterprise reporting |
| **An accounting identity or correlation mistaken for a mechanism** | Debt-growth correlation; profit share decompositions; MV = PY |
| **Extrapolating an extensive phase into an intensive one** | Soviet growth projections; every "this new economy is different" |
| **Suppressed information flow** | Famines under planning; off-balance-sheet vehicles pre-2008; SVB's held-to-maturity book |

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Socialism failed because people are lazy" | The diagnosis that held up was informational and incentive-structural, not about character. Mises and Hayek said so in 1920 and 1945 |
| "The gold standard would prevent inflation" | It prevented inflation and produced deflation, panics, and a mechanism that deepened the Depression |
| "Austerity was caused by an Excel error" | Comforting and false. Austerity was a political programme; the paper supplied a citation it would otherwise have found elsewhere |
| "The IMF never admits error" | It published Blanchard and Leigh on its own multiplier failure, and its ex post evaluation of the Greek programme was self-critical |
| "Japan has been in crisis for thirty years" | Per working-age adult, growth is comparable to peers. The zombie-lending failure was real; the catastrophe framing is not |
| "Nobody predicted 2008" | Rajan (2005), Borio and White at the BIS, Shiller, Baker. The failure was consequence-free warning, not absent warning |
| "Limits to Growth was proven wrong" | The popular version of its claims was never its claims |
| "Economists were wrong about inflation, so ignore them" | They were wrong in *both directions*, which means neither camp's prior was vindicated. The correct update is toward humility, not toward the other side |

## Open questions

- [ ] Is there any planning mechanism that solves Hayek's knowledge problem
      given modern data and computation, or is the tacit-knowledge component
      irreducible? **This is now a live question again and deserves better
      than reflex answers from either direction.**
- [ ] Was the euro's crisis a design flaw, a policy failure in 2010-12, or
      both? The counterfactual is unavailable.
- [ ] Would the eurozone survive a repeat without a fiscal union?
- [ ] Why did the profession fail in opposite directions in 2010 and 2021?
      Is there a common cause, or is macro simply this noisy?
- [ ] What is the actual half-life of a macroeconomic consensus? Casually it
      looks like about fifteen years, and nobody has measured it.
- [ ] Are there failures missing from this file because they were never
      recorded as failures? Almost certainly, and that is the deepest problem
      with a control group assembled from published history.

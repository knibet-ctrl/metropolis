# What We Actually Know — The Confidence Ledger

> **The key file.** Everything in this notebook, sorted by how much weight it
> can bear. Read this before arguing with anyone, including yourself.

> Written from general knowledge without source verification. The tier
> assignments are judgements, not measurements.

**The tiers:**

- **TIER 1 — Established.** Multiple independent lines of evidence, or true by
  construction. Overturning it would require extraordinary evidence.
- **TIER 2 — Contested but tractable.** Serious people disagree; evidence and
  argument are relevant; progress is possible.
- **TIER 3 — Speculative.** Coherent, unfalsified, weakly evidenced.
- **TIER 4 — Unanswerable or normative.** No empirical procedure settles it.

**The uncomfortable summary before you start:** a great deal of
macroeconomics sits in TIER 2 or TIER 3. **This is unusual for a field whose
outputs set interest rates for eight billion people.** Microeconomics,
economic history and the accounting identities are much sturdier than the
macro layer that gets the airtime.

---

## TIER 0 — True by construction

Not a knowledge claim. **Accounting identities. You cannot argue with them and
you cannot derive a policy from one without adding an empirical premise.**
Listed separately because conflating this row with TIER 1 is the single
biggest source of public confusion in economics.

| Identity | What it says | What it does *not* say |
|---|---|---|
| **Sectoral balances**: private + government + foreign = 0 | One sector's surplus is another's deficit | That any deficit is good, bad, or sustainable |
| **MV = PY** | Velocity is defined so this balances | That money growth causes inflation. That requires stable V |
| **S = I** (ex post, closed economy) | Realised saving equals realised investment | That saving *causes* investment, or vice versa |
| **GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)** | An expenditure decomposition | That government spending "adds to" GDP causally. The M term is a subtraction of imports already counted, not a penalty for trade |
| **Deflator = unit labour costs + unit profits + unit taxes** | An income decomposition | That rising profits *caused* inflation |
| **Current account = −capital account** | A country importing goods is importing capital | That trade deficits are debts, or losses |
| **α = r × β** (Piketty's first law) | Capital's income share = return × capital/income ratio | Anything about causation between them |

**If someone states one of these and then draws a policy conclusion without
adding an empirical claim, they have made a logical error. This happens on all
sides of every economic debate, constantly.**

## TIER 1 — Established

| Claim | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Banks create deposits when they lend** | Balance sheet mechanics. Published by the Bank of England (2014) and Bundesbank (2017). Not a heterodox view |
| **The money multiplier is not how modern banking works** | Several major economies have zero reserve requirement. Central banks target rates, so reserves are supplied on demand |
| **No pure barter economy has ever been documented** | Humphrey (1985) and the whole ethnographic record. A strong *negative* result |
| **Credit and accounting predate coinage by millennia** | Mesopotamian records c.3000 BCE; first coinage c.600 BCE |
| **Marginal utility resolves the diamond-water paradox** | Jevons, Menger, Walras, 1871-74. Value is a relation, not a property |
| **Maturity transformation makes banks structurally run-prone** | Diamond-Dybvig (1983). Two equilibria; a solvent bank can fail |
| **Prices aggregate dispersed information** | Hayek (1945). Demonstrated negatively by the planning failures |
| **Central planning without price signals fails at allocation** | The largest natural experiment ever run. Mechanism identified in advance by Mises (1920) and Hayek (1945) |
| **Countries that left the gold standard earlier recovered earlier from the Depression** | Staggered exits, matching recovery order. Unusually clean for macro |
| **Rapid private credit growth is the best available leading indicator of financial crises** | Schularick & Taylor (2012), BIS work. **Best available ≠ good enough to time** |
| **Long-run, high inflation is a monetary phenomenon** | Above ~40%/yr the money-price relationship is near one-to-one |
| **Every hyperinflation involved fiscal and real-output collapse, not printing alone** | Weimar, Hungary, Zimbabwe, Venezuela all share the structure |
| **Hyperinflations end quickly when the fiscal regime credibly changes** | Sargent (1982). Days-to-months, not years |
| **Comparative advantage produces gains from trade** | Ricardo (1817). Distribution of the gains is a separate question |
| **Price controls produce shortages or surpluses** | Very high consensus. Magnitude and whether it's worth it are separate |
| **Rent control reduces housing supply and quality over time** | One of the highest-consensus findings in the discipline, and among the least popular |
| **Adverse selection can destroy a market entirely** | Akerlof (1970). Not inefficiency — non-existence |
| **Common-pool resources are sometimes managed sustainably without markets or states** | Ostrom (1990). Falsifies the market-or-state dichotomy |
| **Global extreme poverty fell dramatically 1990-2019** | From roughly 36% to under 10% of world population |
| **Costs and fees compound against an investor** | Arithmetic, not prediction |
| **Most active funds underperform their benchmark net of fees over long horizons** | Consistent across decades, countries and datasets |

## TIER 2 — Contested but tractable

| Question | State of play |
|---|---|
| **What caused the 2021-23 inflation, and in what proportions?** | Supply shocks, fiscal transfers, energy, labour tightness, margins — all present, weights disputed. Bernanke-Blanchard (2023): mostly supply first, demand later |
| **How much did policy cause the 2023-24 disinflation?** | No counterfactual. May never be settled |
| **What caused the 2008 crisis?** | The official inquiry (FCIC, 2011) split 6-4 with two dissents |
| **Is the Phillips curve non-linear or was 2021 a supply artefact?** | Live |
| **Why are some countries rich?** | Institutions, geography, culture, human capital, energy — all live, all endogenous. Plausibly the biggest open question in social science |
| **Does r > g drive wealth concentration?** | Rognlie (2015) — it's housing — is the strongest critique. Piketty's *data* is a separate and much stronger contribution |
| **Does inequality harm growth?** | Weak identification; results specification-sensitive |
| **What is the fiscal multiplier?** | Regime-dependent. Higher at the zero lower bound and in slumps. The IMF's own 0.5-vs-1.5 error is the cautionary case |
| **Does QE work, and through which channel?** | Signalling and market functioning look strongest; bank lending weakest |
| **Do higher bank capital requirements reduce lending?** | Admati-Hellwig say no in the long run; transition costs are real |
| **Has market power structurally increased?** | De Loecker et al. say yes; Traina and Basu raise serious measurement objections |
| **How much did colonial extraction contribute to Western industrialisation?** | Genuinely disputed. Both "everything" and "nothing" are unsupportable |
| **Employment effects of minimum wages** | The literature has moved substantially toward small effects at moderate levels since Card-Krueger (1994). Not settled at high levels |
| **What is the true rate of inflation?** | ±1pp measurement uncertainty. Enough to flip the sign on "have real wages stagnated?" |
| **Is unconditional convergence happening?** | Failed 1960-2000 (Pritchett); may have appeared post-2000. Sample window drives the answer |
| **Optimal exchange rate regime for a developing economy** | The impossible trinity constrains it; the choice within the constraint is unresolved |
| **Whether crypto has a durable non-speculative use** | Fifteen years in, the answer is not obviously yes or obviously no |

## TIER 3 — Speculative

Hold loosely. Serious people hold these; nobody should be confident.

| Claim | Note |
|---|---|
| **Secular stagnation** | Summers's revival of Hansen. Coherent; hard to distinguish from a long series of shocks |
| **Austrian business cycle theory** | Elegant; the methodological stance largely exempts it from testing, which is the problem |
| **Sellers'/profit-led inflation as a distinct cause** | The decomposition it rests on is an identity that cannot separate it from demand-driven margin expansion |
| **The fiscal theory of the price level** | Cochrane, Sims, Leeper. Internally coherent, empirically hard to distinguish from monetary explanations |
| **Kondratiev / long waves** | Popular, poorly evidenced, heavily overfitted |
| **Piketty's r > g as a general law** | See TIER 2. The *law* framing is TIER 3 |
| **That AI will produce a discontinuity in measured productivity** | No precedent supports rapid measured productivity gains from general-purpose technologies; Solow's 1987 remark about computers still applies |
| **Degrowth as a feasible macroeconomic programme** | The critique of GDP is accepted; the macroeconomics of a managed contraction is undeveloped |
| **That modern computation could solve the socialist calculation problem** | Live again. Hayek's objection was about tacit knowledge and incentives to misreport, neither of which compute solves directly. Both confident answers are unwarranted |
| **Optimal currency area membership predictions for any specific country** | The theory is sound; applying it prospectively has a poor record |

## TIER 4 — Normative or unanswerable

| Question | Why no empirical procedure settles it |
|---|---|
| **What should the inflation target be?** | Requires weighing unemployment against price stability, and choosing whose unemployment |
| **What is the right social discount rate?** | How much a person in 2200 counts. **This single ethical parameter drives most of the disagreement in climate economics** |
| **Is inequality unjust?** | Depends on a theory of desert and of what distribution is owed |
| **Should some things be outside markets?** | Sandel vs Roth. Both have a point; neither has evidence that settles it |
| **Who should bear the cost of disinflation?** | The cost is real and someone bears it. Which someone is a political choice presented as technical |
| **What is a human life worth?** | Every health system answers implicitly; no method survives public scrutiny |
| **Is seigniorage a legitimate revenue source?** | A question about taxation by consent |
| **What do we owe future generations?** | Prior to economics, not derivable from it |

## The things people most commonly get wrong

Sorted roughly by frequency. **This is the longest table in the notebook and
that is the finding.**

| Belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Banks lend out deposits" | Lending creates deposits. Both sides of the balance sheet expand at once |
| "The money multiplier" | Not how it works. Zero reserve requirement in several major economies |
| "Printing money always causes inflation" | 2009-2019 across the US, euro area and Japan is a decade-long counterexample |
| "QE is printing money" | An asset swap. Broad money rises only when buying from non-banks, and the seller is no richer |
| "Barter came first" | No such economy has ever been documented. In the textbooks anyway |
| "Money must be backed by something" | Nothing is, and it works. Backing is one anchor among several |
| "Weimar was reckless money-printing" | Reparations in gold and foreign currency, the Ruhr occupation, and paying strikers while output stopped |
| "Inflation is falling means prices are falling" | It means they rise more slowly. The level does not come back |
| "The government is like a household" | A currency issuer is not. **But it also faces inflation and exchange-rate limits a household doesn't.** Both halves get ignored by opposite camps |
| "Trade deficits mean we're losing" | The current account and capital account are two sides of one identity. A deficit means capital is flowing in |
| "Imports subtract from GDP" | They're subtracted because they were already added in C, I and G. It's a bookkeeping correction, not a penalty |
| "The national debt must be repaid by our grandchildren" | Debt is rolled, not repaid. The real question is the interest burden relative to growth, which is a different and better question |
| "Your money is in the bank" | You are an unsecured creditor of a commercial bank |
| "The 1929 crash caused the Depression" | The 1930-33 banking panics and the one-third money stock contraction did |
| "Deregulation caused 2008" | Partly; the biggest failures were in areas Glass-Steagall never covered. The unregulated shadow system was the larger gap |
| "Bailouts cost taxpayers a fortune" | TARP was largely repaid at a profit. **The real cost was distributional and moral hazard** — a legitimate grievance stated in the wrong currency |
| "Nobody predicted 2008" | Rajan (2005), Borio and White, Shiller, Baker. Warnings without consequences |
| "Things have intrinsic value" | Dissolved in 1871 |
| "Price should reflect cost of production" | Both blades of the scissors. Cost constrains supply, not price |
| "Adam Smith was a market absolutist" | He supported public education, banking regulation, and worried about merchant collusion. "Invisible hand" appears three times in his corpus |
| "Hayek proved markets always win" | He proved prices aggregate dispersed knowledge — which tells you exactly where markets fail too |
| "Coase showed regulation is unnecessary" | He showed transaction costs determine which institution is efficient |
| "Tragedy of the commons" | Hardin described open access. Ostrom documented hundreds of working commons |
| "Piketty was debunked by the FT" | He responded in detail; the core series held. The UK data was weak |
| "Capital's share is rising because of robots" | Rognlie: it's housing. Net of depreciation, non-housing capital share is roughly flat |
| "Reinhart-Rogoff was debunked so debt doesn't matter" | The 90% cliff was. A milder association survives with unresolved causation |
| "Austerity was caused by a spreadsheet error" | Comforting, and false |
| "GDP measures wellbeing" | It measures marketed output. **Kuznets, who built it, warned explicitly against this use** |
| "Correlation isn't causation, so we know nothing" | Modern applied micro has instruments, natural experiments, RCTs and difference-in-differences. **Micro identification is genuinely good; macro identification is genuinely bad.** Don't generalise across them |
| "Economists all agree" / "Economists never agree" | Both wrong. High consensus on trade, price controls, rent control, incentives. Low consensus on almost all of macro |
| "Rent control helps tenants" | Existing tenants yes, future tenants no, supply falls. This is one of the most robust and least popular results in the field |
| "Sweatshops / minimum wage / immigration — [confident claim]" | These are the three where confident claims most often outrun the evidence in *both* directions |
| "The stock market is the economy" | Roughly half of US households own equities at all; ownership is heavily concentrated |
| "Gold is a hedge against inflation" | Over long horizons the correlation is weak. Its real record is as a crisis and currency-debasement hedge, which is a narrower claim |

## Where the strongest convergences are

Independent methods reaching the same conclusion is the best signal available
in a field this soft. There are four.

**1. Money is a record, not a substance.** Anthropology (Mesopotamian
ledgers), monetary theory (Kocherlakota's "Money Is Memory," 1996), the Yap
stones — and **Milton Friedman and David Graeber agree on this**, from
opposite ends of the political spectrum. That convergence is worth more than
either alone.

**2. Prices carry information that cannot be centralised.** Argued a priori
(Mises 1920), reframed epistemically (Hayek 1945), and demonstrated
empirically by seventy years of planning outcomes and by the sharp response of
Chinese agriculture to the 1978 reforms.

**3. Financial fragility comes from maturity transformation and leverage, not
from asset prices.** Theory (Diamond-Dybvig 1983), history (Kindleberger,
Reinhart-Rogoff), and the 1987-vs-2008 contrast all point the same way.

**4. Hyperinflation is a fiscal and state-capacity failure.** Four independent
cases with different politics, different decades and different continents,
sharing one structure — and all ending abruptly when the fiscal regime
changed.

## What would change my mind

Stated in advance, because a confidence ledger without falsifiers is a
manifesto.

- **On money creation:** a central bank publishing operational evidence that
  reserves constrain lending in a rate-targeting regime.
- **On barter:** a documented pre-monetary economy with generalised barter.
- **On hyperinflation:** a case with intact fiscal capacity, intact real
  output, and inflation above 50% per month.
- **On growth:** a single-factor account that correctly post-dicts the Koreas,
  China, Botswana, Argentina and Japan without free parameters.
- **On active management:** persistent, cost-adjusted, out-of-sample
  outperformance by an identifiable class of manager over multiple decades.

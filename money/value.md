## The question

Why is anything worth anything? For two centuries this was *the* question in
economics, and the answer changed completely in about three years in the
1870s. Most people have never heard about it.

---

## The classical answer: value comes from labour

**Adam Smith**, *Wealth of Nations* (1776), separates **value in use** from
**value in exchange** and immediately hits the **diamond-water paradox**:
nothing is more useful than water and it buys almost nothing; a diamond is
nearly useless and buys a great deal. Smith could not resolve it. He fell back
on labour: in a "rude and early state," if it takes twice the labour to kill a
beaver as a deer, one beaver trades for two deer.

**David Ricardo**, *Principles* (1817), tightened it and immediately ran into
capital and time. If two goods embody equal labour but one takes a year to
mature, they don't trade at par — profit intervenes. Ricardo spent the rest of
his life hunting an "invariable measure of value" and never found it.

**Karl Marx**, *Capital* Vol. I (1867), built the most systematic version:
value is **socially necessary labour time**. The crucial move is the
distinction between **labour** (the activity) and **labour power** (the
capacity, which is what the worker sells). Labour power's value is what it
costs to reproduce the worker; the labour extracted is worth more; the
difference is **surplus value**. That is where profit comes from.

**Steelman:** the labour theory is not primarily a theory of relative prices —
that is how critics beat it, and it is a misreading. It is a theory of *where
the surplus comes from and who gets it*. As an account of distribution and
class it is coherent, and its central question — why does capital receive a
return at all? — has never been answered as cleanly by its successors as they
tend to assume. Marx's analysis of accumulation, concentration and crisis
tendency remains one of the most influential frameworks in social science.

**The strongest objection:** the **transformation problem.** Marx needed to
convert labour values into observed prices of production, and Volume III
(published posthumously in 1894) does not do it consistently. Bortkiewicz
(1907) showed the arithmetic fails in the general case. A century of attempted
repairs has not produced an agreed solution. Beyond that: the theory cannot
explain why an untouched plot of land, a vintage wine that improved by sitting
still, or a failed product with enormous labour embedded in it are worth what
they are worth.

## The marginal revolution, 1871-74

Three men, independently, in three countries, in four years:

| | Work | Country |
|---|---|---|
| **William Stanley Jevons** | *The Theory of Political Economy* (1871) | England |
| **Carl Menger** | *Grundsätze der Volkswirtschaftslehre* (1871) | Austria |
| **Léon Walras** | *Éléments d'économie politique pure* (1874) | Switzerland/France |

(Precursors exist and were ignored: Gossen 1854, Dupuit 1844 on consumer
surplus, Cournot 1838. "Revolution" flatters the discontinuity.)

**The move:** value is not a property of an object. It is a relation between
an agent's ends and **the marginal unit available.** Combined with diminishing
marginal utility — each additional unit is worth less than the last — the
diamond-water paradox dissolves in one sentence:

> Where water is abundant, the *marginal* glass is worth almost nothing, even
> though water in total is worth everything. Where diamonds are scarce, the
> marginal diamond is worth a great deal.

Total utility and marginal utility are different quantities, and prices track
the second. **A confusion that had defeated Smith, Ricardo and Mill collapsed
the moment someone took a derivative.**

**Consequences, which are larger than the price theory:**

- There is no "true price" or "intrinsic value." Prices are equilibrium
  outcomes of subjective valuations meeting scarcity.
- **Exchange is positive-sum.** Both parties value the goods differently; both
  gain. Under a labour theory, exchange at equal value looks like a wash and
  profit looks like extraction. This is a genuine fork in the road.
- "Just price" becomes a category error in economics — though not in ethics.
- Cost does not determine price. Sunk costs are sunk. **The most expensive
  product ever built is worth zero if nobody wants it.**

**Marshall's correction (1890)**, which everyone should know:

> We might as reasonably dispute whether it is the upper or under blade of a
> pair of scissors that cuts a piece of paper, as whether value is governed by
> utility or cost of production.

Demand sets what people will pay; supply and cost set what will be produced.
Neither blade cuts alone. **This is the settled position and it has been for
135 years.**

## Prices as information — the best idea in the file

**F.A. Hayek, "The Use of Knowledge in Society," *American Economic Review*,
September 1945.** If you read one thing from this notebook's reading list,
read this. It is about 12 pages.

The argument:

1. The economic problem is *not* allocating known resources against known
   ends. If we had all the data, it would be a maths problem.
2. But the relevant knowledge **does not exist in aggregated form anywhere.**
   It exists dispersed — the local shortage, the machine that runs better
   warm, the customer about to default, the tacit skill nobody has written
   down. Much of it cannot be articulated even by the person who has it.
3. A price change transmits **the net result of all of it** as a single
   number. Hayek's example: tin becomes scarce somewhere. Users worldwide
   economise on tin without knowing why — whether a mine flooded or a new use
   was found. They don't need to know. **The system economises on how much
   anyone has to understand.**
4. So the market is best understood as a **communication and discovery
   mechanism**, not an optimiser.

**Why it matters even if you dislike its politics:** this argument identifies
precisely *where markets fail* — wherever the price does not carry the
relevant information. Every item in the next section is an instance of that.
**Hayek gives you the strongest available theory of market failure, which is
not what he is usually cited for.**

**On the misreading:** the paper is routinely deployed as a general argument
against state action. It is narrower and better than that. Hayek himself
accepted a substantial state role — a minimum income, public goods,
regulation of externalities — in *The Road to Serfdom* and *The Constitution
of Liberty*. Quoting him as a libertarian absolutist requires ignoring what he
wrote.

## Where prices fail

Five canonical failures. Each is TIER 1 as a mechanism; magnitudes are TIER 2.

### Externalities

Costs or benefits falling on third parties, absent from the price. Pollution
is the archetype.

- **Pigou (1920):** tax the externality to internalise it.
- **Coase, "The Problem of Social Cost" (1960):** with clearly assigned rights
  and zero transaction costs, the efficient outcome results regardless of who
  holds the right. **Coase is misread by everyone.** His actual point was that
  transaction costs are *never* zero, and therefore the interesting question
  is which institutional arrangement minimises them. Free-marketeers cite him
  to argue against regulation; the paper is a foundational text of
  institutional economics.

### Public goods

Non-rival and non-excludable (Samuelson, 1954). Lighthouses, basic research,
national defence, clean air. Markets undersupply them because free-riding is
individually rational.

**Ostrom's correction, which is important.** Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the
Commons* (1990), Nobel 2009, documented hundreds of cases where communities
sustainably managed common-pool resources — Swiss alpine pasture, Japanese
forests, Spanish irrigation, Maine lobster fisheries — through neither markets
nor state ownership, using local rules, monitoring and graduated sanctions.
This falsifies the standard market-or-state dichotomy. **It also corrects
Hardin's "Tragedy of the Commons" (1968), which described *open access*, not
*commons*, and conflated them.** (Hardin's own views on population and
immigration were repellent; that is a separate matter from the analytical
error, and both are true.)

### Information asymmetry

**George Akerlof, "The Market for Lemons" (1970)** — rejected by three
journals before publication, later a Nobel. If sellers know quality and buyers
don't, buyers pay the average, good sellers exit, the average falls, and the
market can **unravel entirely.** Not inefficiency — non-existence.

Michael Spence added **signalling** (education as a costly signal, whatever it
teaches); Joseph Stiglitz added **screening**. Joint Nobel, 2001. Plus **moral
hazard** (insured behaviour changes) and the whole principal-agent literature.

This is why used cars have warranties, why doctors are licensed, why health
insurance markets are structurally difficult everywhere, and why "just let
the market handle it" fails hardest exactly where the informational gap is
biggest — medicine, finance, law.

### Natural monopoly

Where average cost declines over the whole relevant range — water networks,
electricity grids, rail track — competition is wasteful and one firm wins.
Options are regulation, public ownership, franchise auctions, or contestable
market design (Baumol). **All four have failed somewhere.** Nobody has a
general solution; this is a genuine TIER 2 institutional-design problem.

### Market power

Distinct from natural monopoly. Whether markups have risen structurally is
contested: De Loecker, Eeckhout and Unger (2020) find a large rise in US
markups since 1980; critics (Traina, Basu) argue the result is sensitive to
how overheads are treated in the cost measure. **A prominent finding with a
serious measurement objection — currently TIER 2.**

## Things markets price badly, and the honest version of the objection

Beyond the textbook failures, there are goods where the price mechanism works
mechanically but the output is suspect:

| Category | Problem |
|---|---|
| **The far future** | Discounting. The Stern Review (2006) used a near-zero pure rate of time preference and found aggressive climate action justified; Nordhaus used a market-calibrated rate and found gradual action optimal. **The entire disagreement turns on an ethical parameter — how much a person in 2200 counts — not on any empirical question.** This is the clearest case in economics of a value judgement wearing a technical costume, and it should be named every time |
| **Ecosystem services** | No owner, no market, no price. Valuation attempts are heroic and contested |
| **Care and household work** | Excluded from GDP by convention. Marilyn Waring's *If Women Counted* (1988) is the standard critique |
| **Sacred / protected values** | Tetlock's work on taboo trade-offs: people find pricing certain things not merely wrong but offensive, and *offering* the trade damages the relationship |
| **Anything bounded by ability to pay** | Willingness to pay is willingness *and ability*. A market allocates to the highest bidder, not the greatest need. This is not a market failure — it is the market working as designed — and it is why distribution has to be decided somewhere else |

**Sandel's objection** (*What Money Can't Buy*, 2012): marketising some goods
**corrupts** them — paid queuing, paid blood donation, paid line-standing in
Congress. The good changes character when priced.

**The economist's reply, steelmanned:** the trade-off exists whether or not
you price it. Refusing to price organ donation does not eliminate the
allocation decision; it moves it to waiting lists and death. Al Roth's work on
"repugnant markets" and matching mechanisms (kidney exchange chains) is the
serious response: build allocation systems that respect the repugnance
constraint while still clearing.

**Both are right about different things, and this is a TIER 4 question with
TIER 1 consequences.**

## Behavioural: is "value" even coherent?

Kahneman and Tversky's **prospect theory** (1979): people evaluate outcomes
relative to a **reference point**, not in absolute levels; losses hurt roughly
twice as much as equivalent gains please; and probabilities are weighted
non-linearly. Add the endowment effect (you value what you hold more than what
you'd pay for it), framing effects, and preference reversals.

**The deep challenge:** if valuations depend on framing and reference point,
"preferences" cannot be a stable primitive, and welfare economics loses its
foundation. Whose preferences count — the framed ones or the ones you'd have
under reflection? Nobody has a satisfying answer.

**Necessary caveat.** Parts of behavioural economics have replicated poorly.
Ego depletion and social priming largely failed replication. Nudge effect
sizes in large field trials came in far below the published literature. And
the field suffered severe reputational damage from the 2021-23 data
fabrication findings involving prominent researchers in the honesty-and-ethics
subfield. **Prospect theory itself is robust; the popular-book layer built on
top of it is much weaker than it looks.**

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Things have an intrinsic value" | Marginalism dissolved this in 1871. Value is a relation, not a property |
| "Price should reflect cost" | Cost constrains supply; it does not determine price. Both blades of the scissors |
| "The labour theory of value was disproved" | Too crude. It fails as a price theory and the transformation problem is a real technical failure. As a theory of surplus and distribution it is coherent and remains influential |
| "Marx thought prices equal labour values" | He explicitly did not — Volume III is about why they diverge. He failed to complete the derivation, which is different |
| "Adam Smith was a free market absolutist" | He wrote *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, worried about merchant collusion, and supported public education and regulation of banking. The "invisible hand" appears three times in his whole corpus |
| "Hayek proved markets are always better" | He proved something narrower and stronger: prices aggregate dispersed knowledge. The same argument tells you exactly where they fail |
| "Coase showed regulation is unnecessary" | Coase showed transaction costs determine which institution is efficient. He assumed zero transaction costs to demonstrate they matter |
| "Tragedy of the commons means commons fail" | Ostrom documented hundreds of commons that don't. Hardin was describing open access |
| "Markets are efficient" | Efficient at allocating given a distribution of purchasing power. That is a much smaller claim than it sounds |
| "Nudges are a well-established, powerful tool" | Effect sizes in large field trials are much smaller than the popular literature suggests |
| "GDP measures how well we're doing" | It measures marketed output. Kuznets, who built it in the 1930s, warned explicitly against this use |

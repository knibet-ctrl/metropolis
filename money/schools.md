# Schools of Economic Thought

> **STATUS: TIER 2 throughout.** No school is right and none is worthless.
> Each is best understood as a set of questions plus an assumption about which
> mechanism dominates. Their disagreements are usually about *which mechanism
> is omitted from the other's model* — not about facts.

> Written from general knowledge without source verification.

> **Rule for this file: every school is stated as its best adherents would
> state it.** If a summary here would make a serious practitioner wince, it is
> a bug.

---

## Why economists disagree

Three reasons, and it is worth diagnosing which is operating before assuming
bad faith:

| Reason | Signature | Resolvable? |
|---|---|---|
| **Different models** | Each side's model omits the mechanism the other thinks is central | In principle, by evidence — but macro evidence is weak |
| **Different values** | The objective function differs: employment vs price stability, present vs future, efficiency vs distribution | **No.** These are TIER 4 |
| **Different data readings** | Same series, different sample, window, or identification strategy | Sometimes, slowly |

Most public "economists disagree" stories are the second kind presented as the
first.

## The comparison table

| School | Core claim | Strongest point | Strongest objection | Predicted well | Got wrong |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Classical** (Smith 1776, Ricardo 1817, Mill, Say) | Markets self-regulate; value from production cost/labour; supply creates its own demand | Comparative advantage (Ricardo, 1817) is one of the few genuinely counterintuitive true results in social science | Say's Law fails when money is hoarded — demand deficiency is real | Gains from trade; the division of labour; the danger of merchant collusion | Persistent involuntary unemployment; the value problem |
| **Marxian** (Marx 1867, Luxemburg, Sweezy) | Value from socially necessary labour; profit is surplus value; accumulation is crisis-prone | Capitalism's tendency to concentration, recurring crisis, and the reserve army of labour are real observed dynamics. The best available theory of *why* profit exists as a category | Transformation problem unsolved (Bortkiewicz 1907); prices can't be derived; every planned implementation performed badly | Concentration of capital; globalisation of production; recurring crises; commodification spreading into new domains | Immiseration of the working class in rich countries; the falling rate of profit; revolution in advanced economies |
| **Neoclassical** (Marshall 1890, Walras, Arrow-Debreu 1954) | Optimising agents, marginal analysis, general equilibrium | Marginalism solved the value problem; general equilibrium is a genuine intellectual achievement; the welfare theorems are precise about exactly when markets are efficient | Requires assumptions (complete markets, no externalities, rational agents) that are known false. Equilibrium existence proofs say nothing about stability or attainment | Relative price behaviour; the effects of price controls; the incidence of taxes | Aggregate instability; finance; anything involving crises |
| **Keynesian** (Keynes 1936; Hicks IS-LM 1937; Samuelson) | Output is demand-determined in the short run; equilibrium can persist at high unemployment; animal spirits and uncertainty matter | The 1930s. Also: uncertainty (not risk) is genuinely different, expectations are not always well-founded, and the paradox of thrift is real. Keynes's *Chapter 12* on expectations is better than most of what came after | The 1970s. The naive Phillips curve trade-off collapsed; the framework had no adequate account of supply shocks or expectations | The Depression; the effectiveness of fiscal policy at the zero lower bound; the danger of premature austerity in 2010-12 | Stagflation; the stability of the Phillips relation; treating expectations as adaptive |
| **Monetarist** (Friedman, Schwartz 1963; Brunner, Meltzer) | Money supply drives nominal income; the private sector is stable; policy should follow rules not discretion | **They won the 1970s argument.** Friedman (1968) and Phelps predicted the breakdown of the Phillips trade-off *before* it happened. The Depression-as-monetary-failure account reshaped central banking | Velocity became unstable after ~1980, breaking the operating rule. Money aggregates lost their forecasting content in low-inflation economies | Stagflation; the 1930s Fed failure; long-run neutrality of money; the case for central bank independence | Money-supply targeting as a practical rule — abandoned by the Fed in 1982 after three years; predicted inflation after 2009 QE that never came |
| **Austrian** (Menger 1871, Mises, Hayek, Kirzner, Rothbard) | Subjective value; markets as a discovery process; prices as dispersed knowledge; capital has structure and time dimension; business cycles from credit-induced malinvestment | **Hayek 1945 is one of the best papers in social science.** Mises's 1920 calculation critique was vindicated in the most expensive natural experiment ever run. The emphasis on capital heterogeneity and entrepreneurial discovery is a real gap in neoclassical theory | The Austrian business cycle theory has weak empirical support and is largely untested by design — the *praxeological* methodological stance rejects empirical testing, which insulates it from evidence. The persistent prediction of hyperinflation after 2009 was a public, dated failure | Central planning's failure; the informational role of prices; credit booms preceding busts | Post-2008 inflation (badly, repeatedly). The methodological rejection of econometrics has left the school with few checkable claims |
| **New Classical / RBC** (Lucas 1976, Kydland & Prescott 1982, Sargent) | Rational expectations; markets clear; fluctuations are optimal responses to real (technology) shocks | **The Lucas critique is devastating and correct**: policy regime changes alter the behavioural relations you estimated, so you cannot exploit historical correlations. Time inconsistency (Kydland-Prescott) is why central banks are independent | RBC's claim that recessions are optimal responses to negative technology shocks strains credibility — what technology was forgotten in 2009? Involuntary unemployment is assumed away | The instability of estimated policy relations; the value of rules and commitment | The Great Recession, comprehensively. Models with no financial sector |
| **New Keynesian** (Mankiw, Woodford 2003, Clarida-Galí-Gertler; the DSGE mainstream) | Rational expectations plus sticky prices and monopolistic competition; monetary policy has real effects; this is the workhorse of central banks | Microfoundations plus real frictions. Provides an actual operating framework for inflation targeting, which produced the Great Moderation | **Pre-2008 versions had no banks, no default, no balance sheets.** The models could not represent the crisis they were meant to guard against. Post-crisis financial frictions were bolted on rather than derived | Inflation dynamics in normal times; the case for credible targets | 2008. The whole thing |
| **MMT** (Mosler, Wray, Kelton, Mitchell; from Knapp 1905, Lerner 1943, Godley, Minsky) | A currency-issuing sovereign with floating FX and own-currency debt faces an inflation constraint, not a financing constraint. Taxes drive currency demand; the deficit is not the relevant variable | **Right about the decade 2010-2019**: no bond vigilantes, no crowding out, no inflation, and the deficit panic was misplaced. Its operational description of money creation is accepted by central bankers | The inflation constraint binds earlier and less legibly than the framework implies; the corrective (raising taxes to withdraw stimulus) assumes a legislature that has never existed; exchange-rate and imported-inflation constraints are underweighted, so the theory is weakest for the countries that most want it | QE's non-inflationary character; the 2010-12 austerity error; sectoral balance dynamics | The scale of the 2021 inflationary response to direct fiscal transfers |
| **Behavioural** (Kahneman & Tversky 1979, Thaler, Shiller) | Agents are predictably non-rational; reference dependence, loss aversion, bounded self-control | Prospect theory is one of the best-replicated findings in social science. Shiller's excess-volatility work is a serious challenge to efficient markets | It is a catalogue of deviations, not a theory — no unified alternative model of behaviour. **And parts of the field have replication problems**; the popular layer is much weaker than the core | Asset price bubbles; default effects in retirement saving; the equity premium puzzle's behavioural component | Many published effect sizes; nudge magnitudes in field trials; some prominent findings were fabricated |
| **Institutional / New Institutional** (Veblen, Commons, North 1990, Ostrom 1990, Acemoglu-Robinson) | Rules, property rights, enforcement and norms determine outcomes; "the market" is an institution, not a state of nature | Explains persistent cross-country differences that neither geography nor prices explain. Ostrom's commons work is a genuine empirical correction to both market and state orthodoxy | The popular version verges on unfalsifiable: good institutions cause growth, and we identify good institutions by growth. Endogeneity is severe | Post-communist transition outcomes varying by institutional inheritance; the persistence of colonial-era arrangements | China, which is a standing problem for the inclusive-institutions thesis |
| **Structuralist / development** (Prebisch, Amsden, Wade, Chang, Rodrik) | Late developers face different constraints; industrial policy and sequencing matter; free trade advice is not what rich countries followed | **East Asia is the evidence.** Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, China all used directed credit, capital controls, and export discipline. Chang's *Kicking Away the Ladder* (2002) documents that today's advocates of free trade industrialised behind protection | Industrial policy has a long list of expensive failures, and the successes share features (export discipline, competent bureaucracy, geopolitical support) that are hard to replicate. Selection on the dependent variable is a real risk | East Asian success; the failure of rapid liberalisation sequencing | Import-substitution industrialisation in Latin America, which mostly did not work |
| **Ecological / feminist** (Georgescu-Roegen, Daly; Waring, Folbre) | The economy is a subsystem of a finite biosphere; unpaid care work is systematically excluded from measurement | Both identify things national accounting genuinely omits. GDP excludes household production and treats resource depletion as income. Kuznets warned about this himself | Limited operational apparatus — clear about what is wrong with the measure, less clear about what replaces it. Degrowth's macroeconomics are underdeveloped | The mismeasurement critique has been broadly accepted; satellite accounts now exist | Specific catastrophist timelines |

## Where they actually agree

Underreported, and it is a lot:

- Comparative advantage and gains from trade (magnitudes and distribution
  disputed, direction not)
- Price controls produce shortages or surpluses
- Incentives change behaviour
- Long-run inflation is monetary
- The importance of property rights and contract enforcement
- Rent control reduces housing supply over time (one of the highest-consensus
  results in the field, and one of the least popular)
- Opportunity cost, sunk costs, marginal analysis
- **That macro forecasting is unreliable** — the disagreement is over which
  model is least unreliable

## Where the disagreement is really about values

Label these honestly. They are TIER 4 wearing lab coats:

| Apparent dispute | Actual disagreement |
|---|---|
| Optimal inflation target | How much unemployment is an acceptable price for price stability, and *who* bears it |
| Social discount rate | How much future people count |
| Optimal top tax rate | Weight on distribution vs efficiency, plus contested elasticities |
| Deficits | Intergenerational obligation, and how much you trust future legislatures |
| Immigration's effects | Whose welfare counts — natives, migrants, or everyone |
| Minimum wage | Genuinely empirical *and* genuinely about values. Card-Krueger (1994) and the subsequent literature moved the profession substantially toward small disemployment effects at moderate levels; that is a real empirical update, not a political one |

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Keynesian vs Austrian is the main axis" | Almost no working macroeconomist is either. The mainstream is New Keynesian; the live disputes are within it |
| "Economists all believe in free markets" | Survey evidence shows large majorities supporting carbon taxes, redistribution and public health insurance in various forms. The profession is well to the left of its public image on most policy questions and to the right on trade and rent control |
| "Neoliberalism is an economic school" | It is a political-historical term, used mainly by critics. Nobody self-identifies as one, which makes it a poor analytical category |
| "Austrians are just libertarians" | Menger and Hayek are serious economists whose contributions (subjective value, dispersed knowledge, capital structure) are broadly accepted. The political layer is separable and Hayek's own politics were more moderate than his reputation |
| "MMT says deficits don't matter" | It says the *inflation* constraint matters and the financing constraint doesn't. That is a real claim, and a narrower one than the caricature |
| "Marxist economics was refuted" | The price theory failed. The analysis of accumulation, concentration and crisis remains influential across the social sciences. Different claims |
| "Behavioural economics overturned rational choice" | It documented systematic deviations. It has not produced a replacement general framework |
| "Economics is just ideology" | Some of it is. The parts with clean identification and replication — trade gains, price controls, tax incidence, rent control — are as solid as anything in social science |

## Open questions

- [ ] Can DSGE modelling be repaired with financial frictions, or is the whole
      representative-agent equilibrium approach the wrong object?
- [ ] Is there a testable version of Austrian business cycle theory? If the
      methodology forbids testing, in what sense is it economics?
- [ ] Does MMT's operational description actually imply its policy programme,
      or are they logically separable? (Several central bankers accept the
      first and reject the second, which suggests they are.)
- [ ] Can institutional explanations be made falsifiable, or are they
      permanently circular?
- [ ] Why has the profession's forecasting record not improved despite
      enormous gains in data and computation? This is the uncomfortable one.
- [ ] Is heterogeneous-agent modelling (HANK and successors) a genuine advance
      or a more complicated way of being wrong?

## The functional definition

Money is whatever performs three jobs. The definition is circular and everyone
knows it; it survives because it is useful.

| Function | Test | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| **Medium of exchange** | Sellers accept it without wanting it | Acceptance network collapses |
| **Unit of account** | Contracts and prices are denominated in it | Inflation makes it a bad measuring rod |
| **Store of value** | Purchasing power persists | Inflation, or confiscation |

The three come apart under stress, and that is diagnostic. In Argentina people
have long quoted property prices in US dollars, bought coffee in pesos, and
saved in dollars or bricks. Three monies, three jobs. **When you see the
functions separating, the monetary system is telling you something.**

A better one-line definition, and the one this notebook uses:

> **Money is a transferable record of credit that a large enough group agrees
> to treat as final settlement.**

Kocherlakota's "Money Is Memory" (*Journal of Economic Theory*, 1996) proved a
version of this formally: in a class of models, money is *equivalent* to a
perfect public record of everyone's past contributions. Money is what you use
when you cannot remember who owes what — which is to say, among strangers.

## The two theories

### Commodity / catallactic theory

**Claim:** money emerges spontaneously from exchange. Carl Menger, "On the
Origin of Money" (*Economic Journal*, 1892), gives the strongest version:
goods differ in *saleability* (Absatzfähigkeit). Self-interested traders
accumulate the more saleable goods as intermediate steps. The process is
self-reinforcing; one good wins. No state, no agreement, no design.

**Steelman:** this is a genuinely excellent piece of reasoning. It explains
convergence on a single money without central direction, predicts the physical
properties winners will have (durable, divisible, portable, fungible,
recognisable, scarce), and correctly predicts money emerging in prisons, POW
camps and prisons-of-the-market like post-collapse economies. Radford's "The
Economic Organisation of a P.O.W. Camp" (*Economica*, 1945) documents
cigarettes becoming money in Stalag VII-A, complete with a price level, a
Gresham's-law dynamic as prisoners hand-rolled thinner cigarettes, and
deflation when Red Cross deliveries stopped.

**Objection:** it is a story about how money *could* arise, presented for 250
years as how it *did*. And the POW camp is not evidence for the origin
question — those men already knew what money was. It shows re-emergence, not
emergence.

### Credit / chartalist theory

**Claim:** the unit of account and the recording of debt come first; the
circulating token is a late convenience. Two strands:

- **Chartalism** — G.F. Knapp, *The State Theory of Money* (1905). Money is a
  creature of law: the state names the unit and, crucially, chooses what it
  accepts in payment of taxes. Keynes endorsed this opening his *Treatise on
  Money* (1930), writing that money of account had been the primary concept
  "for some four thousand years at least."
- **Credit theory** — A. Mitchell Innes, "What Is Money?" (*Banking Law
  Journal*, 1913). All money is credit; a coin is a token of a debt
  relationship, and its metal content is incidental.

**Steelman:** taxation demonstrably creates currency demand. Colonial
administrations in Africa imposed hut and poll taxes payable only in the
colonial currency, explicitly to force subsistence households into the cash
economy and wage labour. This is not theory; it is documented administrative
policy. It shows the mechanism works.

**Objection:** it over-generalises. Currencies circulate across borders where
no tax obligation exists; dollarisation happens *against* the state's wishes;
and the theory struggles to explain why some state monies fail anyway. Taxes
create a floor under demand. They do not fix the value.

## The barter myth

**This is the largest single error in the popular understanding of economics,
and it is in the textbooks.**

Adam Smith, *Wealth of Nations* Book I Chapter IV (1776), tells the story: a
butcher, a brewer and a baker; the "double coincidence of wants"; the
inconvenience of barter; the invention of money as a solution. Nearly every
introductory textbook since has reproduced it.

**The anthropological record does not contain this economy.** The canonical
statement is Caroline Humphrey (1985): no example of a barter economy, pure
and simple, has ever been described, let alone the emergence of money from
one. This is not a fringe view among anthropologists; it is the consensus, and
it has been for decades.

What the record shows instead:

| Evidence | Date | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Mesopotamian temple and palace accounts | c.3000 BCE | Debts denominated in weights of silver (the *shekel*) and volumes of barley, settled by ledger. Silver often never moved |
| Cuneiform loan tablets, interest rates, debt amnesties | c.2400 BCE onward | Interest, collateral, and periodic royal debt cancellations (*mīšarum*, later the Jubilee tradition) |
| First coinage, Lydia | c.610-560 BCE | Roughly 2,400 years *after* the accounting |
| English exchequer tallies | c.1100-1826 | Split hazel sticks recording Crown debt, circulating as transferable claims. The 1834 burning of the accumulated tallies set fire to the Palace of Westminster |
| Rai stones, Yap | pre-modern | Enormous limestone discs, ownership transferred verbally without moving the stone; one at the bottom of the sea remained good money |

Milton Friedman wrote about Yap in "The Island of Stone Money" (1991) and drew
the right conclusion: the stones are a ledger. He paired it with the Bank of
France's 1932-33 practice of asking the New York Fed to *earmark* gold in its
vaults rather than ship it — labels changed, metal stayed put, and markets
reacted as though gold had crossed the Atlantic. **A monetarist and an
anarchist anthropologist agree that money is a record. That convergence is
worth more than either alone.**

Where barter *does* occur: between strangers or enemies with no shared credit
relationship, at trade frontiers, and — importantly — *after* a monetary
system collapses. Russia in the 1990s ran enormous inter-enterprise barter.
Argentina's *clubes de trueque* after the 2001-02 crisis had millions of
participants. **Barter is a symptom of monetary breakdown, not a precursor to
monetary invention.**

## On Graeber, carefully

David Graeber's *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (2011) is the reason most people
have heard any of the above. It deserves credit and it deserves caution.

| Holds up | Does not |
|---|---|
| The absence of barter economies (this is Humphrey's and the discipline's finding, not Graeber's) | Specific factual errors, some corrected in later printings — a widely-cited passage about Apple Computer being founded by IBM engineers in a garage is simply wrong |
| Credit predating coinage in the Near East | Several economists dispute his reading of Smith and of "the myth of barter" as a deliberate ideological construction |
| Debt cancellation as a recurring historical institution | Some Assyriological and classical specifics have been challenged by area experts |
| Coinage correlating with warfare, slavery and standing armies (the "military-coinage-slavery complex") | The causal story here is a hypothesis presented with more confidence than the evidence carries |

He was also an avowed anarchist writing a book with a thesis. That is not
disqualifying — everyone has one — but it means the specifics need checking.

**The correct position: the anthropology is right and Graeber is unreliable in
detail. These are separate claims and collapsing them is how the argument gets
lost.** Critics who discredit the book think they have rescued the barter
story. They have not; Humphrey's finding predates Graeber by 26 years.

## What actually makes a good money

Menger's checklist is a bad theory of origins and an excellent description of
selection pressure:

| Property | Why | Failure case |
|---|---|---|
| Durable | Must survive storage | Perishable commodity monies |
| Divisible | Must make change | Cattle, Rai stones |
| Portable | Value density | Iron, salt |
| Fungible | Units interchangeable | Diamonds — every stone differs |
| Recognisable | Cheap verification | Counterfeiting; hallmarking and coinage exist to solve this |
| Scarce / supply-inelastic | Or it inflates away | Cowries in West Africa after industrial-scale European imports |
| **Legally and socially anchored** | The one Menger underweights | Any collapsed currency |

## The hierarchy of money

Minsky's line, developed by Perry Mehrling: **anyone can create money; the
problem is getting it accepted.** Money is a hierarchy of promises, and in a
crisis the hierarchy becomes visible as everyone scrambles up it.

```
        gold / ultimate settlement asset (historically)
                        |
              central bank reserves & currency
                        |
                  bank deposits
                        |
        money market funds, repo, commercial paper
                        |
              corporate & personal IOUs
```

In normal times these all trade at par and feel like the same thing. In a
crisis, par breaks — the Reserve Primary Fund "broke the buck" on 16 September
2008 — and everyone discovers which layer they were actually holding.

**Your bank deposit is not money in your possession. It is an unsecured loan
you have made to a commercial bank.** Most people do not know this, and it is
the single most useful fact in this file. Deposit insurance exists precisely
because the fact is true.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Barter came first, then money" | No such economy has ever been documented. Credit and accounting come first in the record |
| "Money was invented to solve the double coincidence of wants" | Plausible mechanism, no evidence it is the historical one |
| "Money must be backed by something" | No currency in the world is, and they function. Backing is one anchor among several, not a requirement |
| "Fiat money is a recent aberration" | Song dynasty China issued paper money in the 11th century; the classical gold standard lasted about 44 years |
| "Cash is money and the rest is derivative" | Backwards. Cash is a small, passively supplied share; deposits are the bulk |
| "The gold standard meant stable prices" | Low *average* inflation, high volatility, and the deflation of 1873-96 was severe and politically explosive |
| "Bitcoin is money" | It performs one of three functions well. Almost nothing on earth is priced in it, including by holders |
| "Your money is in the bank" | Your money *is* the bank's promise. You are a creditor |
| "Gresham's law: bad money drives out good" | Only under a fixed legal exchange rate between them. Without that, the good money wins — which is what dollarisation is |

## Open questions

- [ ] Is the credit-first record general, or an artefact of what survives?
      Clay tablets last; anything a barter society wrote down would not.
- [ ] Can the tax-driven-demand mechanism be quantified against network
      effects? No convincing identification strategy exists.
- [ ] What exactly happens at the moment a currency's acceptance convention
      breaks? The dynamics are non-linear and poorly modelled.
- [ ] Does a permissionless digital money genuinely escape the trust question,
      or does it relocate trust from an institution to a protocol and a set of
      exchanges — which are institutions?
- [ ] Is "money is memory" a deep result or an artefact of the model class in
      which it was proved?
- [ ] Why does the barter myth persist in textbooks 40 years after the
      anthropology settled it? The sociology-of-knowledge question here is
      more interesting than the monetary one.

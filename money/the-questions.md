# The Questions — Disaggregated

> **STATUS: MIXED — TIER 1 through TIER 4.** This file is a router, not an
> answer. Its claim is that "what is money?" is at least five separate
> questions with different methods, different evidence bases and wildly
> different confidence levels. That claim is TIER 1. The answers are not.

> Written from general knowledge without source verification.

---

## The problem

"What is money?" is asked as one question and answered with whichever of five
questions the answerer happens to work on. An economist answers *what does it
do*. An anthropologist answers *where did it come from*. A monetary operations
specialist answers *who creates it*. A philosopher answers *what makes it
valuable*. A political theorist answers *what is it for*. All five give
correct answers to different questions and then argue.

**Sort the question first.** Most monetary argument on the internet is two
people answering different questions at each other.

**The key asymmetry:** Q4's mechanics are among the most solidly established
facts in the whole notebook — they are literally double-entry bookkeeping,
published by the institutions that do it — and they are also the most widely
misunderstood. Established does not mean known.

## Q1 — What makes something function as money?

The standard functional answer, dating to Jevons (1875) and stable since:

| Function | What it means | Can fail independently |
|---|---|---|
| **Medium of exchange** | Accepted in trade without the seller wanting it for itself | Yes — gold today |
| **Unit of account** | Prices, debts and contracts are *denominated* in it | Yes — dollarised pricing in Argentina |
| **Store of value** | Purchasing power survives across time | Yes — any inflating currency |
| *(sometimes)* **Standard of deferred payment** | Debts settle in it | Usually collapses into unit of account |

These come apart constantly, and that is the useful part. In high-inflation
economies people routinely price in dollars (unit of account), transact in
local currency (medium of exchange), and save in property or dollars (store of
value). **Three different objects doing the three jobs is the normal state of a
stressed monetary system, not an anomaly.**

Bitcoin is a clean modern test: strong-ish store of value claim, weak medium of
exchange, essentially no unit of account role — almost nothing is priced in it,
including by people who hold it.

**Confidence: TIER 1.** This is a description, not a theory. Its weakness is
that it is close to unfalsifiable — it tells you what money does, not what
money *is*.

## Q2 — Where did money come from?

Two rival stories, and one strong negative finding.

| Account | Claim | Best evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Commodity / catallactic** (Smith 1776; Menger 1892) | Barter → a most-saleable commodity emerges → coinage | Menger's saleability argument is genuinely elegant; POW camp cigarettes (Radford 1945) | Theoretically strong, **historically unsupported** |
| **Credit / chartalist** (Innes 1913; Knapp 1905; Graeber 2011) | Credit and accounting first; coin much later; the state defines the unit | Mesopotamian temple accounts c.3000 BCE; coinage only c.600 BCE Lydia | Better fit to the record, **weaker as a general theory** |
| **Negative finding** (Humphrey 1985 and the ethnographic record) | No pure barter economy has ever been observed | Absence across the whole ethnographic corpus | **TIER 1 as a negative claim** |

The negative result is much stronger than either positive story. We know the
textbook origin myth is wrong. We do not have a single confident replacement.

## Q3 — What gives money value?

| Answer | Mechanism | Strongest evidence | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Commodity backing** | Redeemable for a real thing | Gold standard era | Every modern currency has none and still works |
| **State demand / taxes** | The state accepts only its own unit for taxes, so everyone needs it | Colonial hut taxes explicitly created currency demand | Doesn't explain currencies that survive weak states, or dollarisation |
| **Legal tender law** | Compulsion | Real but thin | Laws haven't saved collapsing currencies |
| **Network effect / convention** | Valuable because others accept it (a coordination equilibrium) | Explains persistence, multiple equilibria, and sudden collapse | Explains *stability*, not *origin*; permits self-fulfilling failure |
| **Expected future purchasing power** | Backward induction on the above | Explains currency collapse as an expectations phenomenon | Circular unless anchored by one of the above |

**The honest answer is "several of these at once, in proportions that differ
by country and era."** Currency collapses are the natural experiment: they
happen when the fiscal anchor goes *and* the convention breaks, which is why
they are fast and non-linear.

**Confidence: TIER 2.**

## Q4 — Who creates money?

Split into three sub-questions with sharply different tiers:

| Sub-question | Answer | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanically, whose balance sheet expands? | Commercial banks create the bulk of broad money by lending. Central banks create reserves and cash. | **1** — accounting, published by central banks |
| What constrains it? | Capital, profitability, creditworthy demand, regulation, and the policy rate. **Not** a reserve multiplier. | **1-2** |
| What are the consequences of a given path of money creation? | Genuinely disputed. | **2** |

This is the file where the myth density is highest. See
how-money-is-created.md.

## Q5 — What is money for?

Not empirically answerable. This is where values live and they should be
labelled as such:

- Should money be neutral infrastructure or an instrument of policy?
- Is seigniorage a legitimate revenue source or a hidden tax?
- Should the price level be stable, or should employment come first when they
  conflict? (They do conflict, sometimes.)
- Should there be things money cannot buy? (Sandel yes; most economists say
  the trade-off happens whether or not you price it.)
- Who should bear the cost of disinflation? This is the sharpest one, because
  the cost is real, someone bears it, and *which* someone is a political
  choice dressed as a technical one.

**Confidence: TIER 4.** No procedure settles these. Anyone presenting an
answer to Q5 as an empirical finding is doing something else.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Popular framing | What's actually going on |
|---|---|
| "What is money?" is one question | It is at least five, with tiers from 1 to 4 |
| Economists agree on the basics and differ on politics | Partly backwards. They largely agree on Q4's *mechanics* and disagree on Q3 and on consequences |
| Monetary disputes are about facts | Many are about which mechanism a model omits, or about the objective function — see schools.md |
| "Money is just a social construct, therefore arbitrary" | Social constructs can be extremely hard to change and can kill you. Constructed ≠ optional |
| The gold standard was the natural state of money | The classical gold standard ran roughly 1870-1914. Forty-four years. Most monetary history is not metallic |
| Somebody knows the answer to Q5 | Nobody does. It isn't that kind of question |

## Open questions

- [ ] Is the credit-first account *general*, or is it a fact about the
      particular societies whose records survive? Cuneiform survives because
      clay does. Selection bias may be doing real work here.
- [ ] Can Menger's saleability argument be rescued as an account of *unit of
      account* emergence even if barter never happened?
- [ ] What is the actual empirical weight of the tax-driven-demand mechanism
      versus network effects in sustaining a modern currency? Nobody has a
      clean identification strategy.
- [ ] Does a purely private digital money change any of the answers to Q3, or
      does it just relocate the trust question to a protocol?
- [ ] Is there a defensible sense in which Q5 has *partial* empirical content —
      e.g. via revealed preference across monetary regimes — or is that
      smuggling values back in?

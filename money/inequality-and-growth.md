# Inequality and Growth

> **STATUS: TIER 1 that the modern growth episode happened. TIER 2-3 on why.
> TIER 2 on inequality measurement. TIER 2 on r > g. TIER 4 on whether
> inequality is bad.** "Why are some countries rich?" is plausibly the largest
> unresolved question in social science, and every confident answer is
> politically convenient to someone.

> Written from general knowledge without source verification. Growth estimates
> before 1900 are reconstructions with wide error bars.

---

## The single most important fact

For almost all of human history, output per person grew at roughly **0.1% per
year or less** — imperceptible within a lifetime. Then, somewhere between 1750
and 1850 in northwest Europe, it went to 1-2% per year and stayed there.

| Period | Approx. growth in output per person | Doubling time |
|---|---|---|
| 1 CE - 1500 | ~0.01-0.05%/yr | Millennia |
| 1500 - 1820 | ~0.05-0.1%/yr | ~1,000 years |
| 1820 - 2000 (industrialised) | ~1.5-2%/yr | ~40 years |

(Estimates from Angus Maddison's project; the reconstructions are contested in
detail and the *shape* is not.)

McCloskey calls it the Great Enrichment. **A roughly thirtyfold increase in
material living standards in the countries that experienced it, and a gap of
50-100x between the richest and poorest countries today.** Explaining the
timing and the location of that break is the central question of economic
history, and it is not solved.

## Candidate explanations

Each is live. Each is endogenous to the others, which is why identification is
so hard.

| Explanation | Champions | Best evidence | Best objection |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Institutions** | North; Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson; Nobel 2024 | "Colonial Origins" (2001) uses settler mortality as an instrument for institutions. "Reversal of Fortune" (2002): places rich in 1500 are poor now, which geography cannot explain. Nogales/Nogales, the Koreas | **Albouy (2012)** challenged the settler mortality data — coding, small samples, high-mortality outliers doing the work. AJR responded; not fully resolved. And the popular version is near-circular. **China is a standing anomaly** |
| **Geography** | Diamond (*Guns, Germs and Steel*, 1997); Sachs | Continental axes and domesticable species (Diamond); tropical disease burden, landlockedness, transport costs (Sachs) | Cannot explain the Koreas, Botswana vs Zimbabwe, or reversals of fortune. Explains 1500 far better than 2000 |
| **Culture** | Weber (1905); Landes; **Mokyr, *A Culture of Growth* (2017)** | Mokyr's is the serious version: the Republic of Letters, Baconian belief that knowledge should be *useful*, competitive fragmentation of European states preventing suppression of heterodox ideas | Cultural explanations are unfalsifiable ex post and have an appalling track record. **The same "Confucian values" explained why East Asia couldn't grow before 1960 and why it grew after 1980** |
| **Human capital** | Glaeser et al. (2004) | Schooling levels precede institutional improvement in the data, suggesting causation runs from human capital to institutions rather than the reverse | Measurement of "institutions" is bad enough that the ordering test is weak |
| **Energy and technology** | Wrigley; Allen; Smil | Every growth episode coincides with an energy transition. Allen's high-wage-economy argument: British wages were high and coal cheap, so labour-saving machinery paid there and nowhere else | Doesn't explain why the *scientific* culture that made machines usable was where it was |
| **Colonial extraction and slavery** | Williams (1944); Beckert; Baptist | The profits were real, the cotton-textile complex was central to British industrialisation, and the moral case is not in dispute | The **quantitative** share is genuinely disputed. Engerman and others find colonial profits were small relative to British GDP and investment. **Baptist's cotton productivity figures were sharply criticised by Olmstead and Rhode (2018) on measurement grounds.** Both "it was everything" and "it was irrelevant" are unsupportable |

**The honest conclusion: multi-causal, deeply endogenous, and no single-factor
account survives contact with the full set of cases.** Anyone who tells you it
was institutions, or geography, or coal, or colonialism, is compressing a
TIER 2-3 debate into a slogan.

## The growth accounting embarrassment

**Solow (1956):** decompose growth into capital deepening, labour, and a
residual. The residual — Total Factor Productivity — is meant to capture
technology.

**In practice the residual accounts for the majority of measured growth in
developed economies.** Abramovitz called it "the measure of our ignorance,"
and the name stuck because it is accurate. Growth accounting tells you how
much you cannot explain, precisely.

**Romer (1990, Nobel 2018)** endogenised it: ideas are **non-rival** — one
person using an idea doesn't stop another — so they generate increasing
returns and growth can be self-sustaining rather than exogenous. This is a
genuine theoretical advance. It does not tell you why some countries generate
and adopt ideas and others don't.

**Convergence:** the neoclassical prediction is that poor countries grow
faster and catch up. **Pritchett's "Divergence, Big Time" (1997)** documented
the opposite for 1870-1990. Conditional convergence — convergence among
countries with similar characteristics — has better support, which is close to
saying "similar countries end up similar." More recently, Patel, Sandefur and
Subramanian (2021) argue unconditional convergence has actually appeared since
around 2000. **Live, and the sample window drives the answer.**

## Measuring inequality: harder than growth

Every choice below changes the number, sometimes the sign of the trend:

| Choice | Options | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Unit | Individual, household, equivalised household | Household size changes have masked real trends |
| Concept | Income, consumption, wealth | Consumption inequality is consistently lower and rises less |
| Stage | Market income, gross, post-tax-and-transfer | Redistribution shrinks Gini by 10-20 points in European states |
| Source | Surveys, tax records, national accounts | **Surveys miss the top; tax records miss the informal.** The gap between them is the whole argument in some countries |
| Scope | Within-country, between-country, global-individual | These move in *opposite directions* |
| Statistic | Gini, top 1% share, 90/10 ratio, poverty headcount | Gini is insensitive to the top tail; top shares are insensitive to the bottom |

**Two facts that are both true and usually presented as contradictory:**

1. **Global inequality between individuals has fallen substantially since
   ~1990**, driven overwhelmingly by China and secondarily India. Extreme
   poverty fell from roughly 36% of world population (1990) to under 10%
   (2019) — one of the largest improvements in human welfare ever recorded.
2. **Within-country inequality rose in most rich countries over the same
   period.**

Milanović's **elephant curve** displays this — big gains for the global middle
(Asia) and the global top 1%, near-stagnation for the rich world's lower
middle. **Caveat: Corlett (2016) showed the shape is sensitive to whether you
track the same population percentiles over time (anonymous) or the same
people, and to the inclusion of China and the former Soviet bloc.** The
picture survives the critique; the vividness doesn't.

## Piketty: score the data and the theory separately

*Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (French 2013, English 2014).

**Core argument:** when the rate of return on capital $r$ exceeds the growth
rate $g$, existing wealth compounds faster than the economy grows, so capital's
share of income and the concentration of wealth rise unless something
interrupts them. The 20th century's compression was the interruption — two
wars, depression, inflation, and the tax regimes they justified — not the norm.

Two "fundamental laws": $\alpha = r \times \beta$ (capital's income share
equals return times the capital/income ratio — **an identity**) and
$\beta = s/g$ (the steady-state capital/income ratio — **a model result**).

**The serious critiques:**

| Critique | Substance | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| **Rognlie (2015)** | Almost the entire rise in capital's share is **housing**. Net of depreciation, the non-housing capital share is roughly flat. Short-lived capital (software, equipment) depreciates fast, so gross and net diverge | **The strongest one.** It reframes the problem from "capital" to "land and planning restrictions," which implies a completely different policy response |
| Elasticity of substitution | The mechanism needs capital and labour to substitute with elasticity > 1; most micro estimates are below 1 | Real, though Piketty's "capital" is wealth including land, which changes what is being estimated |
| **Giles / *Financial Times* (2014)** | Alleged data construction errors, particularly in the UK wealth series | Piketty responded in detail; most economists judged the core series survived, though the UK numbers were weak. **Reporting this as "Piketty debunked" is wrong** |
| Acemoglu & Robinson (2015) | The "laws" don't hold in panel data once institutions and politics are included | Serious; argues the mechanism is political, not mechanical |
| Logical | $r > g$ is a statement about returns, not automatically about concentration. It needs assumptions about savings rates, inheritance division among heirs, fertility, and taxation | Correct. $r > g$ has held for most of history without producing indefinite concentration |

**What survives and is major:** the **World Inequality Database** — long-run
top income and wealth shares built from tax records rather than surveys, with
Saez, Zucman, Atkinson and many others. This is a large, largely accepted
empirical contribution and it exists independently of whether $r > g$ explains
anything. **Score the data high and the mechanism as contested.**

## Does inequality harm growth?

**Genuinely unresolved.** IMF and OECD papers from the 2010s find negative
associations above some threshold; identification is weak and the results are
sensitive to specification. Mechanisms proposed in both directions:

| Harms growth | Helps growth |
|---|---|
| Credit constraints prevent poor households investing in education | Returns to skill and risk incentivise effort and investment |
| Political capture distorts policy toward incumbents | Concentrated savings finance capital formation |
| Reduced social trust raises transaction costs | |
| Demand shortfalls from high savings at the top | |

*The Spirit Level* (Wilkinson and Pickett, 2009) correlates inequality with a
long list of social ills. **It has been heavily criticised for country
selection and for treating correlations across ~20 rich countries as causal.**
The pattern is suggestive; the book overclaims.

**The most credible micro contribution is Raj Chetty's**: linked
administrative data on intergenerational mobility (the Opportunity Atlas).
Finding: **absolute mobility — earning more than your parents — fell from
around 90% for Americans born in 1940 to around 50% for those born in 1984**,
with the majority of the decline attributable to slower and more unequally
distributed growth rather than to reduced relative mobility. This is unusually
well-identified for this literature.

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| "The rich got richer and the poor got poorer" | Global extreme poverty roughly quartered since 1990. Within-country inequality rose in rich countries. **Both are true and neither is the whole picture** |
| "Piketty was debunked by the FT" | He responded in detail; the core series held. The UK numbers were weak. This is a myth with a long life |
| "Capital's share is rising, so workers are losing to robots" | **Rognlie: it's housing.** Net of depreciation, non-housing capital share is roughly flat. This changes the policy implication entirely |
| "The West got rich from colonialism" | It contributed, and the moral case is separate. The quantitative share is genuinely disputed, and the timing doesn't fit cleanly — Sweden, Switzerland and Japan industrialised without colonial empires |
| "Colonialism had nothing to do with it" | Equally unsupportable. Extraction was real and the institutional legacies are measurable |
| "Institutions explain everything" | China. Also: institutions are endogenous to growth, and measuring them is close to circular |
| "Trickle-down has been disproven" / "proven" | "Trickle-down" is not a theory anyone holds by that name. The real question is the elasticity of investment and labour supply to top tax rates, which is empirically contested with real estimates on both sides |
| "Inequality is at record highs" | In the US, top income shares are near 1920s levels. In much of Europe they are far below historic peaks. Globally, individual inequality has *fallen*. Which claim is true depends entirely on the unit |
| "GDP growth doesn't improve wellbeing past a point" | The Easterlin paradox is contested. Stevenson and Wolfers (2008, 2013) find no satiation point in the cross-country data. Genuinely unresolved |
| "Aid doesn't work" / "aid works" | Macro aid-growth regressions are a graveyard. Micro RCT evidence on specific interventions is much better. Different questions, routinely conflated |

## Open questions

- [ ] Why northwest Europe, and why around 1800? Sixty years of scholarship and
      no consensus. The best answers are conjunctural, which is unsatisfying
      and may be correct.
- [ ] Is China evidence against the inclusive-institutions thesis, a delayed
      confirmation of it, or a case that will look different in thirty years?
      **Everyone's answer correlates suspiciously with their prior.**
- [ ] Can the settler-mortality instrument be rescued, or does the Albouy
      critique kill it? A large literature rests on this.
- [ ] Is there a threshold above which inequality harms growth, and could any
      feasible study identify it?
- [ ] How much of measured productivity growth is mismeasurement of quality
      and free digital goods? Estimates range from trivial to large.
- [ ] Does the Rognlie housing result generalise outside the Anglosphere?
- [ ] Has growth in rich countries slowed permanently (Gordon) or is it a
      measurement and diffusion lag (Brynjolfsson)? A live and consequential
      disagreement between serious people using the same data.

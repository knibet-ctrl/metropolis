## Part 1 — How to read an economic claim

The most useful skill in this notebook. Ten questions, in order.

| # | Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Is this an identity, an empirical claim, or a theoretical claim?** | The single highest-yield question. See below |
| 2 | **Nominal or real?** | A 5% pay rise in 8% inflation is a pay cut |
| 3 | **Level or rate of change — or rate of change of the rate?** | "Inflation is falling" ≠ "prices are falling" |
| 4 | **Stock or flow?** | Debt is a stock; the deficit is a flow. Comparing a stock to a flow (debt to annual GDP) is legitimate but is a *ratio of unlike things* and needs care |
| 5 | **Per what?** | Per capita, per worker, per working-age adult. Japan's "lost decades" largely disappear under the third |
| 6 | **What's the counterfactual?** | "The stimulus failed, unemployment stayed high" requires knowing what would have happened otherwise. Usually unknowable |
| 7 | **What sample, what window?** | Convergence, factor returns, the Simon-Ehrlich wager — all sensitive to the decade chosen |
| 8 | **Is the identification credible?** | RCT, natural experiment, instrument, or a cross-country regression with 30 observations? **Micro identification is often excellent; macro identification is usually poor** |
| 9 | **Does the model make the conclusion inevitable?** | If a model assumes markets clear, it cannot find involuntary unemployment. That's not a finding, it's an assumption |
| 10 | **What would change their mind?** | If nothing would, you are reading a commitment, not an analysis |

### The identity trap, in detail

**This is where most people are fooled, including numerate people.**

An accounting identity is true by construction and contains no causal
information. It constrains stories; it does not tell them.

| Identity | The illegitimate move |
|---|---|
| Private + government + foreign balance = 0 | "Therefore government deficits are necessary / fine / good" |
| MV = PY | "Therefore money growth causes inflation" (needs stable V and independent Y) |
| GDP = C + I + G + (X − M) | "Therefore government spending increases GDP" |
| Deflator = unit labour costs + unit profits + unit taxes | "Therefore profits caused inflation" |
| Current account = −capital account | "Therefore trade deficits are debt" |

**The test:** ask whether the statement could conceivably be false. If not, it
is an identity, and any conclusion drawn from it needs an extra empirical
premise — which the speaker has probably not stated and may not have noticed.

**Both political directions do this constantly.** Noticing it is not a
partisan act.

### Fast heuristics

- **"Studies show" without a study is nothing.** Ask which one.
- **A number without a denominator is a rhetorical device.** "£350 million a
  week" — per what, net of what?
- **Beware of the word "costs the economy."** Almost always a gross figure
  with no counterfactual and no netting.
- **Distinguish "X is associated with Y" from "X causes Y" from "X causes Y
  *and the effect is large enough to matter*."** Three different claims,
  routinely compressed into one.
- **Forecast horizon is inversely related to reliability.** Macro forecasts
  beyond about a year are close to worthless; this is well documented and
  rarely disclosed.
- **When an economist's finding is exactly what their politics would want, the
  prior should shift.** Including when you agree with them.

## Part 2 — Where the personal-finance evidence is genuinely strong

Short list. Everything on it is either arithmetic or replicated across
decades, countries and datasets.

### Costs compound, and this is arithmetic, not prediction

A 1% annual fee is not 1% of your outcome. Over 40 years it consumes roughly a
quarter of the terminal balance, because you lose the compounding on every
pound taken. **This is the single most reliable fact in personal finance and
the least intuitive.** It requires no forecast about markets.

### Most active management underperforms net of fees

Consistent across the SPIVA reports and academic literature: over 10-15 year
horizons, the large majority of active funds underperform their benchmark
after fees, in most markets, most of the time. And **persistence of
outperformance is roughly what chance would predict** — past winners are not
reliably future winners.

**Steelman the objection:** this is a statement about the average fund and
average investor, not a proof that skill doesn't exist. Some managers do
outperform. The problem is **identifying them in advance**, and the evidence
on that is discouraging.

### Diversification is close to a free lunch

Markowitz's result: combining imperfectly correlated assets reduces
variability without a corresponding reduction in expected return. It is the
one genuine free lunch in finance, and it is free specifically because
idiosyncratic risk is uncompensated — the market does not pay you for holding
a risk you could have diversified away.

**Caveat that matters:** correlations rise in crises. Diversification works
least well exactly when you most want it to.

### The controllable variables are costs, taxes, and your own behaviour

You cannot control returns. You can control fees, tax wrappers, and whether
you sell in a panic. **The "behaviour gap" studies (Dalbar, Morningstar's
"Mind the Gap") consistently find that investor returns lag the returns of the
funds they hold**, because of the timing of contributions and withdrawals.
The gap is typically one to two percentage points a year.

### High-interest debt is a guaranteed negative return

Paying down debt at 20% is a certain 20% return. No investment offers a
certain 20%. This one is arithmetic.

### A cash buffer prevents catastrophic borrowing

Not an investment argument. It is about avoiding the situation where an
unexpected bill forces high-cost credit or the sale of assets at a bad moment.

### Saving and investing are different activities

| | Saving | Investing |
|---|---|---|
| Preserves | Nominal value | Expected real value |
| Loses to | Inflation, reliably | Nothing reliably; loses to volatility unpredictably |
| Appropriate for | Money you may need soon | Money you will not need for a long time |
| Certainty | High nominal, **negative real over long periods** | Low short-run, historically positive long-run |

**"Cash is safe" is true nominally and false in real terms over decades.**
This is the most consequential confusion between nominal and real that most
people make.

### Insurance is for catastrophes

Insurance transfers risk at a cost (the insurer's margin). It is rational for
losses you could not absorb and irrational for losses you could — which is why
extended warranties on small appliances are a poor deal and liability cover is
not.

## Part 3 — Where the evidence is weak, and nobody should pretend otherwise

| Question | Honest state |
|---|---|
| **What will markets do?** | No reliable evidence anyone can time markets consistently. This survives every attempt to overturn it |
| **Optimal asset allocation** | Depends on horizon, obligations, and risk capacity. The theory is clear; the parameters are unknowable |
| **Factor investing** (value, momentum, quality, size) | Robust in-sample, decayed substantially out-of-sample. **Harvey and Liu documented 300+ published "factors"; most do not survive replication or transaction costs.** Finance has its own replication crisis |
| **The 4% safe withdrawal rate** | Bengen (1994): **one country, one century, one asset mix.** International evidence (Estrada; Anarkulova, Cederburg and O'Doherty) is substantially worse. Widely quoted as if it were a constant of nature |
| **Housing vs equities** | Depends on leverage, taxation, transaction costs and location. No general answer |
| **Crypto** | Fifteen years of price history, no established valuation framework, and both bull and bear cases rest on assertions |
| **Inflation forecasts** | See the-control-group.md. The profession was wrong in both directions within a decade |
| **Whether to fix or float a mortgage rate** | Requires a rate forecast. See previous row |
| **Anything involving your specific tax and pension situation** | Jurisdiction-specific, rule-heavy, and changes. This is what advisers are for |

## Part 4 — Forecast humility

Philip Tetlock's work on expert political judgement found that credentialed
experts' long-range forecasts were barely better than chance and that
**confidence was negatively related to accuracy** — the more famous and more
certain the pundit, the worse the record. The superforecasting follow-up found
what does help: base rates first, many small updates, explicit probabilities,
and comfort with saying "I don't know."

Applied here:

- **Start from base rates.** "How often do recessions follow this signal?" is
  better than "does this feel like a recession?"
- **Keep a written record with dates and probabilities.** Memory reconstructs
  your past beliefs to match your current ones. This is not a moral failing;
  it is how memory works.
- **Update in small increments.** Large swings usually indicate you were
  overconfident before.
- **Notice when a claim is doing political work for you.** That is when your
  standards drop.

## Part 5 — A minimum viable literacy

If you retain six things from this notebook:

1. **Banks create money by lending.** Not a fringe view; the Bank of England
   published it.
2. **Accounting identities cannot generate policy conclusions on their own.**
3. **Hyperinflation is a state-failure disease, not a printing disease.**
4. **Value is a relation, not a property.** There is no true price.
5. **Prices carry dispersed information — which tells you both why markets
   work and exactly where they fail.**
6. **Macro forecasting is unreliable, and the field's own record over
   2010-2023 demonstrates it in both directions.**

## Where the popular story is wrong

| Belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| "You need to understand markets to invest" | The evidence points the other way: the interventions with the best support are low-cost, diversified and behaviourally boring |
| "Financial literacy education fixes financial outcomes" | Meta-analyses find disappointingly small effects on behaviour. **Defaults and automatic enrolment work far better than education** — Thaler and Benartzi's Save More Tomorrow is the best-supported result in the area |
| "Pay off the mortgage vs invest — there's a right answer" | It depends on rates, tax treatment, and how much you value certainty. It is partly a preference question |
| "Complexity signals sophistication" | In retail finance, complexity usually signals fees |
| "Past performance tables help you choose a fund" | Persistence is roughly chance. Regulators require the warning because it is true |
| "The 4% rule is safe" | One country, one century, one asset mix |
| "Economists can tell you what will happen" | They can tell you what happened, sometimes why, and rarely what's next |
| "Nudges reliably change behaviour" | Field-trial effect sizes are much smaller than the popular literature. Defaults are the exception that does work |
